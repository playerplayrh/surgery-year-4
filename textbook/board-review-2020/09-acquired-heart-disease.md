# Acquired Heart Disease 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 418-437 (PDF pages 422-441)

## Introduction
- Adult heart disease is as unfamiliar to surgery residents as pediatric heart disease; in MCQ it causes "chest pain, cyanosis, palpitations" (as printed, joke). In emergencies, residents just send a LINE consult to the medicine resident or cardio-med fellow; as printed, "help ask about the heart for the surgery residents a bit".
- Still, we should know some basics of valve, coronary and (bonus) pericardium disease — enough for the idea, so that on a CVT rotation you can follow what they say, or at least get through teaching medical students.

## Mitral Stenosis (MS)
- Cause: usually rheumatic heart disease → inflamed leaflets with fibrosis and calcification.
- Normal valve cross section = 4-6 cm²
- Mild MS: < 2 cm²
- Severe MS: < 1 cm²
- MS impedes flow from Lt atrium to Lt ventricle → cardiac output falls, blood backs up in the lungs → can cause CHF.
- Symptoms: early symptom = dyspnea on exertion, palpitations; with pulmonary congestion → orthopnea.
- Heart rate: tachycardia; with a large left atrium and raised left atrial pressure, AF in up to 30% → clot formation that may embolise; MS patients often die from massive cerebral emboli.
- Heart sound: opening snap and diastolic rumbling murmur at the apex.
- **Investigation**
  1. CXR: double density at the Rt atrium area from enlargement of the Lt atrium
  2. Echo: valve features — how thick, whether it moves well
- **Management**
  1. Start with medical treatment: beta-blocker, digoxin, diuretic. If CHF is frequent and getting worse, and the patient's age suggests he will not last long, treat the valve.
  2. **Valve treatment** when valve area < 1 cm²; method depends on valve morphology:
     - 2.1 Balloon valvuloplasty: valve not stiff or not very thick, still mobile leaflets
     - 2.2 Stiff, calcified valve → open and replace the valve
     - 2.3 Open valve commissurotomy (open, then cut the fused commissures apart) is no longer used, because percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy can stretch it wider.

## Mitral Ragurgitation (MR)
(heading as printed)
- Cause: usually rheumatic heart disease.
- Figure: normal heart vs mitral valve prolapse with regurgitation (blood leaking back into the left atrium).
- **Symptoms**
  1. Leaking valve → more blood stays in the Lt ventricle → Lt ventricle enlarges; blood in the left ventricle also flows back into the Lt atrium → pulmonary hypertension and CHF. CHF is more common in MR than MS.
  2. AF, especially in end-stage disease.
- Heart sound: holosystolic or pansystolic murmur, loudest at the apex.
- Confirmed by echo, as in MS.
- **Management**
  1. Start with medical treatment as in MS; if CHF is still not controlled despite full medication, with repeatedly worsening symptoms → indication for surgery
  2. Surgery: valve repair or valve replacement

## Aortic Stenosis (AS)
- **Causes**
  1. Acquired calcific disease — most common (จำ); elderly, calcium deposits on the valve
  2. Congenital bicuspid aortic valve
  3. Rheumatic heart disease — uncommon, unlike mitral valve
- Problem in AS: blood leaves the heart with difficulty and needs great force → high Lt ventricle pressure, and blood backs up in the lungs → pulmonary congestion.
- **Symptoms** (pay attention — สนใจหน่อยนะ)
  1. Children: CHF
  2. Adults: asymptomatic early; when the Lt heart can no longer cope → angina, syncope, then CHF
- Heart sound: systolic ejection murmur at Lt upper sternal border.
- EKG: Lt ventricular hypertrophy. Chest x-ray: calcification at the aortic valve with enlarged cardiac silhouette.
- Echo: for diagnosis and to grade regurgitation (as printed).
- Normal valve area about 2-3 cm (as printed); < 1 cm is bad.
- **Indication for valve replacement**
  1. Symptoms of exertional dyspnea, syncope, angina, heart failure and poor ventricular function; symptomatic and not operated → average survival about 3 years, death from VF
  2. Asymptomatic, but yearly echo follow-up shows valve area < 0.6 cm²
- Remember: valve calcification is not an indication, since almost every patient with this disease has calcium on the valve.
- Balloon valvulotomy suits children because the valve is not yet very stiff.

## Aortic Regurgitation (AR)
- **Causes**
  1. Rheumatic heart disease — most common
  2. Aortic dissection
  3. Connective tissue disease, e.g. Marfan
- The leaking valve lets blood flow back into the Lt heart → over time Lt heart failure.
- Symptoms: angina, progressive dyspnea, palpitation, peripheral vasomotor change.
- **Physical examination**
  1. Bounding pulse from wide pulse pressure (not narrow)
  2. Normal cardiac rhythm
  3. Diastolic murmur (not systolic)
- Chest x-ray: enlargement of the Lt ventricle.
- Echo: helps diagnosis and grades the degree of regurgitation.
- **Indication for valve replacement** — depends on magnitude of regurgitation:
  1. Progressive CHF
  2. Progressive ischemia
  3. Severe AR (left ventricular ejection fraction < 45%)
  4. Progressive enlargement of the Lt ventricle

## Pulmonary Stenosis (PS)
- Cause: usually congenital anomaly.
- Obstruction of blood flow → very high Rt ventricle pressure.
- Symptoms: depend on how narrow the opening is; may have none, or dyspnea on exertion (right-sided CHF).
- Murmur: systolic ejection murmur at left upper sternal border.
- Diagnosis: echo (best) — shows anatomy and severity of obstruction.
- Management: balloon valvulotomy is now popular.

## Auscultatory Finding in Acquired Heart Disease
- MS: diastolic rumbling murmur at apex
- MR: halo systolic (as printed) or pansystolic murmur at apex
- AS: systolic ejection murmur
- AR: diastolic murmur

## Coronary Artery Disease
- Cause: mostly atherosclerosis.
- Risk factors: as for PAD in general — smoking, DM, HT, DLP, male sex, family history.
- Key symptom = angina: patients feel heavy pressure over the left chest, or tightness, which may radiate to the shoulder, left upper arm or epigastrium; each episode lasts 2-10 min; classic angina in about 75%.
- **Stable angina**: chest pain for more than 4-6 weeks, typically on brisk walking or exercise.
- **Unstable angina**: chest pain more severe and longer than usual (> 15 min), occurring at rest; not relieved by sublingual nitrite.
- **Investigation**
  1. Non-invasive: EKG, thallium scan, echo and exercise stress testing
  2. Invasive: coronary CT angiography (as printed), done only with an indication
- **Indication for coronary angiography**
  1. +ve exercise stress test
  2. Unstable angina
  3. Chronic stable angina
  4. Recurrent angina
  5. Post MI

## Coronary Revascularization
- Two ways: percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA), done by cardio-med, and CABG, done by surgeons. PTCA need not be known in detail; CABG is the CVT staff's domain, but general surgery residents should know it.

## Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG)
- CVT residents now pick good cases, giving MTR < 2% (as printed, joke).
- **Indication for CABG**
  1. Lt main coronary stenosis > 70% — take it, since PTCA is hard here
  2. 3-vessel disease (LAD, circumflex, Rt coronary) stenosis > 75% with depressed Lt ventricular function
  3. 2-vessel disease with involvement of the Lt anterior descending branch
  4. Failed full medical treatment and failed PTCA
- **Indications for Coronary Artery Revascularization**
  1. Angina unresponsive to medical therapy
  2. Unstable angina
  3. Congestive heart failure with viable myocardium
  4. Cardiogenic shock
  5. Left main stenosis > 50%
  6. Left main equivalent disease — combination of hemodynamically significant lesions involving the proximal circumflex and LAD territory
  7. Concomitant triple-vessel CAD, EF < 50%, and diabetes
  8. Acute coronary occlusion after failed PCI
  9. Mechanical complication of acute MI
- Contraindication for CABG: CHF with pulmonary hypertension, or short expected survival.
- **High-risk CABG patients**
  1. Elderly
  2. Women (จำ)
  3. Many comorbidities, e.g. DM, ESRD, PAD, stroke, COPD
  4. Low ejection fraction
  5. Presence of cardiogenic shock
  6. Lt main coronary stenosis
  7. Emergency or redo surgery
- Single best determinant of CABG operative risk: Lt ventricular function, from the Lt ventricular ejection fraction.
- **Medical treatment**
  1. Lifestyle change: stop smoking, less salt, control sugar, BP and lipids, exercise, avoid stress
  2. Drugs for coronary disease:
     - 2.1 Nitrate, e.g. nitroglycerin, isosorbide: dilate coronaries, reduce myocardial preload, reduce myocardial O2 demand
     - 2.2 Beta-blocker: lowers HR, contractility and afterload
     - 2.3 Calcium channel blocker: lowers afterload and prevents coronary vasoconstriction; however, not used in unstable angina
     - 2.4 ASA or clopidogrel (Plavix) — antiplatelets
- **Revascularization**: intervention = PTCA (percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty) with stenting, and CABG (coronary artery bypass graft). PTCA is now done more and CABG less; surgery residents sitting boards should know CABG a bit.
- **Surgical risk stratification** for choosing treatment — if any risk is present choose PTCA; if none, choose CABG:
  1. Advanced COPD on home oxygen, frail
  2. Dementia, recent hemorrhagic stroke
  3. CHILD B or C cirrhosis
  4. Advanced cancer with short life expectancy
  5. Nonviable myocardium
  6. Prohibit surgical risk (as printed)
- **Facts to know** (ข้อมูลที่น้องๆควรรู้ไว้)
  1. Unstable angina presenting within 4 hours: thrombolytic therapy with PTCA gives success reperfusion rate > 90%
  2. CABG beats PTCA in long-term survival, less recurrent MI and less re-intervention
  3. CABG beats PTCA in multivessel coronary artery disease
  4. After CABG, patients take low-dose ASA for life
  5. In unstable angina still in shock despite full treatment, with progressing infarction → urgent intraaortic balloon pump (IABP), then cath to consider PTCA before surgery
- **Postoperative complications of CABG** (the likely-to-be-asked ones)
  1. AF — most common; mostly from electrolyte imbalance and hypoxia
  2. Focal neurological deficit — usually during aortic cross-clamp or aortic cannulation, when emboli travel up (as printed, "ซวยจริงๆเบย")
  3. Technical problems, e.g. bleeding or tamponade, often need re-exploration
  4. Sternal infection after median sternotomy → acute mediastinitis, mortality > 20%; usually obese, diabetic, or both internal mammary arteries used. Suspect it when there is sepsis and widened mediastinum after surgery. Treat with extensive sternal debridement, daily washing and debridement, then cover with a pectoralis muscle flap.
- Reocclusion rate post CABG about 5-7% per year:
  1. Early occlusion: technical problem
  2. Late occlusion: progressive atherosclerosis, in the coronary itself more than in the conduit
- **Conduit of CABG**
  1. Arteries: internal mammary (internal thoracic), radial, or Rt gastroepiploic. Best patency = internal thoracic artery, 10-year patency 85-90%.
  2. Vein: GSV — advantage is length, enough to bypass several stenoses; patency not as good as artery.
- **Vessels patency**
  1. Internal mammary artery: 90% at 10 years (best)
  2. GSV: 50% at 10 years
  3. PTCA + stent: 80% at 1 year
- New developments in the past 5 years: read "What's new" in Schwartz edition 11 (summarised elsewhere in the book).

## Intraaortic Balloon Pumping (IABP)
- A catheter with a balloon is placed in the descending aorta; it inflates only in diastole and deflates in systole. By raising aortic diastolic pressure, IABP lowers Lt ventricular afterload, raises cardiac index, increases coronary blood flow by pushing blood into the coronary arteries, reduces myocardial oxygen consumption and improves diastolic perfusion. So IABP helps BP and reduces preload — it does not increase preload.
- **Major indications of IABP**
  1. Refractory angina — IABP to increase coronary perfusion and prepare for PTCA or CABG
  2. Hemodynamic instability despite full inotropes
  3. Acute mitral insufficiency secondary to ruptured papillary muscle post MI
  4. Failure PTCA
  5. Ventricular failure or arrhythmia after cardiopulmonary bypass
  6. Post-infarction VSD
- Stenosis of the Lt main coronary artery alone is not an indication for IABP; it must be stenosis with angina.
- Do not use IABP when the aortic valve leaks — it stretches the valve and worsens the leak.
- Do not use IABP in aortic dissection or aortic aneurysm.

## Rupture of the Papillary Muscle
- Papillary muscles help open and close the mitral valve (figure: valve open — chordae tendineae slack, papillary muscles relaxed; valve closed — chordae taut, papillary muscles contracted).
- Occurs 3-7 days after acute MI; without surgery most die within 1 month.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Rapid fall in cardiac output from severe MR
  2. Very rapid pulmonary congestion
  3. Ends in shock and death
- (Figure text: the entire muscle may detach from the ventricular wall → the mitral valve suddenly becomes incompetent → blood flows back into the lungs → acute pulmonary edema.)
- **Management**: diagnose by echo, then urgent surgery to replace the valve — only early surgery gives survival. Before surgery give inotropic drugs and insert intraaortic balloon pump (IABP).

## Ventricular Aneurysm
- After MI, the ischemic area thins the ventricular wall → large aneurysm, usually on the anterior left ventricle.
- Figure: True aneurysm vs pseudoaneurysm —
  - True aneurysm: 1. wide base; 2. walls composed of myocardium (infarcted segment, thinned-out myocardial scar, mural thrombus); 3. low risk of rupture
  - Pseudoaneurysm: 1. narrow base; 2. walls composed of thrombus and pericardium (transmural infarct with rupture); 3. high risk of rupture
- **Symptoms** — ischemic muscle replaced by fibrous tissue → poor pumping function:
  1. Dyspnea & angina
  2. CHF from mitral regurgitation (MR)
  3. Arrhythmia
- (Chest film and CT show a large left ventricular aneurysm.)
- **Indication for surgery**
  1. Symptoms of left heart failure
  2. Recurrent ventricular arrhythmia
  3. Size > 5 cm in greatest dimension — small, asymptomatic ones can be observed
  4. Thromboembolism
- **Procedure**
  1. Aneurysmectomy
  2. Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG added as a bonus)
- **Complications of acute myocardial infarctions**

| Complication | Diagnosis | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiogenic shock | Echocardiography, pulmonary artery catheter | Inotropes, intra-aortic balloon pump, VAD |
| Ventricular septal defect | Echocardiography, cardiac catheterization | Surgical ventricular septal defect repair |
| Papillary muscle rupture | Echocardiography | Surgical valve replacement |
| Ventricular free wall rupture | Echocardiography, ventriculography | Emergent repair of ventricular defect |

## Cardiac Tumors
- Benign 75%; most common = myxoma.
- Malignant 25%; most common = angiosarcoma, then rhabdomyosarcoma — both aggressive tumors, short survival.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Tumor obstructs valve opening/closing → presents with mitral or tricuspid stenosis
  2. Tumor fragments break off → tumor embolus
  3. Tumor impairs contraction and relaxation → CHF, cardiomyopathy or arrhythmia
- Diagnosis: symptoms, echo, MRI.
- Treatment: surgical excision of the tumor.

## Cardiac Myxoma
- Primary cardiac tumors are very rare; most of the few are benign myxoma. Primary cardiac cancer is even rarer, mostly rhabdomyosarcoma neoplasm. Metastases are usually secondary from lung cancer.
- Most common primary benign cardiac tumor in children = rhabdomyomas.
- Myxoma: more common in women; mostly in the Lt atrium (75%).
- **Symptoms**
  1. Local invasion
  2. Mass effect
  3. Embolization — a pedunculated gelatinous tumor, so it moves about
- Myxoma patients seldom have arrhythmia.
- **Investigation**
  1. Echo = initial study of choice; shows mass attached to the interatrial septum
  2. If echo shows a mass, follow with CT or MRI
- **Management**: operate on every case, soon after diagnosis, to prevent embolization.
  1. Median sternotomy (as printed "stenotomy")
  2. Open both atria or bilateral approach to see both Lt and Rt sides, to be sure the tumor is completely removed
- Recurrence post-excision about 10%; knowing this much is OK.

## Pericarditis
- What a surgery resident should know:
  1. Acute & pyogenic pericarditis
  2. Chronic constrictive pericarditis
  3. Post pericardiotomy syndrome

## Acute Pericarditis
- Causes: many; once asked in exams; general surgery residents mostly guess, so follow Table 1.
- **Table 1. Causes of acute pericarditis**
  - Acute idiopathic pericarditis
  - Infectious pericarditis: viral (Coxsackie, influenza, ECHO — enteric cytopathic human orphan), tuberculosis, bacterial infections
  - Postmyocardial infarction pericarditis
  - Postpericardiotomy syndrome
  - Chest trauma (penetrating or nonpenetrating)
  - Uremia
  - Primary or metastatic neoplastic disease
  - Irradiation
  - Collagen diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, scleroderma)
- Symptoms: fever, chest pain, especially on deep breathing.
- Most classic: pericardial friction rub (จำ).
- **Diagnostic criteria for pericarditis**
  - Typical chest pain
  - Pericardial friction rub
  - Suggestive ECG changes (typically widespread ST-segment elevation, PR depression)
  - New or worsening pericardial effusion — inclusion not universally accepted; not necessary but a confirmatory finding when present. Elevated CRP is confirmatory and required by some authors for diagnosis of acute and recurrent pericarditis.
- Chest pain here is not from coronary stenosis, so cardiac cath is not needed (จำ).
- Treatment: according to cause.

## Pyogenic Pericarditis
- More common in children than adults.
- **Organisms**
  1. Children: H. flu and strep
  2. Adults: staph and gm-ve
- Diagnosis: aspirate fluid for examination (pericardiocentesis).
- **Management**
  1. IV antibiotic with serial aspiration or subxyphoid window
  2. Pericardiectomy is rarely needed, except in loculated cases where it may be required

## Chronic Constrictive Pericarditis
- **Causes**
  1. Virus
  2. TB
- Chronic pericarditis → parietal and visceral pericardium stick firmly together → limitation of diastolic filling of the right ventricle.
- Patients have high Rt ventricular diastolic pressure, elevated jugular venous pressure, reduced cardiac output, and equalization of Rt- and Lt-sided cardiac pressures.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Fever, fatigue
  2. Dyspnea on exertion
  3. Ascites, hepatomegaly and peripheral edema
- Pathology (figure text): pericardium thickened > 5 mm by CT, scarred, loss of elasticity.
- Kussmaul's sign: neck veins distend on inspiration.
- Heart sound: normal, no murmur.
- Chest x-ray & CT: normal heart size; calcified pericardium in 25%.
- EKG: low voltage, no Q wave (as printed "Q ware"), ST segment still normal.
- Echo helps distinguish it from restrictive cardiomyopathy.
- Treatment: pericardiectomy.

## Postoperative Complications
- General surgery residents should know 3:
  1. Postoperative bleeding
  2. Pericardiotomy syndrome
  3. Acute mediastinitis

## Postoperative Bleeding
- Distinguish surgical bleeding from the coagulopathy seen in patients who have been on pump.
- Heparinization and hypothermia are linked to bleeding risk.
- **Indications for re-exploration**
  1. All causes sought and corrected, yet bleeding > 500 cc or > 10 cc/kg in the first hour
  2. Persistent bleeding > 200 cc/hr (> 5 cc/kg/hr) for 3 consecutive hours despite normal or near-normal coagulogram
  3. Signs of cardiac tamponade
  4. Massive retained blood clot in the mediastinum and pleural cavity

## Postpericardiotomy Syndrome
- Also called Dressler syndrome; occurs in 10-40%.
- Usually 2-4 weeks after opening the pericardium; temporary pericardial inflammation.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Fever, chest pain, bronchial hyperactivity
  2. Pericardium friction rub
- **Lab**
  1. Leukocytosis, but the rise is in lymphocytes
  2. Elevated ESR
  3. EKG: diffuse ST-segment elevation in multiple leads
  4. Imaging looks like pericarditis — thickened pericardium (arrows)
- Treatment: short course NSAID ± steroid.

## Acute Mediastinitisitis
(heading as printed)
- Deep sternal wound infection; 1-4% after CABG; bad luck if it happens — mortality > 20%.
- Suspect in patients with sepsis + sternal instability; imaging shows widened mediastinum, substernal collection with pocket of air (CVT staff like to ask what this is). (CT shows collection + air.)
- **Treatment**: antibiotics; remove sternal wires; then complete sternal resection with debridement of dead tissue; closed irrigation / wound vacuum-assisted drainage system until infection clears; then close the mediastinal space with a muscle flap.
