# Neck Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 203-226 (PDF pages 2337-2360) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated ๓ กุมภาพันธ์ ๒๕๕๙)

## Introduction
- The neck is a small area, but it holds important organs of several systems, especially the vascular and aerodigestive systems. This chapter focuses mainly on the approach to neck injury; cervical spine and spinal cord injury, and vascular injury, are written up separately.
- Mechanism of injury (printed "Mechannism"): penetrating injury > 95%; blunt trauma is far less common.

## Anatomy
- **Fascia**
  1. Superficial fascia — contains the platysma, covering the front (2/3). A wound not through the platysma is a superficial wound; a wound through the platysma muscle layer is a deep wound, which may injure important organs.
  2. Deep fascia — 3 layers:
     - 2.1 Investing layer — covers SCM and trapezius
     - 2.2 Pretracheal layer — wraps thyroid and larynx
     - 2.3 Prevertebral layer — wraps the prevertebral muscles
- The deep fascia wraps the neck as a closed space, so bleeding produces a tamponade effect that compresses the trachea until the patient cannot breathe.
- On opening the neck, the external carotid artery (ECA) lies in front of the ICA.
- On the left side of the neck lie the esophagus and thoracic duct, so with a left-sided penetrating wound beware these 2 organs too.

## Zones of the Neck
- Monson (1969) divided the neck into 3 zones as a treatment guide; Roon and Christensen (1979) later refined Monson's zones (read in passing — not examined), giving the zones used today.
- Today the neck has 3 zones:
  1. **Zone 1** — from the root of neck and thoracic outlet above both clavicles up to the cricoid cartilage. Injury here may hit upper mediastinal organs: trachea, esophagus, great vessels, lung apex and thoracic duct.
  2. **Zone 2** — from the cricoid cartilage to the angle of mandible; the most frequently injured zone. Injury may hit CCA, ECA, ICA, internal jugular vein, larynx and cervical esophagus.
  3. **Zone 3** — from the angle of mandible to the skull base. Injury may hit the distal ICA and vertebral artery; the hardest zone to expose.

## Clinical Presentation
- History and examination usually tell easily whether an important organ is injured.
- Asymptomatic case = only a wound or bruise, with no symptoms or signs of injury to the aerodigestive tract, vessels, or spine and spinal cord.
- **Vascular injury** — look for hard signs and soft signs; for the carotid, hemiplegia and aphasia. If investigation is needed, the author chooses CT neck and chest.
- **Larynx, trachea and bronchial injury** — symptoms of airway obstruction, e.g. hoarseness, difficult breathing, dysphonia, hemoptysis, or subcutaneous emphysema. If investigation is needed, the author chooses laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy.
- **Pharynx and esophagus** — e.g. saliva leaking from the wound, painful swallowing, difficult swallowing, palpable subcutaneous emphysema; film shows prevertebral air or pneumomediastinum. If investigation is needed, the author chooses esophagography first; esophagoscopy is an add-on option.
- Subcutaneous emphysema comes from injury to:
  1. Pharynx and esophagus
  2. Trachea and bronchus
  3. Air entering subcutaneous tissue through the wound opening
- Note: subcutaneous emphysema from airway injury grows much faster than from GI injury — every cough or breath leaks air, and the face, neck and chest swell up in no time.

## Initial Management
- Follow ATLS; guard the airway closely. Neck swelling, stridor, difficult breathing, drowsiness, cyanosis — especially with bleeding — mean the airway can distort rapidly; in this situation place an endotracheal tube first; if unlucky and the tube will not go in, do cricothyroidotomy straight away. Do not forget C-spine protection.
- If the trachea is visibly transected with an open hole, push a tube in first; repair can wait.
- Fiberoptic-assisted intubation should be for reasonably stable patients, as it takes time; it suits difficult intubation or suspected incomplete tracheal tear.
- Beware zone 1 penetrating injury hitting the lung apex → pneumothorax; in that case insert an ICD at once.
- After A and B, in C (circulation): spurting bleeding → direct pressure on the wound with cloth first; in zone 1 or zone 3 a Foley can be used.
- **Do not** (ห้ามทำ):
  1. Blind clamp or tourniquet around the neck
  2. Probe or local wound exploration
  3. Pull out a knife still stuck in the neck in the ER
  4. Insert NG tube — the patient may cough/gag, the clot dislodges and bleeding floods out
  5. Head-up position — with neck injury, lie slightly head-down: better brain perfusion, and it helps treat venous air emboli from internal jugular vein injury
  6. IV in the arm on the same side as the neck injury
  7. Overresuscitation — give IV only to keep SBP 80-90; high BP dislodges clot and increases bleeding. (As printed, Mattox said when speaking in Pattaya that excessive IV makes patients die faster.)

## Signs of Neck Injuries
1. **Hard signs** — likely need OR
   - 1.1 Airway compromise
   - 1.2 Shock
   - 1.3 Active bleeding, pulsatile or rapidly expanding hematoma, diminished or absent pulse, thrill or bruit, massive hemoptysis
   - 1.4 Extensive subcutaneous emphysema or a sucking neck wound

   **Indications for immediate operative repair by system** (table)

   | System | Finding |
   |---|---|
   | Vascular | Shock; pulse deficit (absent radial pulse)*; uncontrolled bleeding; rapidly expanding hematoma |
   | Respiratory | Stridor*; hemoptysis*; dysphonia* |
   | Digestive | Hematemesis*; dysphagia* |
   | Neurologic | Neurologic deficits* |

   \* In conjunction with other clinical findings.
2. **Soft signs** — likely need investigation
   - 2.1 Hoarseness or stridor
   - 2.2 Hemoptysis, not massive
   - 2.3 Dysphagia
   - 2.4 Painful swallowing
   - 2.5 Widening mediastinum
   - 2.6 Stable hematoma
   - 2.7 Venous bleeding
   - 2.8 Subcutaneous hematoma, not extensive

## Management for Penetrating Neck Injuries
- For the penetrating neck injury guideline: the Schwartz 9th ed guideline combined with the 10th ed suits year 3 residents sitting the MCQ; year 4 should also read the guideline in Trauma 7th p.416 and p.419 — life will be happier.
- The guideline below is from Schwartz 9th ed, adjusted by the author with the 10th edition guideline to make it more complete.
- Figure: Penetrating neck injury algorithm, as steps —
  1. Hemodynamically unstable / uncontrolled hemorrhage → operative exploration.
  2. Hemodynamically stable, symptomatic (symptoms = expanding hematoma, airway compromise, dysphagia, subcutaneous emphysema, hoarseness):
     - Zone I → CT neck/chest {CTA, esophagram, bronchoscopy} → if positive → operative exploration.
     - Zone II → operative exploration.
     - Zone III → angio → if positive → IR embolization.
  3. Asymptomatic:
     - Zone I → CT neck/chest {CTA, esophagram, bronchoscopy} → if positive → operative exploration.
     - Zone II → transcervical GSW → CT neck/chest work-up as zone I; all others → observe.
     - Zone III → observe.
- **Hemodynamically unstable** — uncontrolled bleeding, rapidly expanding hematoma, cannot breathe, or air bubbling from the wound → no investigation; send to OR, not to the CT room.
- **Hemodynamically stable with hard signs of neck injury**
  1. Zone 1 — the author requests CTA neck/chest; esophagography and bronchoscopy may also be needed. If positive → OR, explore neck.
  2. Zone 2 — hard signs → OR, explore neck; soft signs → CTA neck/chest first.
  3. Zone 3 — as for zone 1; if positive, the author passes it to intervention such as embolization.
- Figure: Superior thoracic aperture (caption) — boundaries: vertebra T1, rib I, costal cartilage I, manubrium of sternum; contents: apices of right and left lung, brachiocephalic, subclavian arteries and veins, trachea, esophagus, vagus nerves, sympathetic trunk, phrenic nerves.
- **Hemodynamically stable without symptoms**
  1. Zone 1 — CTA neck/chest first; may add esophagogram and bronchoscopy
  2. Zone 2 and zone 3 — observe, except the following, which should be investigated like zone 1 first:
     - 2.1 Transcervical GSW or a wound crossing the midline
     - 2.2 Multiple injury — many stab wounds, unclear which zones are involved
     - 2.3 A single wound, e.g. entering zone 2 but with the exit or the weapon/bullet track pushing out into zone 3
- GSW and stab wound — choose treatment the same way, even though GSW is more severe.
- Zone 2 injury: it was once believed that any wound through the platysma must be explored; now selective management is used, with CTA, esophagography and bronchoscopy — reducing unnecessary exploration (printed "unnessessary") by 30-80%.
- Checking the platysma is hard in the middle of the night with a busy ER; the author looks at the strap muscles — a wound in the strap muscle means it has gone through the platysma.
- In a hospital lacking out-of-hours resources — alone on call, consults nearly every day, no time even for noodles — and no CTA: in this situation it is better to take the patient to explore; the author, in zone 2, tends to explore the neck because the hospital has no CTA.
- Summary — Schwartz 10th ed differs from 9th ed in:
  1. CTA neck/chest instead of CT neck/chest
  2. Zone 2 with soft signs → investigate first

## Transcervical GSW
- Two approaches:
  1. Most people, including the author, favour neck exploration — significant injury found in 75-80% (a lot)
  2. A minority with a loud voice, Demetriades, did an RCT: on opening the neck, injuries actually needing surgery were found in only 21%; in stable patients he recommends the full investigation set first (CTA neck and chest, esophagography and bronchoscopy)

## Operative Treatment
- Prep skin from neck to knees.
- At intubation, ask the anesthesiologist to look at the vocal cords for us — any cord paralysis.
- **Incision**
  1. Zone 1 — check CTA for which vessel is injured, to choose the right incision
  2. Zone 2 — oblique incision along the anterior border of SCM from the mastoid process to the suprasternal notch
  3. Zone 3 — as for zone 2; if more room is needed, subluxate the TMJ to bring the mandible forward; may also need vertical osteotomy of the mandibular angle
  4. Transcervical wound — collar incision like a thyroid incision; to extend, add upward as a hockey stick or a U shape
- Then treat the specific organ injury.

## Laryngeal Injury
- General surgeons like us just open the neck (airway) so the patient survives first; leave larynx fractures to ENT.
- Figure (labels): fractured thyroid cartilage and fractured cricoid cartilage with swollen vocal cords and narrowed airway; repair — thyroid cartilage repaired with plates, cricoid cartilage sutured.

## Tracheal Injury
- **Treatment**
  1. Suture with absorbable 3-0, interrupted, mucosa to mucosa, knots tied on the outside; key points: no tension and an airtight seal
  2. Gap > 2 cm, or tension when tying → thyroid or suprahyoid release
  3. Gap > 3 cm but not > 5 cm → suture the chin to the chest
  4. Wider gap, usually > 5 cm → repair reinforced with a muscle flap
  5. Trauma 7th: for a wide anterior defect it favours 2 stages — tracheostomy first, definitive repair later (the author agrees with this approach)

## Cervical Esophageal Injury
- Suspect when the patient complains of painful swallowing, vomits blood mixed with saliva, or has subcutaneous emphysema; investigate with esophagography first, possibly adding esophagoscopy to confirm the diagnosis.
- Beware: late treatment or missed injury → infection, and if unlucky, pus tracks from the neck down into the mediastinum (printed "mediastinium") — big trouble for sure (งานช้างแน่นอน).
- **Treatment**
  1. Incision on the left side of the neck, along the anterior border of SCM
  2. Ask the anesthesiologist to place an NG — a guide to feel the esophagus, or to prove a leak by injecting air or methylene blue
  3. Blunt-dissect the esophagus, loop it with a penrose, then deal with the perforation — inspect front and back thoroughly before suturing
  4. Debride and close the perforation, then cover with nearby muscle, e.g. sternothyroid
  5. After the reinforced repair, place about 2 penrose drains alongside
  6. Esophagus badly macerated/friable or delayed diagnosis → cervical esophagostomy only
  7. With associated carotid injury: after repair, place a strap muscle between the esophagus and the carotid artery
  8. Likewise with tracheal injury: after repair, lay nearby muscle — strap muscle or medial head of SCM — across the repair line
- Figure (labels): esophageal repair — extent of mucosal tear larger than the muscle defect ("pouting" mucosa); open the muscle to expose the full mucosal tear down to normal submucosa.

## Thoracic Duct
- Figure (labels): right lymphatic duct empties at the junction of right internal jugular and right subclavian veins; thoracic duct empties into the junction of left internal jugular and left subclavian veins; cisterna chyli is the most inferior part of the thoracic duct.
- Suspect in penetrating injury of zone 1 on the left with milky discharge seeping out.
- Confirm by sending the fluid: protein > 3, TG > 200, alkaline pH, and many lymphocytes on microscopy.
- **Treatment**
  1. Found intraoperatively while exploring the neck → ligation
  2. Diagnosed after surgery → TPN with low fat diet; the fistula usually closes within 2 weeks; if not, go back in and ligate
