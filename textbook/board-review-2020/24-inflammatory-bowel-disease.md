# Inflammatory Bowel Disease 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · volume 2, printed pages 1-(end) (PDF pages 1098-1128; volume 2 printed page n = PDF page 1097 + n, checked at PDF p.1098 = printed p.1)

- Intro: in the USA, IBD, especially Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, is an interesting topic in surgical textbooks; in America almost a million people have it; the opposite in Thailand, where it is found relatively rarely; for differential diagnosis think of IBD as a "last" disease; the real cause is still unknown: blame goes to environment, autoimmune and genetic; many genes that influence immune-mediated disorders are now found.
- Figure (concept): three drivers: genetics (polygenic disease, susceptibility loci, positive family history, HLA gene associations); immune system (disrupted microflora-immune response, Th1 > Th2); environment (alteration in bacterial microflora, nutritional and social factors, hygiene hypothesis). Genetic factors -> cellular network -> clinical phenotypes: autophagy (ATG16L1, IRGM), phagocytosis (NCF4/NCF2), barrier function (PTGER4), Paneth cell failure (NOD2), innate immunity (MST1, MAST3, CARD9), adaptive immunity (IL23R, NKX2-3), antigen presentation (DENND1B, LRRK2) -> early onset/segmental disease, stricturing disease, poor response to therapeutics, environmental risk factors "variance".
- The disease has remission and exacerbation, coming and going, like a good-then-bad cycle; three diseases in this group to know:
  1. Crohn's disease. 2. Ulcerative colitis. 3. Intermediate colitis.
- All three have similar symptoms of inflammation and similar treatment, especially drug treatment; but seen as a whole, especially surgical treatment, there are clear differences; our duty is to diagnose the disease correctly first.

## Crohn's Disease
- The inflammation involves all layers of the bowel wall, called transmural involvement, but with skip areas: some parts look fine and still show mucosal folds, while other parts are diseased with full thickness involvement. Figure: specimen of bowel with mucosal folds but a central ulcerated area (skip).
- Location: found from the mouth to the anus; number 1 is the terminal ileum:
  - 30% isolated small bowel.
  - 40% ileum and right colon.
  - 25% colon and anus, rarely at the rectum.
  - 5% upper GI (duodenum).
- Found in those with high socioeconomic status more than low.
- Symptoms: abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, malnutrition, low grade fever.
- Diagnosis: rule out infectious etiology; relies on complete assessment of symptoms together with x-ray, endoscopy and histopathology, to rule out benign and malignant pathology.
- Endoscopy: the one used often is colonoscopy, intubating to look at the terminal ileum: in the early period only shallow ulcers are seen, called aphthous ulcer; when inflammation is frequent the ulcer becomes deep and runs transversely (transverse ulcer); some say the ulcer looks like a bear claw (bear-claw ulceration); in the late stage it becomes cobblestone appearance; rectal sparing and ileal involvement are usually found in Crohn > UC. Figure: endoscopic photos and cobblestone specimen (skip).
- Capsule enteroscopy: for looking at small bowel lesions at positions the scope cannot reach; now used less, but still recommended in cases negative from endoscopy, CT or MR enterography, small bowel follow through, barium enema.
- For imaging studies, whether small bowel follow through, barium enema, CT/MRI, how they look will be told later.
- Classic feature: since we meet Crohn often at the terminal ileum and cecum, the patient comes with abdominal pain that may be confused with appendicitis; once the abdomen is opened and Crohn is suspected, what is seen in the abdomen: Crohn has full thickness involvement, skip lesions, encroachment of mesenteric fat or fat wrapping covering the serosal surface of the diseased bowel (creeping fat). Figure: mesenteric fat creeping over serosa (the classic finding of Crohn).
- Severity: if erythema nodosum, peripheral arthritis and ophthalmic manifestation are present, the symptoms will be severe.
- In the past severity of Crohn as mild, moderate or severe used the Vienna classification 1998, which had many points that made each doctor understand differently; at present the Montreal classification 2006 is used to state the severity of Crohn, using the age at diagnosis, location in the GI tract and behavior of the disease.
- The Montreal has been revised, but not enough: Paris classification came out to confuse; the author thinks residents need not know it.

| | Vienna | Montreal |
|---|---|---|
| Age at diagnosis | A1 below 40 yr; A2 above 40 yr | A1 below 16 yr; A2 between 17 and 40 yr; A3 above 40 yr |
| Location | L1 ileal; L2 colonic; L3 ileocolonic; L4 upper | L1 ileal; L2 colonic; L3 ileocolonic; L4 isolated upper disease (+ modifier) |
| Behavior | B1 non-stricturing, non-penetrating; B2 stricturing; B3 penetrating | B1 non-stricturing, non-penetrating; B2 stricturing; B3 penetrating; p perianal disease modifier |
- In Shackelford 2019 they use the severity activity of Crohn disease using the American College of Gastroenterology 2009 recommendation, summarized from symptoms and findings:

| Severity of disease | Disease characteristics |
|---|---|
| Mild to moderate | no abdominal tenderness or obstruction, under 10% weight loss, well hydrated |
| Moderate to severe | failed treatment for mild disease, abdominal pain, fever, over 10% weight loss, nausea, vomiting, or anemia |
| Severe to fulminant | failed outpatient therapy, high fever, obstruction, peritonitis, abscess, or cachexia |
- Lately IBD doctors found that the ACG severity index is still not exact: endoscopic findings must be added to say whether the disease is still active or has subsided.

### Little facts for Crohn
- Age at first symptoms about 15-35 years.
- Incidence found more in: 1. smokers; 2. city workers more than those who live in the fields; 3. in the USA black people find it more than white; 4. familial pattern 10-30%; 5. Jews find this disease often.
- IBD that presents with multiple fistula in ano and large anal skin tags: think of Crohn; but if presenting with rectal bleeding, think of UC. Figure: perianal photo (skip).
- Histology:
  1. Early phase: marked lymphangiectasia.
  2. Finding non-caseating granuloma in the bowel wall or lymph node: found less, but if found, suspect Crohn more than UC.
- Location: Crohn likes the right colon, but UC is found often on the left side, and UC always involves the rectum.
- In a case with enterocutaneous fistula, treat conservatively.
- If there is perforation: 1. Crohn: often perforates at the terminal ileum; 2. UC: if it perforates, usually at the transverse colon.
- The real cause is still unknown, but suspicion falls on genetic and extraintestinal manifestations, which are in the autoimmune group, so we understand why steroid and immunosuppressive drugs are used for treatment.
- 80% of Crohn need surgery, 30-50% operated within 5 years.
- At present IBD treatment is better: 20 year survival up to 93%.

## Ulcerative Colitis (UC)
- The inflammation occurs only at the mucosa and submucosa, together with crypt abscess.
- The course is on-and-off like Crohn.
- Location: the main location is the rectum, then the disease extends proximally continuously, with no skip area like Crohn; in UC we find backwash ileitis about 10%, which is an inflammatory reaction of the terminal ileum adjacent to the colon with UC.
- Symptoms: bloody diarrhea (prominent), rectal discomfort, fecal urgency, cramping abdominal pain; this disease must have infectious causes of colitis ruled out.
- Diagnosis: from clinical assessment together with colonoscopy; but avoid doing colonoscopy during acute inflammation because of a high chance of colonic perforation.
- Classic colonoscopic features: starts with swollen mucosa with small bleeding points, followed by friable mucosal inflammation, with continuous involvement from the rectum going up to the proximal colon, bleeding easily, suggesting more severe disease; some may see pseudopolyp and collar button ulcers, with crypt abscess, which is not found in Crohn; doing mucosal biopsy makes the pathologist headache, since the histology of UC is not specific (remember), because it is also found in other IBD. Figure: endoscopic photos; UC pseudopolyps (skip).
- Severity of UC: uses the Montreal classification 2006, by looking at the highest point or extent of proximal involvement:
  1. Ulcerative proctitis (20%): disease only in the rectum, not past the rectosigmoid junction; the lesion here is of low severity.
  2. Left sided colitis (60-70%): disease starts at the rectum and goes up to the left colon, not past the splenic flexure; severity of disease is at a moderate level.
  3. Extensive colitis or pancolitis (10%): disease starts at the rectum to the right colon; 10% involve the terminal ileum, called backwash ileitis; the lesion here is very severe, with bloody stools counted in 10 times.
- Severity in UC: besides the extent, consider the severity of disease together; Montreal 2006 uses the classification of Truelove and Watts 1955 (the author jokes that this must be heaven); the table from General Surgery 2nd ed 2009 of Kirby I. Bland p.716; the fulminant group is placed higher than severe, and a clinical remission group is added, meaning the patient has no symptoms:

| | Mild | Moderate | Severe | Fulminant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stools per day | <4 | 4-6 | >6 | >10 |
| Rectal bleeding | infrequent | intermediate | frequent | continuous |
| Temperature | <37.5 | intermediate | >37.5 | >37.5 |
| Heart rate | <90 | intermediate | >90 | >90 |
| Hemoglobin | >10 | intermediate | <10 | transfusion |
| ESR | <30 | intermediate | >30 | >30 |
- If a perianal lesion is seen, think of Crohn; and when there is a stricture in a UC case, keep cancer in mind too.

## Intermediate Colitis
- Found 15%: IBD in which symptoms, scope and biopsy still cannot separate UC from Crohn, including severity at presentation, while it cannot predict clinical course.
- Treatment: in the early period the same as IBD.
- Indication for surgery: the same as IBD in general, e.g. perforation, failure of medical treatment and malignant transformation.
- If surgery is needed, whether emergency or elective, do the same: total colectomy with the lower end closed as Hartmann and the upper end brought out as end ileostomy first; then wait for the pathology report to see which direction the disease tends toward, UC or Crohn:
  - If the result is UC: when the patient is well, schedule proctectomy with IPAA.
  - If the result is Crohn: do proctectomy with end ileostomy.
- In patients on the pill: the histologic features are hard to separate UC from Crohn.

## Diagnosis of IBD
- At present lab, x-ray, endoscopy and histology biopsy are used for diagnosis and for separating from other colitis.
- Which investigation to use depends on what we think of:
  1. Stool exam: to check for parasites that cause GI symptoms.
  2. Stool culture and toxin assay: in cases suspected of Clostridium difficile colitis.
  3. Stool calprotectin and lactoferrin: new markers for IBD diagnosis, used while there is inflammation.
  4. Plain abdomen: if suspecting toxic megacolon, obstruction, fistula.
  5. CT enterography or MR enterography: the investigation used often, especially in cases suspected of abscess or fistula.
  6. Capsule: suits positions the camera cannot reach, but do not use it in cases with obstruction.
  7. Endoscopy: colonoscopy together with intubation into the terminal ileum: wherever suspicious, biopsy the involved mucosa.
- Serologic markers: 2 used often: Anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibody (ASCA) and Perinuclear Antineutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibody (pANCA):
  - 48-69% of Crohn have ASCA +ve, pANCA -ve.
  - 48-82% of UC have ASCA -ve, pANCA +ve.
- Disadvantage of the markers: sensitivity and specificity are rather low; at present serologic markers for diagnosis and for assessing response to treatment still do not work well; even though research looks for new markers such as anti-Omp C antibody, even gene level research does not help.

## Treatment for IBD
- 2 main treatments: drugs and surgery.
- Treatment has 2 phases: 1. active disease: induction of clinical remission; 2. maintenance of clinical remission.
- In IBD the patient must quit smoking, especially Crohn: smoking makes the disease worse (remember).
- In treating IBD the drugs used go from least aggressive to most aggressive, a measure of stepping up from light to heavy; but in severe forms one may skip straight to heavy weapons; evaluation of results, besides the patient feeling better, should include scope assessment of endoscopic healing.
- Therapeutic pyramid: from light to heavy, called bottom-up or step-up strategy, from the base upward: start with drugs of low efficacy, few adverse drug reactions, and cheap first; if no effect, or the patient is at risk, with severe symptoms or complications, step up to stronger drugs step by step, like a pyramid. Figure: pyramid base to top: 5-ASA, antibiotics; prednisone, budesonide; azathioprine, 6-MP, methotrexate; biologic therapy; surgery.
  1. Topical 5-ASA: a suppository, the lightest measure; if no effect use the oral form; for Crohn give an antibiotic together; if still no effect or symptoms do not improve, bring in steroid to help.
  2. Corticosteroid: start with the oral form first; if severe or cannot eat, give IV form; if used to the point of steroid dependence and still not under control, go to step 3.
  3. Immunosuppressive drug or immune modulator: 3.1 1st line: 6-MP and azathioprine; 3.2 2nd line: methotrexate; 3.3 3rd line: cyclosporine.
  4. Infliximab, a monoclonal anti-tumor necrosis factor, suitable for Crohn; if still no effect, try step 5.
  5. IL-10 azathioprine [as printed]: if after all this there is still no effect, surgery will be needed.
- Criteria saying medical treatment has failed: on steroid more than 6 months and still cannot control symptoms.
- Drugs are treatment to control symptoms only: they do not change the natural course of the disease.
- Disadvantage of pyramid treatment: stepwise treatment gives slow results; the bowel that is inflamed often and for a long time will be followed by many complications, e.g. stricture, fistula, and the harm from using steroid often.
- At present treatment has begun to turn into an aggressive approach from the very first period (top-down strategy), the pyramid shape upside down, especially in patients with severe disease or at risk of severe symptoms, e.g. young people, many positions (extensive disease), especially with perianal disease, or patients who cannot stop steroid: use high-efficacy, expensive drugs such as infliximab from the start, beginning at the active disease period; when the disease subsides, slowly reduce the drug size, and control the disease using less efficacy drugs; at present centers favor the top-down approach. Figure: reversing the treatment pyramid in Crohn's disease: early: infliximab (+ AZA/6-MP/MTX), steroids, surgery.
- However, at present it is still recommended to use bottom-up.

### 5-Aminosalicylates (5-ASA)
- Treats both Crohn and UC; used as 1st line treatment for mild to moderate inflammation.
- Mechanism: anti-inflammatory effect directly at the gut mucosa; the more the drug contacts the mucosa, the better the effect.
- The group has many kinds, many preparations, acting at different sites.
- In active disease, 5-ASA for induction of subsiding inflammation appears to give little effect.
- In the past 5-ASA was used to maintain remission, to keep the disease quiet for long, but now experts say maintaining remission is not that good.
- Disadvantage of this group: watery diarrhea found up to 15-30%.
- To choose a drug in this group, see which target it acts on:
  1. Acting at the small bowel: drugs with ileal activity, since they dissolve there, so suit maintenance of remission in Crohn and help reduce steroid dose:
     - 1.1 Pentasa.
     - 1.2 Mesalamine (Asacol): previously recommended as first line therapy; at present from a Cochrane review it is found that mesalamine should not be used as first line because maintaining remission, including reducing steroid use, is not as good as believed.
  2. Acting at the colon: the drug that acts mostly at the colon is sulfasalazine, so it suits maintenance of remission in UC; if used in Crohn, it should be colonic Crohn.

### Corticosteroid
- A drug suitable in the active disease period or acute exacerbation: helps remission quickly, but not effective for preventing recurrence; so steroid does not work for maintenance of remission.
- Steroid works well in both Crohn and UC:
  1. Mild to moderate IBD that does not respond to 5-ASA: give oral steroid.
  2. Severe IBD: give steroid in IV form.
  3. In UC with not much severity, only proctitis or proctosigmoiditis: an enema or suppository steroid still gives results.
- In pregnant women: steroid can still be given with safety, especially in cases with a flare up of disease.
- Disadvantages: raises blood sugar, so beware in DM patients, and limits growth in children.
- Duration of steroid: usually give 4-8 weeks, then slowly reduce the dose, using about 4-8 weeks until off; do not give for a long time because steroid dependence occurs; at present there is a new steroid called budesonide, believed to have fewer adverse effects than prednisolone.
- In cases given steroid with no effect, or cannot stop the drug: must use immune modulator drugs to help.

### Antibiotics
- Use antibiotic if there is evidence of infectious complication of IBD, especially Crohn, which needs antibiotic often; while in UC, antibiotic is given only when fulminant UC occurs.
- Crohn with ileocolitis, fistula, pouchitis, perianal involvement should get antibiotic to reduce bacterial load.
- The drug popularly given is metronidazole, next quinolones.
- Disadvantages of metronidazole: 1. teratogenic effect, so should not be used in pregnant people; 2. GI upset; 3. peripheral neuropathy.

### Immune Modulator
- Works in both Crohn and UC that fail 5-ASA.
- Helps induction and maintenance of remission; should be given when steroid does not work or steroid cannot be stopped.
- This group of drugs helps reduce the steroid dose.
- 6 MP is the one used most, next azathioprine; both are in the antimetabolite group; to be effective, these 2 drugs take about 6-12 weeks.
- Disadvantages of 6 MP and azathioprine: bone marrow suppression, higher infectious complication, and 2% acute pancreatitis.
- Cyclosporine: little role; if used, beware the kidney.
- If 6 MP and azathioprine do not work: there is also methotrexate as 2nd line.

### Infliximab (Remicade)
- A monoclonal anti-tumor necrosis factor group drug, the first one used.
- Works well in Crohn, but in UC it works somewhat, though it seems to lose to Crohn; it helps induction of remission, but to be effective takes 6-12 weeks, so in acute exacerbation where quick results are hoped for, it does not seem suitable.
- In the past it was recommended as the last weapon for patients who failed everything; this drug is an IV form, not an oral form (remember), injected IV once every 2 months, effective 50%; at present it is recommended to give right away in cases resistant to first line treatment.
- Infliximab suits Crohn with enterocutaneous fistula because it helps the fistula close faster; besides, the drug helps healing of perianal Crohn, and does not increase the risk of wound complication after stoma closure.
- A little more: forbidden to use this drug in septic cases not yet treated, e.g. an abscess not yet drained (remember); the drug also promotes reactivation of histoplasma and increases the risk of TB infection, so use it carefully.
- At present many new drugs have come out, e.g. adalimumab, certolizumab; but the one talked about most, and now used clinically, is vedolizumab.

## Medical Treatment for Crohn's Disease
- Active disease: look at severity mainly; Schwartz uses the Harvey-Bradshaw index, but most use the Crohn disease activity index (CDAI), dividing severity into mild, moderate, severe.
- Table (Crohn's Disease Activity Index; item (day), weight): number of liquid or very soft stools (each day for 7 days) x2; abdominal pain, sum of 7 d rating (0 none, 1 mild, 2 moderate, 3 severe) x5; general well being (1-4) x7; extraintestinal (1 per finding): arthritis/arthralgia, mucocutaneous lesion, iritis/uveitis, anal disease (fissure, fistula, etc), external fistula, fever over 36.8 x20; antidiarrheal use x30; abdominal mass (none 0, equivocal 2, definite 5) x10; hematocrit (males 47, females 42) x6; body weight (1 - body weight/standard weight) x100 x1; total CDAI score.
1. Mild: CDAI score 150-220: should give metronidazole; steroid should not yet be given.
2. Moderate: CDAI score 221-450: symptoms moderate, fever, abdominal pain but no rebound, nausea, vomiting, weight loss but no bleeding, no gut obstruction symptoms.
   - Treatment: 2.1 should give steroid; 2.2 immune modulator is brought in when the steroid given gives only an incomplete response or has become steroid dependent.
3. Severe: CDAI score over 451: fever, abdominal pain with rebound, persistent vomiting suggesting features of gut obstruction, patient looks thin, cachexia.
   - Treatment: aggressively with IV steroid together with IV antibiotic; if still no response, IV infliximab; handled to this extent and still no response, will be operated.
- AGA 2014 divided Crohn disease to use as a guide for treatment into 2 groups:
  1. Low inflammatory burden: no fever, no weight loss, no extra GI manifestation, mild perianal disease, labs no anemia, albumin not low, CRP normal, scope shows only shallow ulcer; treatment in the active disease period: mainly steroid.
  2. High inflammatory burden: fever, weight loss, extra GI manifestation, severe perianal disease, labs severe anemia, albumin low, CRP high, scope shows severe rectal disease with deep ulcer; treatment in the active disease period: mainly IV steroid, infliximab or vedolizumab.
- Maintenance of remission:
  - The main drug is the aminosalicylate group that dissolves at the terminal ileum, i.e. mesalamine and pentasa; for the colon sulfasalazine; if the aminosalicylate group still cannot maintain remission, should use 6 MP or azathioprine; in very severe cases may need infliximab added.
  - Remember: a patient who got steroid: stopping the drug must be gradual: reduce the size of the drug, do not stop it cold: reduce 5-10 mg per week until stopped, because steroid has no role in maintaining remission.
  - Some Crohn patients are so thin that nutritional support is important; some must be NPO for a long time, especially those with fistula: TPN helps maintain lean body mass and promotes healing of the fistula.
  - Patients with disease at the terminal ileum may lack B12; if lacking, must keep giving it.
  - Patients who get steroid often, or each time must get it a long time, have a risk of osteoporosis, so advise the patient to take vitamin D and calcium straight away, without waiting for osteoporosis to occur.
  - TPN is not a 1st line treatment: give it only in cases where it is necessary; and TPN is not better than enteral nutrition at all.

## Medical Treatment for UC
- The goal of treatment is to reduce inflammation (anti-inflammation) and adjust immunity (immunomodulation) with drugs, so the disease subsides and relapse is prevented.
- The disease subsides: look at clinical: bloody stool must stop, including scope showing mucosal healing.
- Active disease: treatment looks mainly at the location of disease: those lying low or at the left side with mild severity:
  1. Distal disease: i.e. ulcerative proctitis, severity little: use a suppository, whether topical steroid or topical mesalamine, enough.
  2. Left-sided colitis: UC here has moderate severity; treatment uses the principle of treating from light to heavy:
     - 2.1 Start with a retention enema, with steroid or mesalamine enema.
     - 2.2 If it can be retained, no problem; give the 5-ASA group that dissolves at the colon, e.g. sulfasalazine.
     - 2.3 If the 5-ASA group still does not work: give oral steroid short term.
     - 2.4 If steroid given still does not improve: give 6 MP or azathioprine; if left-sided UC, it will be controlled; then give immune modulator together with steroid for another 3-4 months.
  3. Extensive colitis (pancolitis): a severe form of UC: admit quickly and order aggressive treatment:
     - 3.1 Start with IV methylprednisolone 40-60 mg for 7-10 days.
     - 3.2 If not controlled, give IV infliximab: controlled with drugs 75%.
     - 3.3 If not improved, a surgeon will be needed to cut.
- Maintenance of remission:
  1. Distal disease group: no drug needed.
  2. Left sided colitis group: give sulfasalazine, but steroid is not needed.
  3. Extensive colitis (pancolitis) group: must give 6 MP or azathioprine.
  4. In female patients with UC in remission: pregnancy does not affect the course of colitis; but if there is a flare, steroid can be given together with sulfasalazine.

## Surgical Treatment for Crohn's Disease
- About 90% of Crohn patients will be operated on after diagnosis, at about 30 years, and 50-70% will be operated on more than once; remember to leave a trace: operations in Crohn do not cure like UC.
- Recurrence after surgery for Crohn is very high: 70% recur in 1 year and 85% recur in 3 years; usually recurring at the site just proximal to the cut bowel anastomosis; patients with recurrence are usually seen endoscopically within 1 year after surgery.
- Especially ileocolic disease has higher recurrence than ileal disease alone; or if granuloma is found in the resected specimen, recurrence is about 40-45%.
- Reoperation: within 5 years, about 33% are operated a second time.
- Indication for surgery: mainly failure of medical treatment, the most common indication, and complications arising from the disease itself; in a case where a stricture is seen on imaging but the patient has no symptoms, can still pass stool, there is no need to operate.
- Figure (indications for surgical intervention in Crohn's disease): Acute: perforation, sepsis, hemorrhage, toxic megacolon. Subacute: neoplasia, abscess, obstruction, symptomatic fistula, refractory diarrhea, malnutrition. Elective: recurrent/medically refractory disease; failure to wean/steroid dependence; growth retardation (pediatric).
- Currently laparoscopic resection is more accepted because less pain, shorter hospital stay, less ileus, while recurrence is no different from open technique.

### Principles of Operation
- Cutting out the diseased bowel gives the best treatment result.
- Rules of thumb that must be known before operating on a Crohn patient:
  1. Conservative resection: cut as little as necessary, because the disease is not cured and often recurs; should leave small bowel longer than 100 cm.
  2. No need for frozen section at the resection line: because F/S does not reduce recurrence at the margin; even if the margin is not free, after the cut one still has to treat it; so in practice use fingers to feel that the bowel at the touch feels normal, grossly margin uninflamed bowel about 2 cm, with good blood supply, the cut not tense: acceptable.
  3. Avoid bypass: because blind loop occurs and the risk of cancer at the bypassed segment is up to 50 times higher than normal.
  4. Do not make a pouch (remember): because the disease often involves the ileum, there will be recurrence in the pouch.
- Contraindication for primary anastomosis: 1. hemodynamically unstable; 2. malnourished; 3. receiving steroid and immunosuppressive drug; 4. extensive intraabdominal contamination with sepsis.
- Indication for bypass: even though bypass is something to avoid, since the diseased bowel is still there, in some cases one must resolve to do it, in these conditions: 1. elderly, poor risk; 2. duodenal Crohn: if needing a Whipple it would be too much, just gastrojejunostomy is enough; 3. previous extensive small bowel resection; 4. presence of inflammatory abscess or large inflammatory phlegmon; 5. dense inflammatory mass making its mobilization unsafe, especially if stuck to large vessels or important retroperitoneal organs.
- Stricturoplasty (of interest): a procedure used in Crohn patients who have had extensive previous small bowel resection, because if more bowel is taken out there is a chance of short bowel syndrome; remember: if there is no short bowel problem, one should use the method of cutting and joining the bowel, which is better; positions where stricturoplasty is done most often are ileum, then jejunum, ileo-cecum in sequence; even if there are many strictures, multiple stricturoplasty can still be done.
  - Complication of stricturoplasty: only 9%, from leak, abscess, fistula; and re-stricture 2%; reoperation rate of stricturoplasty equals resection; 30% have re-stricture in 5 years and often at a new site.
  - Indication for stricturoplasty: 1. diffuse small bowel involvement with stricture; 2. prior small bowel resection over 100 cm or short bowel syndrome already occurring; 3. rapid recurrence of Crohn with obstruction; 4. obstructed fibrotic stricture without sepsis.
  - Contraindication for stricturoplasty: 1. perforation of the bowel segment that has the stricture; 2. hemorrhagic stricture; 3. multiple strictures in a short segment (should resect and then anastomose once, better); or a stricture lying close to bowel that will need to be cut anyway; 4. the stricture segment where the pathologist says dysplasia or cancer; 5. phlegmon inflammation at the position to be done, or a fistula, whether external or internal; 6. malnutrition or hypoalbuminemia (under 2.0 g/dL).
- Techniques of stricturoplasty: use the same technique as pyloroplasty; which to use depends on the length of the stricture (figures skipped):
  1. Short stricture not over 7 cm: do like Heineke-Mikulicz pyloroplasty: cut lengthwise, suture crosswise.
  2. Long segment more than 7 cm but not over 15 cm: do like Finney or Jaboulay pyloroplasty.
  3. But if the stricture is longer than 15 cm, or multiple short strictures over a lengthy segment: do Michelassi side-to-side isoperistaltic enteroenterostomy (side-to-side isoperistaltic stricturoplasty).

### Minimal Invasive Surgery
- Whether laparoscopic, robot or NOTES, they have begun to be used because Crohn patients are operated repeatedly; but whoever will do it must have much experience, having done these cases more than 10-50 per year; conversion rate to open is 5-30%.
- At present, if the operator has enough experience, the laparoscopic approach should be the first choice.
- Advantage: PO ileus and hospital stay are shorter than open.
- Contraindications to MIS in Crohn disease: 1. uncorrected coagulopathy; 2. intraabdominal varices in portal hypertension; 3. dense intraabdominal adhesion or history of multiple open abdominal exploration; 4. diffuse peritonitis; 5. severe obstruction with massive dilated bowel.

### Crohn with Hemorrhage
- Rather rare, usually occult bleeding, but can be life threatening.
- Because Crohn is found often in the small bowel, the problem is finding the location of the bleeding point.
- Treatment: 1. start with non-operative methods first: if bleeding still does not stop, intervention is needed; 2. if after intervention it still bleeds, must open and go in to resect and then join.

### Crohn with Obstruction
- The most common indication for surgery in Crohn, found up to 33%.
- Most are partial more than complete obstruction.
- Initial investigation is plain film as usual.
- Treatment: 1. start with conservative first; 2. if no result, must open and cut then join, trying to avoid multiple anastomosis; 3. for a short segment stricture at the duodenum, terminal ileum and colon: some use a scope to look and dilate the stricture; some use local steroid injection at the stricture together; when there is time to walk, in cases where we have tried everything, steroid, injection, balloon and still nothing, drag into the OR.

### Crohn with Free Perforation
- In Crohn most are contained perforation; free perforation is found very rarely.
- Treatment: 1. without severe contamination: cut the diseased segment that perforated, then join it immediately; 2. with severe contamination: cut but do not join, bring out an ostomy first; when the patient's condition is better and infection subsides, join it back some days later.

### Crohn with Abscess
- Investigation is CT.
- Treatment: 1. start with percutaneous abscess drainage (PAD) first; 2. next step when the patient is able: elective surgical resection; but if the patient's state is not able, treat Crohn first with drugs.

### Crohn with Enterocolic or Enterovesical Fistula
- Found 33% in the first 10 years; following 20 years found 50%.
- Most common is GI to GI, next GI to bladder or vagina; in some severe cases there is a fistula to both GI and non-GI.
- Treatment: try limited resection once, in one stage, neatly: 1. resection of the bowel or rim of the bladder; 2. end to end anastomosis of the bowel; 3. repair the bladder then leave a Foley in place; 4. 30% must do fecal diversion.
- Beware: if it is enterocutaneous or enteroenteral fistula, the treatment still recommends trying conservative first; the indication for surgery of this fistula is still controversial.

### Duodenal Crohn
- About 3% involve the duodenum, usually presenting with obstructive symptoms; usually the chance of being operated is small.
- If needing surgery, one should do bypass gastrojejunostomy more than resection, which has a high chance of leaking; or if it is feasible to do stricturoplasty, it seems better than resection, because one may have to do a Whipple.

### Crohn with Cancer
- Risk of cancer rises 10 times; cancer in Crohn often arises directly at the bypass segment or at the part of the bowel that has had Crohn a long time.
- Found rarely, M : F = 3 : 1.
- Treatment: the same as cancer; prognosis poor.

### Crohn with Growth Retardation
- In children with Crohn 25% grow slowly from lack of nutrition.
- Treatment: should operate before epiphyseal closure, by resecting the diseased segment, then join, and feed immediately.

### Crohn with Ureteric Obstruction
- Put a stent or divert the urine to come out first, e.g. PCN.
- Treatment: the next step is to treat the primary bowel disease; a few cases may need ureterolysis.

### Colorectal Crohn
- If the bowel must be cut, try to preserve as much bowel as possible.
- Forbidden: ileoanal pouch (remember) or continent Kock pouch ileostomy.
- In case of toxic megacolon: initial treatment is the same as for TMC of UC.

### Anal and Perianal Crohn
- Found up to 35%; 4% of Crohn will be found only at the anus, in whom no disease of other bowel is found, so one finds Crohn at the anus; so looking only at the other bowel and not seeing Crohn is wrong.
- Most common perianal lesion is skin tag (40-70%).
- Whenever IBD is suspected and an anal lesion is seen, think of Crohn; often when we resect the disease in the abdominal cavity, we find the anal lesion looks better.
- Before treating the anal lesion, do GI study to see whether there is Crohn elsewhere or not.
- Examination under anesthesia (EUA) is the gold standard.
- Most experts do not recommend resecting proximal disease in the hope of improving anorectal Crohn; it is believed that it is a disease at the anus, so treat the anus.

### Crohn with Skin Tag or Hemorrhoid
- Usually no symptoms, except while there is active disease: hard and painful.
- No need to bother with them, because the wound does not heal easily, the patient gets fever and goes to bad-mouth the doctor, and if the wound is infected and a fistula forms again, the doctor is dead for sure.
- Indication for surgery: 1. extremely symptomatic; 2. chronic non-healing wound: if after operating the wound still does not heal, refer to someone we respect.
- Remember: if one must operate, do not do it while Crohn is in the active disease period.

### Crohn with Anal Fissure
- For fissure in Crohn patients, about 33% are located laterally, rather deep, many wounds, not very painful; if painful, we suspect there is an abscess or hidden fistula.
- About 50-70% heal with non-surgical methods, by giving a high fiber diet, sitz bath, a drug like muscle relaxant.
- Treatment: avoid LIS as much as possible, because the wound does not heal and there is a high chance of incontinence; if one must operate, do not do it during active disease; start with medical treatment such as nitroglycerin paste, calcium channel blocker cream, applied or botox injection first: success about 80%.

### Crohn with Cryptoglandular Infection
- Should examine under anesthesia because the pain is severe; in a deep or complex abscess should use EUS as a guide for drainage.
- Treatment: 1. give antibiotic; 2. must drain before giving the immune modulator or infliximab group; 3. if a fistula is present, choose a non-cutting or loose seton, using inert material to pass through first to prevent recurrence of abscess, and this kind of seton can be left in a long time; 4. persistent sepsis: should make a temporary diverting stoma.

### Crohn with Fistula in Ano
- Original principle: Crohn is a disease that is not cured; treatment is only to relieve symptoms; if the fistula has no symptoms, do not touch it.
- Before operating one must identify the tract: some cases need to play with toys such as EAUS or MRI; fistulography has low accuracy (16-50%).
- Complex FIA: from Surg Clin N Am, February 2010, cheers EUA, proctoscopy, pelvic MRI.
- Treatment:
  1. Asymptomatic FIA: observation.
  2. Small FIA with little symptoms: warm sitz bath.
  3. If there is active inflammation or infection or complex FIA, definitive surgery should not yet be done; what should be done: 3.1 give antibiotic such as ciprofloxacin or flagyl; 3.2 if there is still an area of undrained abscess, use a vessel loop to make a non-cutting or draining or loose seton, seeming appropriate; when active infection subsides, then do the definitive surgery; 3.3 treating the intestinal Crohn helps heal perianal Crohn.
  4. What should not be done while active infection: 4.1 definitive surgery of all kinds, because incontinence will surely occur; 4.2 fibrin glue injection, plug; 4.3 cutting seton, because of high incontinence, but doing just I&D seems too little.
  5. Definitive surgery:
     - 5.1 Fistulotomy: used in cases that are low fistula without inflammation, but if it is a complicated FIA it should be used together with long term seton, which helps reduce the incontinence problem.
     - 5.2 Endorectal advancement flap: used in cases with very little proctitis and with rectal mucosa not involved; this method suits high fistula.
     - 5.3 Plug: used with high fistula without inflammation.
  6. If afraid of much incontinence and the tract is rather long, may try fibrin glue injection: success rate about 50%.
  7. 10% of patients may need proctectomy because of intractable perianal sepsis, or some cases may need a colostomy first.
  8. Infliximab is a drug that helps heal the fistula well, next 6-MP and azathioprine.

### Crohn with Anal Stricture
- A result following chronic inflammation or fistula at the rectum and anus.
- Symptoms: stool does not come out well, tenesmus, urgency, incontinence.
- Before treating, biopsy to rule out malignancy first.
- Treatment: 1. no symptoms: do not treat; 2. with symptoms: start with measures from light to heavy: 2.1 anal dilation with finger or balloon; 2.2 chance of proctectomy up to 43% because severe proctitis is often present together.
- Doing only fecal diversion may not work because progressive stenosis from proctitis still occurs.

### Crohn with Acute Appendicitis
- Crohn at the IC area may come with RLQ pain, hard to separate from appendicitis; opening the abdomen and finding Crohn-like findings: grossly inflamed terminal ileum, thick mesentery, large lymph node, mesenteric fat wrapping the serosa. Figure: operative photo (skip).
- Treatment: if the cecal base has no inflammation and the bowel looks normal: do appendectomy right away, to prevent future confusion; but if the cecal base has active disease, do not take out a normal appendix, because the stump may leak.
- Appendix stump: if the cecal base has active disease, do not take out a normal appendix, because the stump may leak.

## Surgical Treatment for UC
### Emergency
- Indication: 1. bleeding; 2. perforation; 3. toxic megacolon (TMC). Treatment by surgery is the same for all.
- TMC is severe or fulminant UC. Figure: operative photo of dilated colon (skip).
- Rapid deterioration: high fever, abdominal pain and distension, tenderness, tachycardia over 100/min, stools over 6 per day, severe dehydration.
- CBC: anemia, WBC over 10,500.
- Plain abdomen: transverse colon diameter over 6 cm, from inflammation-induced paralysis of the colon. Figure: dilated transverse colon on film (skip).
- Precipitating factors: 1. antispasmodic drugs such as Lomotil, morphine; 2. barium enema; 3. electrolyte imbalance: hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypoproteinemia.
- Treatment:
  1. Initial: NPO, IV fluid, NG tube, IV PPI, VTE prophylaxis; then IV antibiotic with IV high dose steroid; no colonoscopy; check CBC, electrolytes, abdominal film every 12 hr; if improving continue conservative; if not improved within 24-48 hr, or rapidly worsening, go to emergency surgery (about 50% are operated).
  2. Emergency surgery: 2.1 best is total abdominal colectomy keeping the rectum, closing the lower end as Hartmann, and bringing the proximal end out as end ileostomy; when the patient recovers, return for completion proctectomy + IPAA. 2.2 In a very toxic, very poor risk patient, do only a loop ileostomy with loop colostomy (Turnbull blow-hole procedure); patients in this group often end up dead, but if they survive, elective surgery is done later.
### Elective
- UC is a mucosal lesion, so the chance of being operated for fistula, perforation or stricture is very low, unlike Crohn.
- Indication for elective surgery:
  1. Intractability or failure of medical treatment: full drugs but cannot stop, or complication from the drugs so that they cannot be continued; the most common indication.
  2. Dysplasia (see surveillance below); the trend is toward an aggressive approach: even mild dysplasia, some are dragged to the OR.
  3. Extraintestinal manifestation, especially eye symptoms: cutting out the colon disease makes eye symptoms better.
- Principle: remove the entire colon and rectum, and in some cases the anus too. Operations:
  1. Best: total proctocolectomy with ileal pouch anal anastomosis (IPAA); the most popular is the J pouch, which reduces frequent stools; because the lower anastomosis is rather low, a diverting loop ileostomy is added. IPAA can be done by MIS; the patient can still defecate through the anus; suits cases without anal incontinence and with good operative risk. Figure: pouch joined to anus (skip).
  2. Next: total colectomy with mucosal proctectomy and endorectal ileoanal anastomosis: the principle is to strip off all columnar epithelium, but in reality this is hard, so some columnar cells remain, giving a chance of rectal cancer in future, so surveillance is still needed; the advantage is less incontinence.
  3. For those with the lower anastomosis, do a temporary diverting loop ileostomy about 6-12 weeks, waiting for the distal anastomosis to heal before closing.
  4. A case with anal incontinence but good operative risk: total proctocolectomy with end ileostomy.
  5. Total colectomy + ileorectal anastomosis is used rarely; not chosen.
  6. UC patients have a rather high risk of DVT and thromboembolic events: give prophylaxis in every case.
### Pouchitis
- Found 5-10% of pouches after IPAA; the real cause is unknown, believed related to fecal stasis with bacterial overgrowth; UC patients with extraintestinal manifestation, especially primary sclerosing cholangitis, have a high chance of pouchitis.
- Symptoms: abdominal cramp, diarrhea, fever, dehydration, bloody stool.
- Diagnosis: from examination and endoscopy of the pouch: mucosa swollen, red, inflamed; biopsy to rule out recurrence of Crohn; CT is for pouchitis that fails to improve.
- Treatment: metronidazole or ciprofloxacin for about 2 weeks usually settles it; ileal diversion or pouch resection seems excessive, except chronic inflammation not controlled by drugs, then the pouch must come out.
- Note: pouchitis occurs with IPAA in UC but is not found in FAP.

## Colonoscopic Surveillance for IBD
- Colonoscopy, besides seeing endoscopic recurrence, also helps cancer surveillance; both Crohn and UC have risk of cancer; UC surveillance is easier: scope, take random biopsies per guideline.
- Endoscopic recurrence in Crohn: use the Rutgeerts score to predict recurrence after surgery:

| Grade | Endoscopic finding |
|---|---|
| i0 | no lesions |
| i1 | fewer than 5 aphthous lesions |
| i2 | i2a lesions confined to the anastomosis; i2b more than 5 aphthous lesions with normal mucosa between lesions, areas scattered with larger lesions |
| i3 | diffuse aphthous ileitis over inflamed mucosa |
| i4 | diffuse inflammation with large ulcers, nodules and/or strictures |
- Grades 0, 1, 2 are low risk: about 95% of patients have no symptoms after surgery for at least 3 years; grades 3, 4 are high risk: grade 3 (moderate risk) about 40% recurrence within 3 years; grade 4 (high risk) about 90%.
- AGA 2017 postoperative Crohn algorithm (figure): Low risk: no meds; colonoscopy 6-12 months postop; no recurrence: colonoscopy every 1-3 years; recurrence: 6-MP/AZA or anti-TNF. Moderate: 6-MP or AZA +/- metronidazole. High: anti-TNF + 6-MP/AZA; colonoscopy 6-12 months postop; no recurrence: colonoscopy every 1-3 years; recurrence: anti-TNF or biologic.
- Cancer in IBD patients is found often at a young age, as flat lesions that are hard to see with the colonoscope, with extensive submucosal spreading, so prognosis is not good.
- Recent meta-analyses: UC patients taking 5-ASA have lower risk of colorectal cancer; mucosal ablation such as mucosectomy does not reduce CRC risk.
- Risk of cancer is related most to extent of disease, next to duration: about 0.1-1% per year.
- Pancolitis CRC risk: disease longer than 10 years 2%; longer than 20 years 8%; longer than 30 years 18%.
- Best screening in UC: colonoscopy with random biopsy of the colon, 4 pieces every 10 cm; the longer the colon, the more biopsies, at least 40-50 pieces; frequency: colonoscopy + random biopsy every 1 year; for Crohn with extensive colonic disease, surveillance as for UC is advised.
- Pancolitis: start annual surveillance 8 years after diagnosis; left-sided: start after 15 years.
- Biopsy while inflammation is still present: a pathology report may read reactive atypia, which has not turned into cancer; when inflammation subsides, reactive atypia resolves by itself, but dysplasia will not resolve; so if inflammation is still present, treat first, wait until remission, then redo colonoscopy and random biopsy.
- If biopsy reports severe dysplasia, everyone accepts prophylactic total proctocolectomy with IPAA.
- Low grade dysplasia is still debated because it carries occult malignancy about 20% within 5 years. Two approaches: 1. aggressive: operate straight away, especially if the lesion looks like an adenoma or a mass; 2. some wait 2 months then repeat colonoscopy with biopsy; if still low grade dysplasia, operate; or if the patient will not operate, scope every 6 months.
- Schwartz believes low grade dysplasia accompanies cancer in 20%; Dr. Hartcore [as printed] concluded the grade of dysplasia does not matter and counts any as an indication for prophylactic surgery; another reason for aggressiveness is that cancer in UC is often poorly differentiated, mucus-secreting, with a chance up to 25% of multiple colon cancers, making prognosis poor; the author agrees with Schwartz: UC with low grade dysplasia should be operated.

## Extraintestinal Manifestation of IBD
- Found in both UC and Crohn; mainly skin, joints, eyes, liver; except CHRPE spots, found in FAP, not in IBD.
- Arthritis is the most common in Crohn, next iritis/uveitis.
- Nodular arthritis: not found in IBD [as printed].
- Figure (body map): eyes: episcleritis, uveitis; mouth: stomatitis, aphthous ulcers; liver: steatosis; biliary tract: gallstones, sclerosing cholangitis; kidneys: stones, hydronephrosis, fistulae, UTI; skin: erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum; joints: spondylitis, sacroiliitis, peripheral arthritis; circulation: phlebitis.
- After treating the primary disease, reversible lesions: 1. fatty infiltration of liver; 2. arthritis that is not SI or AS; 3. ocular problem; 4. anemia.
- Not reversible: 1. cirrhosis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, pericholangitis, bile duct cancer; 2. sacroiliitis (SI); 3. ankylosing spondylitis (AS); 4. erythema nodosum; 5. pyoderma gangrenosum.
### Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG)
- A serious ulcerating skin process, starting from small wounds, then enlarging with a necrotic center, with a cat's paw appearance, painful; found often at the arm and leg; diagnosed from the wound's appearance; PG wounds hardly heal; this is why one should not biopsy the wound (cause unknown); often found around an end ileostomy, which confuses residents with skin maceration; a patient with peristomal PG should be evaluated for IBD. Figure: photo (skip).
- Treatment: 1. local wound care; 2. many drugs to choose from: topical steroid, antibiotic, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, oral and injected; 3. preferred: high dose IV corticosteroid; some favor IV infliximab; the author thinks IV steroid should be primary; 4. if not healing, may need to move the ileostomy to a new site (relocation not needed in every case, only those where the bag cannot be sealed); 5. IV antibiotic and antifungal seldom help.
