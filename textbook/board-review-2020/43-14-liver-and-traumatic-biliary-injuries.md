# Liver and Traumatic Biliary Injuries
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 455-500 (PDF pages 2589-2634) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 24 พฤศจิกายน 2558)

## Introduction
- For liver, gallbladder and extrahepatic bile duct, the author assumes readers already have reasonable basic anatomy; if forgotten, read the liver and biliary surgery chapters first to follow this one.

## Liver Injury
- Since good-quality CT: accurate grading, reliable detection of associated injury, more skilled doctors and better-equipped hospitals; 75% of liver bleeding comes from the low-pressure portal vein, so much of it stops by itself.
- With well-selected patients, outcome is reliably good — unlike the past when most were explored, and at laparotomy 50-67% had already stopped bleeding. Today, for blunt trauma in a well-equipped center, the author thinks the chance of operation is no more than 30%.

## AAST Liver Injury Scale

| Grade | Type | Injury description |
|---|---|---|
| I | Hematoma | Subcapsular, nonexpanding, < 10 cm surface area |
| I | Laceration | Capsular tear, nonbleeding, < 1 cm parenchymal depth |
| II | Hematoma | Subcapsular, nonexpanding, 10 to 50% surface area; intraparenchymal nonexpanding < 10 in diameter (as printed) |
| II | Laceration | Capsular tear, active bleeding; 1-3 cm parenchymal depth, < 10 cm in length |
| III | Hematoma | Subcapsular, > 50% surface area or expanding; ruptured subcapsular hematoma with active bleeding; intraparenchymal hematoma > 10 cm or expanding |
| III | Laceration | > 3 cm parenchymal depth |
| IV | Hematoma | Ruptured intraparenchymal hematoma with active bleeding |
| IV | Laceration | Parenchymal disruption involving 25 to 75% of hepatic lobe or 1-3 Couinaud's segments within a single lobe |
| V | Laceration | Parenchymal disruption involving > 75% of hepatic lobe or > 3 Couinaud's segments within a single lobe |
| V | Vascular | Juxtahepatic venous injuries (i.e. retrohepatic vena cava/central major hepatic veins) |
| VI | Vascular | Hepatic avulsion |

(Table 30-1, Liver injury scale, 1994 revision; footnote: advance one grade for multiple injuries, up to grade III.)
- Subcapsular hematoma usually follows blunt trauma: blood collects in the liver substance or under the liver surface without a tear of Glisson's capsule.
- Grade I, II = minor injury; grade III and above = severe injury.
- Grade VI (hepatic avulsion) seems too severe to survive.
- Caption points: solid organ injury is a major cause of death in abdominal injury and is best evaluated with IV contrast-enhanced CT; the liver is prone to injury and is the most common cause of death after abdominal trauma; injury patterns are subcapsular hematoma, parenchymal laceration, parenchymal hematoma and devascularization; most blunt liver trauma is treated conservatively because many hepatic injuries stop bleeding spontaneously.

## Management of Liver Injury
- Now two approaches:
  1. Nonoperative management
  2. Operative management

## Nonoperative Management (NOM)
- Now the standard treatment for blunt trauma; even grade V succeeds about 20-30%.
- Penetrating injury: success reported in some cases — select carefully: fully conscious, no peritonitis, stable vital signs.
- **Criteria for selecting patients** — the author likes Trunkey's (ป๋า Trunkey), adapted:
  1. Hemodynamically stable from admission, or BP dropped early but stayed stable after initial resuscitation, needing ≤ 4 unit blood replacement
  2. No indication for laparotomy, e.g. no peritonitis, no significant associated intraabdominal organ injury
  3. Hospital ready: ICU with good monitoring, OR available 24 hr, lab and blood bank ready
  4. Good-quality CT, a good radiologist to read it, and someone to do embolization after hours
  5. Surgeon available to re-examine the patient frequently in person and return to the OR at any time, without the family interfering or a backstabber around (หวาดเสียว, as printed)
- CT should be triple contrast for accurate evaluation of hollow viscus.
- Feliciano's view: intra-abdominal blood ≤ 500 cc → NOM is reasonable; but CT showing blood far away, e.g. pooled in the pelvis, means heavy bleeding → likely operation.
- NOM succeeds more in children than adults: success 90% in children, 50-80% in adults.
- Head injury: NOM was once forbidden; now NOM is acceptable if capability and monitoring are good, and above all the patient is hemodynamically stable.
- **NOM guideline for blunt liver injury** — the author likes the guideline in Trauma 7 ed. 2013 p.544; summary:
  1. Meets NOM criteria, emphasis on hemodynamic stability:
     - 1.1 Grade 1, 2, 3 → admit and monitor on the ward; if instability develops → explore. The author wants you to consider, before laparotomy, whether embolization is possible.
     - 1.2 Grade 4, 5 → admit and monitor in ICU until stable, then move to the ward; at least 48-72 hr in ICU.
  2. Whenever there is increasing abdominal pain, jaundice or unexplained sepsis → repeat CT:
     - 2.1 Pseudoaneurysm or hemobilia → embolization
     - 2.2 Collection → percutaneous drainage
     - 2.3 CT unchanged from before → look for another cause
- **Approach**
  1. NPO
  2. NG and Foley
  3. Bed rest, especially the first 3 days
  4. Monitor vital signs, Hct, urine output
  5. Examine the patient regularly
- Compared with operation, NOM does not lengthen hospital stay or use more blood; NOM complications only 6%; importantly, mortality is not higher.
- **Angiographic embolization** has a role in NOM, especially:
  1. Grade 4, 5
  2. Persistent contrast blush
  3. Ongoing bleeding needing > 2 unit blood
  4. Hemobilia
  5. Pseudoaneurysm or AV fistula
  6. Failed pack removal
- **Observation — patients likely to fail NOM**
  1. Age > 55 years
  2. Multiple injury
  3. Head injury
  4. Grade 4, 5
  5. Transfusion > 4-6 unit

## Follow Up CT Scan
- **Inpatient management**
  1. Grade 1, 2, stable and clinically well → follow-up CT rarely needed; repeat CT for more pain, fever or Hct drop.
  2. Grade 3 → still debated; the author favours doing it.
  3. Grade 3, 4, 5 (to be sure, at least 4 and 5) → repeat CT before discharge from ICU; some anxious surgeons repeat at day 7 — acceptable.
- **Outpatient management** — per Mattox 2013 p.544 guideline:
  1. Grade 1, 2, 3 → no repeat CT unless pain and jaundice
  2. Grade 4, 5 → follow-up CT at 1 month. Significant healing → normal activity allowed; poor healing → limit activity and repeat CT every 1 month.
- **Expected follow-up CT findings**
  1. Resorption of blood within 2 weeks
  2. Coalescence of the liver laceration
  3. Defect gets smaller
  4. Complete restoration of liver homogeneity within 4 months
- Note (หมายเหตุ): FU CT is not routine; the surgeon decides from clinical signs and symptoms, which matter more.

## Resumption of Activity
- In general, wait at least 1 month after liver injury before normal activity: torn liver needs 3 weeks to heal to wound strength.
- Heavy activity or contact sports: wait 3-6 months and CT before returning to play; safe if healing > 95%.

## Operative Management
- **Indication for surgery**
  1. Hemodynamically unstable — persistent shock not improving
  2. Peritonitis
  3. Other associated injury needing operation
  4. Failure of NOM
  5. Transfusion > 2 unit needed to keep Hct steady
  6. Hospital and supporting departments not ready
- **Principles**
  1. Bleeding control
  2. Removal devitalized tissue
  3. Establishment of adequate drainage

## Operative Principles
1. Prep from chin to knees; may need sternotomy, or extend the incision to the xiphoid, or laterally (lateral subcostal).
2. Incision: start with upper midline; enter the abdomen fast, do not dawdle. Opening on a ruptured liver is not elective surgery — on entry the patient goes into shock from loss of tamponade effect, so ask the anesthetist to help load warm IV, and no fussing — the surgeon is tense.
3. "Pray, then scoop" (สวมวิญญาณ หลวงพ่อโกย, as printed): scoop the small bowel out of the abdomen, scoop out blood clot, use suction. The author's eyes are failing now, so he removes the suction tip and uses the bare tubing, alternating with fast hand palpation to feel the rupture.
4. Pack all 4 quadrants; if only the liver is ruptured, remove the liver pack last.
5. After removing the packs, if bleeding has stopped or slowed, decide what to do next as appropriate; if still actively bleeding, the author re-packs and then does Pringle.
6. Cutting ligaments to mobilize the liver: at first cut only what is needed; try pressing the liver dome downward by hand, carefully. If dark blood gushes out, beware bleeding from a retrohepatic vein — ask the anesthetist to stop bagging or pause the ventilator briefly; if the dark bleeding slows, the author thinks "unlucky today" — this is probably retrohepatic vein injury and will likely need packing and stopping there.
7. If retrohepatic vein bleeding is not suspected, the author likes to mobilize the liver up onto the abdominal wall, as in formal hepatectomy.

## Temporary Control Bleeding
- Four commonly used methods:
  1. **Manual compression** — our hand or an assistant's squeezes the liver; the author places a swab and presses by hand about 5-10 minutes.
  2. **Swab packing** — both above and below the liver.
  3. **Pringle maneuver** — clamping up to 90 minutes has been reported, but in practice the author clamps only 15-20 minutes then releases 5 minutes, which seems safer.
  4. **Tourniquet application** — penrose drain looped around the liver and tightened; suits ruptures of the lateral segment of the left lobe. The author does not like it — the clamp gets in the way. Similar to the liver hanging maneuver, with penrose instead of tape.

## Operative Approach
- How to handle a liver injury found at laparotomy depends on severity.
- **Shallow wound, not bleeding or only oozing** → press with swab or sponge first; if still annoyingly oozing, cauterize a couple of spots, or apply glue, or cover with gel foam or surgicel.
- **Subcapsular hematoma** — not pulsatile, not expanding, not ruptured: remember the old teaching "don't dig, don't meddle" (ห้ามคุ้ย อย่าซน) — leave it alone; if there is a problem after operation, embolization can help.
- Deep tear that pressure cannot control, a star-shaped burst, or as Trunkey says "like being clawed by a bear" — follow these:
  1. **Hepatorrhaphy** — for tears ≤ 3 cm deep: blunt-tipped liver needle, suture the liver; the author likes to tuck omentum into the hole and tie — just tight enough.
  2. **Tractotomy with suture ligation or clip** — for wounds > 3 cm deep: Pringle first, then use the left thumb and index finger to gently pinch (as printed, Phramongkutklao residents are experts at this) and split the liver tissue open, while the right hand clamps or clips the bleeding points.
  3. **Omentorrhaphy** — for deep ruptures where tractotomy is not feasible: pack omentum into the wound for tamponade effect; the author tacks it to the liver edge with chromic so the omentum does not slip out.
  4. **Resectional debridement** — for a shattered liver with pieces nearly detached: remove the shattered liver; the author covers the raw surface with omentum.
  5. **Perihepatic packing** — for retrohepatic vein tear, or damage control, especially severe bilobar injury that cannot be controlled. Packing stops venous bleeding well but arterial bleeding poorly, so before packing control arterial bleeding as much as possible.
- **How to pack the liver** (วิธี pack ตับ)
  1. Mobilize around the liver without tearing it. Before cutting ligaments, look for a lot of clot behind/under; if so, do not plunge in — it may be retrohepatic venous injury.
  2. Pack behind the liver first; avoid over-packing at the IVC. Then pack around the liver clockwise, above and below, just enough to stop bleeding; beware ACS. Some place a plastic drape before the swabs so that at pack removal liver tissue is not torn off with the swab — to each his own; the author puts the swabs straight in. Remember: pack the swabs gently so the liver does not tear more, and never stuff swabs into the liver wound.
  3. After packing, temporary abdominal closure and move to ICU (as printed, the resident on duty has to accept a sleepless night).
- **How to remove the packs** (วิธี off packing)
  1. Usually removed about 48-72 hours after packing.
  2. Before pulling swabs out, keep irrigating with water, then pull gently; control any small bleeders; wash and remove dead liver tissue.
  3. Bleeding well controlled → place drain and close.
  4. Not controlled → re-pack, then embolization post op (จำ).
  5. Second pack removal: as printed, find someone else willing to take it off for you.
- **Methods to avoid in severe liver injury**
  1. Big needles taking large deep liver sutures → ischemia of that part of the liver, necrosis and abscess. The author tucks omentum in and sutures just enough to stop bleeding.
  2. **Extrahepatic artery ligation** — loses a lot of good liver; if ligating, take one hepatic artery branch, not the hepatic artery proper. If embolization is available, choose it — more selective.
  3. **Anatomical resection** — takes long, bleeds a lot, removes more liver than necessary; except when the liver is nearly detached or a hepatic vein is torn.
  4. **Mesh hepatorrhaphy** — absorbable mesh wrapped tightly enough to stop bleeding; may wrap 1 lobe or both.
     - Advantages: (1) no perihepatic packing, so no ACS; (2) no reopening to remove mesh as with packing.
     - Disadvantages: (1) time-consuming, especially wrapping at the porta hepatis — by the time the wrap is done, more blood is lost; (2) cannot be used with retrohepatic venous injury.
  5. **OLT** — for an unsalvageable shattered liver; usually pack first and wait for a donor liver, which is hard. One successful case was reported; the author did not follow up whether the patient died.
- Drains: about 2 Jackson-Pratt drains, only for liver injury grade 3, 4, 5, tips at the raw liver surface; remove when dry and the patient is eating.
- T-tube to reduce bile leakage has no benefit (as printed, anyone doing it deserves a smack on the head).
- If the abdomen is a mess and ileus will surely be prolonged → place a feeding jejunostomy.

## Summary: Guideline Operative Management of Liver Injury
Figure: Operative management algorithm (สรุป), as steps —
1. Bleeding coming from right upper quadrant → take down falciform ligament; inspect and palpate liver; temporarily control bleeding with packing or Pringle maneuver as needed; make initial assessment of grade of liver injury.
2. **Minor injury (grade I or II)** → apply topical agents; do not drain; close abdomen.
3. **Moderate to severe injury (grade III, IV or V), bleeding controlled with Pringle** → divide coronary and triangular ligaments and open liver parenchyma as needed to expose injuries; topical agents for areas with minimal injury; for superficial injuries ligate individual bleeding vessels or close parenchyma with sutures. Then:
   - Bleeding controlled → close abdomen without drains.
   - Bleeding continues, mostly low pressure before Pringle → suture bleeding vessels even deep in the parenchyma; pack abdomen if necessary; drain as indicated; close. If packed → remove packs in 1 or 2 days.
   - Bleeding continues, mostly high pressure before Pringle → suture bleeding vessels even deep in parenchyma; if necessary ligate right or left hepatic artery; drain as indicated; close.
4. **Moderate to severe injury (grade III, IV or V), bleeding not controlled with Pringle** → divide coronary and triangular ligaments as needed for exposure; topical agents and buttressed sutures as indicated; if bleeding persists, use packs, potentially as definitive treatment. Then:
   - Bleeding controlled → close abdomen without drains; remove packs in 1 or 2 days.
   - Bleeding continues → extend midline celiotomy into median sternotomy for exposure; control bleeding with intrahepatic balloon tamponade, atriocaval shunt or vascular isolation as necessary; repair hepatic vein or vena cava injury; drain as indicated; close.
5. All paths → follow for postinjury complications (bleeding, abscess, hemobilia, etc.); evaluate and treat with arteriography, embolization, imaging and drainage as indicated.

## Transhepatic Penetrating Injury
- GSW through the liver is very serious — bleeds torrentially.
- If lucky and bleeding has already stopped: leave it alone (ห้ามซน ห้ามคุ้ย).
- Most cases are not so lucky — bleeding along the tract: look and feel, or if CT was done preop, re-check how deep the tract is. If shallow, open it up (tractotomy) and suture the bleeders.
- Most tracts are quite deep — opening the liver risks death. Quickly ask the circulating nurse for a Foley; push the Foley into the tract and inflate the balloon with water — never air (a burst balloon → air emboli). Then pull the Foley back and forth; if bleeding stops, the balloon is pressing on the bleeding point — very lucky.
- If a Foley cannot plug it → **balloon tamponade**: cut side holes in a Foley, slip a penrose over it, tie both ends, push into the tract, inflate with fluid mixed with contrast until it balloons, keep 2-3 days, then deflate; if still bleeding, consult for embolization.
- The author has also stuffed a large bundle of omentum into the tract with drains at the entry and exit holes — lucky it stopped (it worked).
- Sengstaken-Blakemore tube, recommended by some: the author thinks it is too expensive, and fears the gastric balloon, even deflated, may scrape and tear the liver on removal.

## Hepatic Artery and Portal Vein Injury
- Principle: repair if repairable.
- In damage control, ligate.
- Details in the intraabdominal vascular injury chapter.

## Retained Bullet within Hepatic Parenchyma
- Whether to remove — the author's guide:
  1. Shallow and easy → remove.
  2. Deep, digging may cause trouble, or patient is high operative risk → leave the bullet.

## Juxtrahepatic Venous Injury
- = retrohepatic vena cava and hepatic vein injury; hard to expose and the liver injury is usually severe — whoever meets it is surely unlucky, as mortality is > 80%.
- The author on duty met 3 cases bleeding torrentially — all 3 died; he expects his 4th would die too.
- **Preoperative diagnosis** is very hard — if known beforehand, we would push it to embolization.
- **Intraoperative diagnosis** — suspect if:
  1. Dark blood pouring out from behind the liver
  2. No portal vein injury, yet dark blood filling the lesser sac
  3. Pringle does not control it — ask the anesthetist to stop the ventilator briefly to lower intrathoracic pressure; if it is really this injury, the bleeding lessens.
- **Management**
  1. Stable retrohepatic hematoma → absolutely do not touch it; manipulating or mobilizing the liver loses the tamponade effect → certain death.
  2. Actively bleeding → the author recommends perihepatic packing and getting out fast; after 48-72 hours, re-operate or try embolization first (as printed, the family will not mind).
  3. If the author has to be the one removing the packs, he will find a reason to go to a meeting, a course, or get diarrhoea needing emergency admission (as printed).
  4. If no one can be found to take it over and you must operate yourself: do it in the morning, fresh, go in gently, pray throughout.
     - 4.1 If lucky and bleeding has stopped → just irrigate, place drain, and observe.
     - 4.2 Still bleeding → may pack again, then post-op consult intervention (คุณป๋า intervention) for embolization; if not possible or not working, you must go in yourself — read on.
     - 4.3 Prepare the chart and films — you will surely present at the MM report.

## Operative Approach (juxtahepatic venous injury)
- Usually needed when perihepatic packing has failed; in theory, get control or vascular isolation first.
- **Vascular isolation technique**
  1. **Shunt approach**
     - 1.1 Atriocaval shunt or Schrock shunt
     - 1.2 Saphenofemoral shunt or Moore Pilcher shunt
     - 1.3 Venovenous bypass
  2. **Nonshunt approach**
     - 2.1 Heaney's maneuver
     - 2.2 Pringle maneuver with finger fracture to direct repair

## Atriocaval (Schrock) Shunt
- Vascular isolation from suprahepatic vena cava to suprarenal vena cava, while blood from the lower body still flows through the shunt into the right atrium.
- Know it a little for when the examiners grill you; the author has never done one — just thinking of the shunt's name and the patient has already arrested.
- **Technique** (by the textbook)
  1. Open sternotomy (as printed)
  2. ET tube or ICD no. 36 through the atrial appendage, down so the tip lies in the infrarenal vena cava; or a large Foley inserted via the infrarenal vena cava upward
  3. Cord tape snare at the intrapericardial vena cava and the suprarenal vena cava
  4. Blow the balloon, then Rumel tourniquet at the snares
  5. As printed: the author says merely thinking of its name, the patient is probably dead already

## Saphenofemoral (Moore Pilcher) Shunt
- Balloon shunt: a balloon catheter inserted at the saphenofemoral junction in the groin and pushed up the IVC under fluoroscopy; when the bleeding point is seen, inflate the balloon to plug the hole so it can be sutured.
- Same as before — just thinking of it, you are already at MM.

## Venovenous Bypass
- A tube shunts blood from the infrarenal vena cava below into the heart via the axillary vein (as in OLT).
- Figure: venovenous bypass — saphenofemoral vein access and portal vein drain to a pump (with flow probe), returning via axillary vein access; suprahepatic and infrahepatic IVC clamped.

## Heaney Maneuver
- Consists of:
  1. Clamp suprahepatic and infrahepatic vena cava
  2. Pringle maneuver
- Load IV before clamping the IVC — BP drops as soon as you clamp.
- Then go in and fight it out (the patient usually dies); few people favour this method.

## Pringle Maneuver with Finger Fracture
- Associated with Pachter (น้า Pachter), who said Pringle can be held 77 minutes; he strongly dislikes the Schrock shunt. For juxtahepatic venous injury he does Pringle, then finger fracture into the liver for direct repair, or if the liver is nearly detached, hepatectomy to reach and repair.
- Pachter does not believe in shunts; he did not state his statistics, but they are surely better than the author's.
- Traditionally OLT is the last resort we dream of.
- From the author's limited experience: at a 4th case he would not fight on — pack, then buy time for embolization. Some have placed an endovascular IVC stent using a venous endograft with reported success (to each his own).

## Complications of Liver Injury
- Only those you should know:
  1. Hepatic necrosis or abscess
  2. Biloma
  3. Bile fistula
  4. Hemobilia
  5. Bilhemia

## Hepatic Necrosis or Abscess
- Symptoms: fever, RUQ pain, localized peritonitis.
- Lab: raised white count, raised N.
- CT with IV contrast: nonperfused liver parenchyma, possibly fluid collection around that part of the liver, or abscess.
- Treatment: explore, debride necrotic tissue, place drain.

## Biloma
- Usually in high-grade injury: bile leaks from an intrahepatic bile duct.
- Symptoms: may be silent early, for weeks or months, then fever, RUQ pain, jaundice.
- CT scan: collection; must be distinguished from abscess or resolving hematoma.
- **Treatment**
  1. US or CT guided percutaneous drainage + ERCP & sphincterotomy with stent; 72% have reduced bile leak within 2 days; complete resolution takes 10-47 days.
  2. Surgery when nonoperative treatment fails — usually debride the mushy liver and patch with omentum.

## Bile Fistula
- Bile leak from the drain site > 50 cc for > 2 weeks.
- Symptoms: fever, abdominal distension, RUQ pain.
- **Investigation**
  1. Initial study — the author chooses MRC
  2. If treatment is wanted — then ERCP
- Treatment: percutaneous drainage with ERCP & EST, then biliary stent. Do not forget nutrition — enteral before TPN.

## Hemobilia
- Blunt or penetrating injury → a false aneurysm ruptures into the bile duct, creating a connection.
- **Classic triad**
  1. Upper GI bleeding — some severe enough to cause shock
  2. Obstructive jaundice
  3. RUQ pain
- **Diagnosis**
  1. Upper endoscopy: red blood spurting from the ampulla
  2. CT with IV contrast: may show a cavity in the liver
  3. If hemobilia is known and only one test is allowed, the author chooses angiogram, so embolization can follow at once
- **Treatment**
  1. Angiographic embolization is best — both diagnostic and therapeutic; in good hands success rate > 95%.
  2. Needing operation is very unlucky — usually failed embolization, large cavity or sepsis → open hepatic resection of that part.
- Figure caption (TIPS complication): unintentional hepatic artery injury during TIPS caused post-procedural hemobilia, treated by right hepatic artery branch embolization.

## Bilhemia or Biliovenous Fistula
- Communication between bile duct and IVC or HV.
- Rare but very severe; usually with a large subcapsular hematoma — as the clot resorbs, bile from the torn duct seeps into an intrahepatic vein.
- Lab: bilirubin quite high.
- Diagnosis: ERCP or MRCP shows the bile duct communicating with hepatic vein or IVC.
- **Treatment**
  1. First reduce bile flowing into the IVC or HV by:
     - 1.1 Endoscopic stenting, or
     - 1.2 T-tube as well
  2. Hepatectomy of the part with the communication
- Only 3 cases reported — whoever puts it in an exam deserves a curse (as printed).

## Gallbladder Injury
- In trauma, usually from penetrating injury with associated injury to nearby organs, especially liver.
- Usually diagnosed at operation.
- CT may show a gallbladder tear or bile collection.
- **Treatment**
  1. Cholecystectomy is the treatment of choice.
  2. Some show-offs like to gamble on the post-op course: for a stab wound where the knife tip just punctured the gallbladder, suture with chromic and leave a drain; worse still, some recommend tube cholecystostomy through the hole to preserve the gallbladder — as printed, crazy (ประสาท).
- The author: even with isolated gallbladder injury or only cystic duct injury, or when the right hepatic artery must be ligated, follows his teachers — don't turn something easy into something hard; at our very expert level, do fundus-downward cholecystectomy — in 3-5 minutes the gallbladder comes away in your hand.

## Extrahepatic Bile Duct Injury
- About 90% from penetrating injury, usually partial transection; blunt trauma usually gives complete transection at a fairly fixed point — the pancreaticoduodenal junction.
- Suspect when bile staining mixes with hematoma around the portal triad — explore it a little. If missed, from post-op day 1 to 6 weeks the patient gets fever, distension, anorexia, jaundice; tapping may yield bile; if unlucky, sepsis and death.
- Simple severity split the author finds works well:
  1. Simple injury: tear < 50% of ductal wall
  2. Complex injury: tear > 50% of ductal wall
- **Operative treatment**
  1. Unstable, needs damage control → just place a T-tube and drain, and get out.
  2. Stable → decide case by case; general principles:
     - 2.1 Injury ≤ 50% of duct wall, tissue not bruised → primary repair, with stent (or T tube) brought out away from the repair.
     - 2.2 Injury > 50% of duct wall, badly bruised, much tissue loss → biliary enteric anastomosis: Roux en Y jejunum to the proximal part, with stent.
     - 2.2 (as printed, numbered again) High injury hard to reconstruct, e.g. right or left hepatic duct, where repair or reconstruction is impossible → ligation is an option; that liver lobe atrophies, usually without problems of biliary cirrhosis.
- In a center, or with a skilled junior/colleague around, bile duct repair or reconstruction should be done by someone experienced. If not, ask yourself whether you can refer; if you can, place a T tube and 2-3 Jackson Pratt drains and refer. In real life referral is mostly hard, so if doing it yourself follow these basic principles.
- **Principles operative management of bile duct injury**
  1. Minimal dissection — do not needlessly destroy the vessels alongside supplying the bile duct; know the bile duct blood supply well.
  2. Debridement back to healthy tissue
  3. Mucosa to mucosa suturing, with absorbable suture
  4. Without tension
  5. Stent — trauma patients usually have small ducts; the author stents if duct diameter < 1 cm — safer, at least decompresses, and allows contrast injection.

## Parting Advice (เรื่องฝาก...ก่อนจาก)
- Be careful with nonoperative management: talk and explain the steps to the patient, relatives and the neighbours too.
- If it fails and you must operate, this avoids complaints such as "the surgeon operated on my relative too late — did you look down on us as poor?"
- If the hospital is not ready, why not refer? (as printed, joke about a relative claiming a VIP "gold card").
- Remember at all times: "If the patient doesn't die, we're even; if he dies, we're both exhausted and unlucky" (คนไข้ไม่ตาย เสมอตัว แต่ถ้าน้าเขาตาย เราทั้งเหนื่อย ทั้งซวย).
- If you end up alone on duty with no help available: operate, but tell the family plainly — 50-50 (% survive-die). If nothing else can be done, pack and find a way to send the patient to a senior to manage.
