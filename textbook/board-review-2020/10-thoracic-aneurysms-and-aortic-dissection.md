# Thoracic Aneurysms and Aortic Dissection 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 438-458 (PDF pages 442-462)

## Introduction
- Of thoracic aorta diseases, the ones a resident should know are probably just aneurysm and dissection. A 3rd-year resident may see them in MCQ; unlikely to be asked in oral. Summarised simply so it is easy to take in (as printed: "น้องๆก็น่าจะกลืนได้หมด ....นิ่มมาก").

## Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm (TAA)
- The thoracic aorta is divided anatomically into 4 parts.
- Figure: Thoracic aorta segments —
  1. Aortic root — valve, annulus and sinuses (sinuses of Valsalva); separated from the ascending aorta by the sinotubular junction
  2. Ascending aorta — root to the origin of the right brachiocephalic A
  3. Aortic arch — right brachiocephalic A to the attachment of the ligamentum arteriosum; proximal (right brachiocephalic artery to Lt subclavian A) and distal/isthmus (Lt subclavian A to attachment of the ligamentum arteriosum)
  4. Descending thoracic aorta — ligamentum arteriosum to the aortic hiatus in the diaphragm; aortic spindle = most proximal portion of the descending thoracic aorta appears slightly dilated
- In the text:
  1. Aortic root consists of the parts of the aortic valve.
  2. Ascending aorta: from the sinotubular junction groove to the start of the innominate artery; this part is still inside the pericardium.
  3. Aortic arch: from the origin of the innominate artery to the origin of the left subclavian artery.
  4. Descending aorta: from part 3 down to the diaphragm.

## Basic of TAA
- Normal thoracic aorta size depends on location: mean about 3.5-3.9 cm at the root, 2.9 cm at the ascending aorta, 2.5-2.9 cm at mid-descending. Larger than normal by > 50% = aneurysm.
- True aneurysm: wall has all 3 layers. Pseudoaneurysm (false aneurysm): wall lacks all 3 layers, usually connective tissue and blood clot.
- TAA is much less common than AAA (TAA 25%, AAA 75%).
- Most TAA are asymptomatic, often found by chance on x-ray; as printed, residents no longer look at plain films and send CT chest whenever something looks odd, and the radiologist reports TAA — having a radiologist friend in your LINE group is useful (as printed, joke).
- 13% have TAA at several sites; 20-25% occur together with AAA.
- If you remember the basics of AAA, the principles are similar; the problem with TAA is that it must be treated before it ruptures. Diagnosis and indications for surgery are similar to AAA.
- **TAA vs AAA**

| | Thoracic | Abdominal |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | AA greater than 4 cm; DA greater than 3 cm. (1) DA should never be larger than the AA at a given scan level; (2) ratio of coronal diameter of AA to DA should be about 1.5:1 | Focal dilatation of the abdominal aorta 50% greater than the proximal normal segment, or > 3 cm in maximum diameter |
| Frequency | Less common | More common |
| Cause | Atherosclerosis (M.C) | Atherosclerosis (M.C) |
| Presentation | Often identified incidentally on chest imaging. Pain (m.c): ascending (anterior), arch (neck pain), descending (mid-scapular); due to compression or rupture | Mostly asymptomatic unless they leak or rupture; ruptured aneurysms present with severe abdominal or back pain and hypotension/shock |
| Usual site | Descending aorta (at the level of the ligamentum arteriosum, just distal to the origin of the subclavian artery) | Below the level of origin of renal arteries |

- **Causes of TAA** — depend on location:
  1. Ascending: often from cystic medial necrosis (40%), seen in Marfan syndrome
  2. Otherwise TAA is from atherosclerosis
- Causes (list):
  - Atherosclerosis (most common)
  - Chronic aortic dissection
  - Vasculitis, e.g. Takayasu arteritis
  - Connective tissue disorders: Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome
  - Mycotic aneurysm
  - Traumatic pseudoaneurysm
  - Anastomotic pseudoaneurysm
  - Inflammatory abdominal aortic aneurysm
- Imaging study of choice for TAA: **CTA** (gold standard); if kidney problems, use MRA instead.
- Aortography is still used in cases undergoing intervention (TEVAR).
- Transesophageal echo (TEE) shows the thoracic aorta well; can be done at the bedside or in the OR; suits unstable patients who cannot go to the CT scanner. However, TEE usually needs sedation or GA — a struggling patient risks esophageal injury.
- Every patient needs treatment of underlying diseases, e.g. HT, DM, COPD, lipid control, stop smoking.
- Decision to operate: if asymptomatic, depends on size; if symptomatic — e.g. compressing nearby organs, compressing the airway causing dyspnea, SVC compression → SVC syndrome, aortic valve insufficiency with CHF, esophageal compression → dysphagia, recurrent laryngeal nerve compression → hoarseness — treat regardless of size.
- **General indications for surgery for TAA**
  1. Diameter: ascending > 5.5 cm; descending > 6.5 cm (Schwartz uses 6 cm)
  2. Growth dilatation > 0.5 cm/yr
  3. With underlying connective tissue disease (Marfan)
  4. Associated with AI (TAA stretches the valve further)
  5. Compressive symptoms
- Besides open surgery, TEVAR is now also a standard treatment.
- **Ruptured TAA** (overall rupture rate 20%)
  1. Strong predictor = diameter of the TAA
     - 1.1 Ascending aorta: diameter > 6 cm → rupture risk 31%
     - 1.2 Descending aorta: diameter > 7 cm → rupture risk 43%
  2. Age: the older, the easier to rupture
  3. Saccular aneurysm ruptures more often than fusiform

| Size (cm) | Ascending | Descending |
|---|---|---|
| Risk for rupture | 6 | 5 |
| Treatment | 5.5 | 6.5 |
| Monitoring | 4.5 | 5.5 |

- Normal growth rate 0.12 cm/year.
- **Fusiform aneurysm**: spindle shape (symmetrical dilatation), from atherosclerosis; bulges around the whole circumference (a localized dilation in which the entire circumference is distended → elongated, tubular or spindle-like swelling).
- **Saccular aneurysm**: outpouching of only part of the aortic wall (a distended sac affecting only part of the arterial circumference); usually from infection or trauma; this type ruptures easily, often needs early surgery.
- TAA is divided into 4 types (enough for a general surgery resident to have the idea): ascending, arch, descending and thoracoabdominal aneurysm. (Figure labels: aortic root aneurysm, ascending aortic aneurysm, aortic arch aneurysm, descending aortic aneurysm.)

## Ascending Aortic Aneurysm
- Most common site.
- Cause: patients with connective tissue disease, e.g. Marfan, with cystic medial necrosis.
- **Marfan patients**
  1. Connective tissue disease, inherited AD; gene controlling myofibril production is reduced → weak media; risk of aneurysm or dissection of the ascending aorta; 50% die before age 45
  2. 90% die from CHF, AR, aortic dissection and rupture into the pericardium (tamponade)
- **Symptoms**
  1. Mostly asymptomatic, found by chance on chest film. In some, TAA here stretches the aortic valve → aortic regurgitation (AR) with diastolic murmur at right 2nd ICS; AR is the most common complication.
  2. If symptomatic, most common symptom = anterior chest pain (not posterior); as it enlarges: compressing vertebra → back pain, RLN → hoarseness, bronchus → dyspnea, esophagus → dysphagia.
- **Treatment**
  1. Operate when indicated; Marfan patients usually need surgery earlier than others; do coronary angiogram before surgery.
  2. **Indication for surgery**
     - 2.1 Pain or compression, or AR, or dissection
     - 2.2 Size ≥ 5.5 cm
     - 2.3 Size ≥ 5 cm in Marfan
     - 2.4 Progressive enlargement > 0.5 cm/yr
     - 2.5 Severe aortic insufficiency
  3. **Operation** — depends on aortic valve involvement:
     - 3.1 None → valve sparing operation or aortic root reimplantation (figure from Schwartz edition 11 p. 864)
     - 3.2 With AR → modified Bentall operation or composite valve replacement: replace both aortic root and aortic valve with a prosthetic valve attached to a vascular graft (don't get confused; it's not on the exam — as printed, the author has never seen this operation either); this operation includes valve replacement.
     - 3.3 Endovascular treatment is not yet recommended here.

## Transverse Aortic Arch Aneurysm
- Seen in the elderly, from atherosclerosis; mostly asymptomatic, found by chance on film.
- If symptomatic, usually pressure symptoms, e.g. chest pain, or dysphagia from compression of the esophagus.
- **Indication for surgery**: same as ascending TAA.
- Surgery here has the highest mortality compared with other TAA sites because the arch gives branches to the brain (carotid). CVT colleagues reduce mortality during surgery with deep hypothermia, increased brain perfusion by antegrade or retrograde cerebral perfusion, then circulatory arrest. Current popular approach = hybrid operation: open surgery plus endovascular stent graft, e.g. elephant trunk procedure; another option = total aortic arch replacement. How CVT colleagues choose is their business; as printed, general surgeons operating on bowel and hernia have an easier life (joke).
- Figure (no labels beyond names): hybrid type 1 (type II, type III) and hybrid type 2 (type I) arch repairs; total aortic arch replacement; frozen elephant trunk.

## Thoracoabdominal Aneurysm (also Descending Aortic Aneurysm)
- Usually elderly with extensive atherosclerosis; mostly asymptomatic, often found by chance on chest film; symptomatic ones are usually quite large.
- Diagnosis and treatment indications are the same as TAA elsewhere.
- **Crawford classification** — by extent of the TAA; now expanded from 4 to 5 types (shown just so residents know of it; as printed, "พี่ก็ลงทุนอ่านนะ").
- Figure: Crawford classification (Extent I-V) —
  - Type I: left subclavian A to renal A
  - Type II: left subclavian A to aortic bifurcation
  - Type III: mid-descending aorta to aortic bifurcation
  - Type IV: upper abdominal aorta [unclear — label cut off]
  - Extent V: shown in the figure, no text label
- **Problems of this surgery**
  1. Renal failure is the most common complication
  2. Paraplegia (often asked): about 2-3% in TEVAR, but 20% with open repair
- **Paraplegia**: an important complication residents may be asked about. From surgery or graft placement occluding the greater reticular artery or artery of Adamkiewicz (Adamchewicz, as printed) — a branch of the aorta supplying the cord at level T9-L2 → spinal cord ischemia → paralysis of both legs.
- Figure text: the largest of the radicular arteries is the artery of Adamkiewicz, often given off at the level of T10 but it can vary in position from T7 to L4.
- **Prevention of paraplegia** (วิธีป้องกัน)
  1. Keep operating (clamp) time < 30 min
  2. Distal shunt to reduce cord ischemia
  3. LP in the OR to reduce spinal pressure → more blood flow to the cord. (Figure text: CSF drainage through a spinal drain, usually controlled to maintain CSF pressure < 10-12 mmHg, reduces CSF pressure and improves spinal cord perfusion.)
  4. Keep aortic pressure > 60 mmHg; control (nasopharyngeal) temperature at about 32-34 °C
  5. Moderate heparinization 1 mg/kg

## Endovascular Repair (TEVAR)
- Now widely used; TAA type suitable for TEVAR = TAA starting below the Lt subclavian artery (descending aorta).
- Size of proximal and distal landing zones and the rules for TEVAR — learn them as a vascular fellow.
- Compared with open repair, TEVAR lowers mortality from 11.7% to 2.1% and paraplegia from 14% to 3%.
- **Problems of TEVAR**
  1. Unsuitable in kidney disease or iodine allergy (needs contrast)
  2. Arch and intra-abdominal segments have many branches → currently hybrid surgery: debranching of the aortic arch with ascending bicarotid or carotid-Lt subclavian bypass. Grafts with good side branches are starting to appear, so in future open surgery may not be needed.
  3. Aorta at the landing zone should not be > 4 cm, and the upper landing point must be ≥ 2 cm from the TAA
  4. TEVAR needs lifelong surveillance
  5. Endoleak can occur, as with EVAR
  6. Unsuitable for Marfan — progressive dilatation of the vessel → stent graft migration and endoleak

## Aortic Dissection
- One of the acute aortic syndromes: aortic dissection, intramural hematoma (IMH) and penetrating aortic ulcer (PAU).
- HT is present with aortic dissection in up to 95%. Uncommon but serious: dissection in pregnancy, in the third trimester or immediate postpartum period.
- Begins with an intimal tear; blood dissects into the media and splits the vessel lengthwise → 2 lumens: the original true lumen and a false lumen, separated by a thin flap. Blood in the false lumen compresses the true lumen, narrowing the aortic lumen. The tear usually involves 1/3 or ½ of the aortic wall.
- Figure (cross-section diagram): false lumen and true lumen separated by the intimal flap within the aortic wall.
- Diagnosis is often delayed: 39% of patients are diagnosed after 24 hours.
- Risk factors: hypertension 70%, family history 15%, Marfan, pregnancy.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ) — the dissection may extend forward (antegrade) or backward (retrograde dissection):
  1. Most common symptom: sudden severe tearing pain, like being stabbed through the chest to the back; may be mistaken for MI. Mainly anterior pain → think ascending aortic dissection; mainly posterior → think descending aortic dissection.
  2. Unequal pulse and BP between the two arms
  3. Coronary occlusion → myocardial infarction
  4. Carotid artery occlusion → stroke
  5. Sudden AR — the most common cardiac complication — from dilatation of the aortic root; diastolic murmur, from mural hematoma distorting the valve → heart failure (congestive heart failure)
  6. SMA occlusion → mesenteric ischemia
  7. If the aortic wall ruptures, the patient usually dies at once
- Figure (complications): entry tear; false lumen and true lumen; rupture; stroke; narrowing of artery; occlusion of coronary artery → myocardial infarction.
- **Chest film**: chest pain patients usually get a chest x-ray first; often normal, but may show widened mediastinum and loss of aortic knob silhouette. Don't forget EKG to rule out MI.
- Figure text: projecting atheromatous calcification > 1 cm inside the lateral aortic border >> suggestive of aortic dissection.
- **Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)**: for patients not stable enough to move to the CT scanner; easy, quick, at the bedside by a skilled operator (มือเทพ); shows the ascending aorta clearly, but blind spots are the aortic arch and descending aorta. Overall echo sensitivity 98%, specificity 95%.
- Diagnostic test of choice = **CTA**: sensitivity 100%, specificity 98%; shows false and true lumen and intimal flap.
- **Aortography**: now kept for cases where CTA is doubtful/inconclusive, and during TEVAR.
- Causes of death in dissection, especially dissection starting in the ascending aorta: aortic regurgitation → death from CHF, or coronary occlusion, or rupture → cardiac tamponade.
- **Classification**
  1. By time of diagnosis
     - 1.1 Acute phase: within 14 days
     - 1.2 Subacute phase: within 15-45 days
     - 1.3 Chronic phase: after 45 days
  2. By anatomy (more popular) — by site of origin of the aortic tear
     - 2.1 DeBakey
     - 2.2 Stanford (easy to remember; the author's favourite)
     - 2.3 Cleveland — by intimal tear, 5 types; no need to memorise
- **DeBakey classification**
  1. Type 1: starts in the ascending aorta, extends all the way to the abdominal aorta
  2. Type 2: starts in the ascending aorta and stops within the ascending aorta only
  3. Type 3A: starts distal to the Lt subclavian artery, stops in the descending thoracic aorta above the diaphragm
  4. Type 3B: starts distal to the Lt subclavian artery but extends down to the abdominal aorta
- **Stanford classification**
  1. Type A: dissection starts in the ascending aorta; entry usually about 5 cm above the aortic valve
  2. Type B: dissection starts in the descending aorta at the subclavian artery
  - Figure text: Type A = all dissections involving the ascending aorta, regardless of the site of origin; Type B = all dissections not involving the ascending aorta.

## Management of Aortic Dissection
- **Initial treatment** — same start whatever the type, once diagnosed:
  1. Intensive continuous monitoring
  2. Promptly reduce stress on the aortic wall: lower BP with IV beta-blocker, commonly esmolol (short duration of action); keep systolic ≤ 110 mmHg and pulse < 60/min. If beta-blocker is contraindicated (e.g. asthma, CHF, COPD), use calcium channel blocker instead; if still not controlled, then nitroprusside.
  3. Maintain HR with beta-blocker at ≤ 70 (as printed)
  4. Pain control
- **Initial medications for blood pressure control in aortic dissection**

| Medication class (agent) | Starting dose | Infusion regimen | Cautions for use |
|---|---|---|---|
| β-blockers: 1. Esmolol | Bolus 500 μg/kg | 50-200 μg/kg/min | COPD or high-risk bronchospasm; consider selective β-blocker such as metoprolol or atenolol |
| β-blockers: 2. Labetalol | Bolus 20 mg every 5-10 min to 80-300 mg total | 1-2 mg/min | (as above) |
| Sodium nitroprusside | — | 0.5-3.0 μg/kg/min | Concomitant use of β-blocker to avoid reflex tachycardia; possible cyanide toxicity with prolonged use |

- **Emergency surgery**
  1. Every Stanford type A — patients die from cardioaortic complications: AI, aortic rupture, coronary artery compromise. So type A needs emergency open surgical repair: median sternotomy and graft placement; if the aortic root is involved, replace the aortic root too. Endovascular stent graft is not yet standard treatment for type A.
     - Figure caption: (A) type A aortic dissection; (B) ascending aortic replacement and aortic valve repair (re-suspension); (C) Bentall procedure — ascending aortic and aortic root replacement and aortic valve replacement.
  2. Stanford type B → emergency surgery if:
     - 2.1 Aortic rupture
     - 2.2 Rapid expansion with persistent or uncontrollable pain
     - 2.3 Uncontrollable hypertension
     - 2.4 Compromised visceral or extremity artery = malperfusion syndrome
     - Rx: emergency TEVAR (thoracic endovascular aortic reconstruction, as printed) — stent graft via the femoral artery; success 94%, and mortality 50% lower than open repair.
- **Elective treatment**: for Stanford type B (distal to Lt subclavian artery) with no indication or complication requiring emergency surgery (e.g. rupture, malperfusion or persistent pain).
- In type B, if the patient is stable there is no hurry to operate: follow up with CT on day 2 or 3 and day 8 or 9 to see whether the aorta enlarges; if stable, continue beta-blocker. About 20-40% of type B become TAA and may later need surgery or TEVAR, with the same indications as TAA above.
- Open repair for aortic dissection type B is shown in a figure (no extra text).

## Penetrating Aortic Ulcer (PAU)
- One of the acute aortic syndromes (aortic dissection, intramural hematoma, penetrating aortic ulcer).
- Ulceration of an atheromatous plaque, from the intima deep into the media → may cause aortic dissection, or rupture into the adventitia → pseudoaneurysm.
- Treatment: endovascular intervention — cover the focal ulceration with a stent graft.
