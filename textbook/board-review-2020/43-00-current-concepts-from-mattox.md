# Current Concepts from Mattox in Trauma
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 1-7 (PDF pages 2135-2141) · Trauma part front matter

(Printed pp. 1-2 and 7 are mostly a personal tribute to Prof. Kenneth L. Mattox and photos; only clinical points are kept.)

## Recent work mentioned
- Dec 2015 article by Mattox on endovascular management of peripheral arterial injury at the thoracic outlet / upper extremity: endovascular treatment was feasible with much less bleeding than open technique; further study is still needed for selection criteria and long-term outcome.

## Resuscitation
- The 3:1 rule for fluid loading (crystalloid), taught by Tom Shires and followed for ~40 years, came from an animal (pig) study; Mattox says that loading IV fluid this way in uncontrolled hemorrhage makes more patients die.
- Give only as much fluid as needed; keep BP no higher than 80 mmHg = **hypotensive resuscitation**, until bleeding is controlled.
- Do not treat at the scene; the right thing is rapid transport to hospital. Surgeons going to the scene of a disaster/building collapse is "a waste of surgeon" (as printed).
- Monitoring can be simple: if the patient can still talk sensibly and a pedal pulse is palpable, that is OK.
- **Venous access**: antecubital fossa is still fine, but beware leak → arm swelling → compartment syndrome. If possible, do not start IV in the ambulance; start it at the hospital instead.
- **Intraosseous fluid resuscitation**: not well supported by data for efficacy in children or adults; use only in children in shock when IV cannot be placed.
- **Type of IV fluid**: does not agree with giving large amounts of crystalloid alone; give blood together with plasma at ~1:1 — whatever fluid is chosen, each has its own problems.
- **MAST (military antishock trousers)**: does not work; BP does not rise, and it adds risk of compartment syndrome, increases cardiac afterload and increases death rate.
- **Renal dose dopamine** to raise BP: do not do it.
- If arrest and CPR > 5 min → dead (previous teaching in 2003 was 10 min).
- **ERT/EDT**: Mattox prefers the term ECT (emergency center thoracotomy); if CPR > 10 min and still no response, ECT is not needed.
- **Airway maintenance**: against the esophageal obturator airway — it can tear the esophagus and cause vomiting, and studies show no survival benefit.
- **CBC, electrolytes**: of little use (as printed: "drawing blood is not useful, but whoever sends it must pay").
- **ABG**: may be helpful.
- **Cross match**: should be done.
- **Urine exam**: should be done.
- **Skull x-ray**: no benefit (does not show brain); send CT instead.
- **CT chest in acute trauma**: use only in mediastinal traverse wound; doing rapid helical scan as routine increases confusion.
- Massive bleeding from pelvic fracture: does not recommend external fixator.
- Patient with ICD who becomes worse/agitated → do not clamp the ICD; clamping can kill the patient.
- **Cardiac injury**: avoid pericardiocentesis and pericardial window.
- **Suturing the heart**: avoid pledgets — hard to find when called in at night, and pledgets can cause problems that need a second operation.
- Avoid the term "cardiac contusion"; use **blunt cardiac injury with description** of the injury instead.
- **Prophylactic antibiotic**: overused; antibiotics surely help, but give for no longer than 8 h after surgery.
