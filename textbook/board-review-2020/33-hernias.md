# Hernias 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 719-757 (PDF pages 1816-1854)

## Introduction
- Hernia (ไส้เลื่อน) is a surgical disease with a long history; hernias were already being repaired in the era of Hippocrates (1700 BC) (as printed).
- Henry Orlando Marcy (1837-1924), Eduardo Bassini (1844-1924) and Chester Mc Vay (1911-1987) — the "3 musketeers" who opened up hernia anatomy and the operations still used today.
- In the past hernia was simple and easy to teach; in the last 10-20 years it has become "hi-so" with lots of data and options; hernia doctors (who love to operate) formed hernia societies, producing odd concepts and many difficult operations pushed onto residents. Uncle Montgomery, the hernia "godfather", even said hernia should be operated by hernia specialists. For the Board, go with the flow — answer per the text, which favours mesh.
- Remember (จำไว้): in the exam follow the American standard — tension-free repair with mesh is better than tissue repair, and lap. looks better than open repair. But after passing and working where instruments and light weight mesh products are unavailable, try the senior's standard (the author's) — may suit hospitals with limited resources and budget.

## History of Hernia
- Old management of incarcerated hernia: hang the patient upside down and push the bowel in the scrotum back into the abdomen; if it does not go in, kicked out (as printed).
- Another old picture: patient lying with legs apart while the doctor uses an iron to push the bowel back in, smartly (as printed).
- **Truss**: device to cover the groin hernia opening so bowel does not slip down; still available to buy, but no longer recommended.

## Basic Principles
- **Hernia**: protrusion of organs through an abnormal opening or defect out of their normal position.
- **External hernia**: usually a palpable mass.
- **Internal hernia**: within the abdomen, e.g. into the foramen of winslow; no palpable mass, so hard to diagnose.
- **General principle** (หลักนิยมทั่วไป): all hernias should be repaired, even in very old age; but in some cases wait and watch first, giving advice and follow-up:
  1. Sliding hiatal hernia
  2. Asymptomatic paraesophageal hiatal hernia
  3. Umbilical hernia before age 4
  4. IIH and DIH in patients with ascites or in pregnancy
  5. Minimally symptomatic IIH and DIH
- **Etiology**
  1. **Congenital**: e.g. IIH in children from a processus vaginalis that does not close, letting abdominal content slip out
  2. **Acquired**: middle or old age, or raised intraabdominal pressure — constipation, chronic cough, difficult urination, ascites — which must be asked in every patient; e.g. DIH, believed to come from acquired wear and tear
- **In elderly patients with hernia** (ในคนแก่ที่เป็นไส้เลื่อน), work up:
  1. **GI**: chronic constipation — tumor or not?
  2. **Urinary system**: cause of difficult urination, especially BPH
  3. **Pulmonary system**: chronic cough, COPD — if not fixed, recurrence after hernia repair is possible

## Tissue Repair
- Principle: suture the defect together to strengthen the floor; problems are pain and recurrence from high tension.
- Tissue repair is now used less, since mesh helps reduce tension (tension free), with less pain and lower recurrence.

## Mesh
- Now and in future, a golden opportunity for mesh companies: surgeons (who like to operate) on hernia increasingly favour prosthetic repair over tissue repair — don't stand against the tide; know mesh a little.
- 2 main groups:
  1. Absorbable mesh
  2. Non absorbable mesh
- **Absorbable mesh**: biologic mesh from pig skin or human dermis via tissue engineering, and absorbable synthetic mesh such as vicryl mesh from polyglactin, which is fully absorbed in 3 months; for hernia surgery with dead tissue or infection.
- **Non absorbable mesh**: acts as an inert FB stimulating fibroblasts to cover it, making local tissue thick and strong. 3 generations:
  - 1st generation: woven metal net — abandoned
  - 2nd generation: plastic, nylon, teflon, silastic — also abandoned
  - 3rd generation: still in use, e.g. PTFE or Gore Tex, dacron, polypropylene or marlex. Now divided by weight into light weight, midweight and heavyweight mesh — the heavier, the more tensile strength.
- Try to avoid non absorbable mesh in infected areas; if mesh is needed where infection is mild, choose monofilament such as marlex — prevents bacterial entrapment and tolerates infection much better than Gore Tex.
- If the mesh site has severe infection with pus, no mesh of any kind should be used; if bowel must be covered, use absorbable mesh such as dexon or vicryl first, and repair the ventral hernia later.
- **Mesh augmentation** — 3 placements, by mesh position relative to muscle:
  1. **Onlay** or overlay: mesh in the subcutaneous layer above the anterior abdominal sheath or midline fascia
  2. **Inlay** or interlay: mesh between sheath edges at the fascial defect ("grnjvxbf" printed — [unclear]), in the preperitoneum, or inside the abdomen against the inner peritoneal surface (as printed)
  3. **Under the sheath or fascia**:
     - 3.1 **Sublay**: extra-pereitoneum (as printed) between the rectus muscle and posterior sheath
     - 3.2 **Underlay**: inside the abdomen, intra-peritoneum or IPOM — laparoscopically place mesh covering the hernia defect from inside and fix it to the edges of the hernia sac

## Anatomy
- Before appreciating hernia, residents must know the anatomy of the abdominal wall and groin.
- The author asks male residents to close their eyes and imagine the organs below the navel — pubic hair and their own groin — then put a hand into the trousers to feel the triangle and canal, all within 1% burn area; the picture becomes clear (as printed, joke continues: female residents may feel their father's testicles at home).
- Remember (จำไว้): this palpation is for study; residents should not get goosebumps, flared nostrils, sudden smiles, bloodshot eyes, and should be careful nobody sees the face (as printed).
- From abdominal wall to spermatic cord layers — remember items 3 and 5:
  1. Subcutaneous tissue → dartos muscle
  2. External oblique → external spermatic fascia
  3. Internal oblique → cremasteric muscle
  4. Transversalis fascia → internal spermatic fascia
  5. Parietal peritoneum → obliterated processus vaginalis
- Lower margin of the external oblique reflects posteriorly as the inguinal ligament.
- Vas deferens lies medial in the cord structure.
- **Linea semicircularis of Douglas**: lower edge of the posterior rectus sheath; a line 3-6 cm below the umbilicus (arcuate line / Douglas' line).
- **Linea semilunaris** or **Spigelian line**: line lateral to the rectus abdominis muscle; a hernia bulging here is a **spigelian hernia**.
- **Hesselbach's triangle boundaries** — named after German surgeon Franz K. Hesselbach (1759-1861) (as printed):
  - Medial: lateral border of rectus muscle
  - Lateral: inferior epigastric artery
  - Floor: inguinal ligament (as printed)
  - A hernia bulging through this triangle is DIH.
- **Femoral canal boundaries**: figure note — femoral sheath does not cover the femoral nerve; from medial to lateral: (*) NAV... (as printed).
  - Superomedial: iliopubic tract
  - Lateral: femoral vein
  - Inferior: Cooper ligament
  - The femoral canal is rigid, so a hernia entering it often gets stuck — bowel easily dies.
- **Fruchaund's concept** (as printed): groin hernia results from failure of the transversalis fascia to cover the peritoneum below completely; this area is called the **myopectineal orifice of Fruchaund (MPO)** — the abdominal wall at the groin not covered by muscle; indirect inguinal, direct inguinal and femoral hernias all emerge through it. This theory promotes a large prosthesis placed over it = GPRVS or Giant Prosthesis Reinforcement of the Visceral Sac.
- **Inguinal canal**: between the internal ring laterally and the external ring above the pubic bone. IIH enters the internal ring (deep inguinal ring), about 2 cm above the midpoint between ASIS and pubic tubercle, slides down along the canal and out through the external ring (superficial inguinal ring), then into the scrotum in men or the vulva in women.
- **Inguinal ligament**: in the Dent era we called it Poupart's ligament, honouring French surgeon Francois Poupart (1616-1708) (as printed); part of the external oblique aponeurosis, between the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and pubic tubercle.
- **Iliopectineal ligament**: in the Dent era called Cooper's ligament, honouring English surgeon Sir Astley Paston Cooper (1768-1841); part of the lacunar ligament fanning out at the pubic tubercle. Greenfield calls Cooper's ligament the pectineal ligament.
- Names of people are not examined — the author writes them to honour past masters.
- **Iliopubic tract**: from the transversalis fascia, from ASIS to the pubis, lying below the inguinal ligament.
- **Groin nerves**
  1. Iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerve: from T12-L1; sensory to the groin skin and scrotum, including the inner upper thigh.
  2. Genital branch of genitofemoral nerve: from L1-2; sensory branch to scrotal/labial skin; motor branch to the cremasteric muscle.

## Groin Hernia
- Residents should know about 4 types well:
  1. IIH
  2. DIH
  3. Femoral
  4. Sliding
- IIH + DIH was formerly called **pantaloon hernia**.
- 75% of all types of hernia is groin hernia.
- **Embryology** — the boys' "ปิ๊กาจู้" area (as printed): in fetal life the inguinal canal develops as a path for the testis, which starts retroperitoneally and travels down through the inguinal canal into the scrotal sac; as it descends it drags peritoneum anteriorly into the scrotal sac, forming the processus vaginalis, which normally closes before birth; if it stays open → IIH. In girls the ovary also descends; the unclosed processus vaginalis is called canal Nuck; the ovary does not pass the inguinal canal as in boys — only the gubernaculum does, becoming the round ligament.
- Hernia through the internal ring out along the inguinal canal = indirect inguinal hernia.
- Hernia bulging through Hesselbach trinngle (as printed) of the abdominal wall = direct inguinal hernia.
- **Groin hernia facts used to tease medical students**
  1. IIH is most common; inguinal hernia : femoral hernia = 5 : 1
  2. M : F = 7 : 1; peaks in males before age 1 and from 40 years up
  3. In females too, IIH is the most common type
  4. Femoral hernia is more common in women than men
  5. DIH is very rare in women — in a choice, mark it wrong first
  6. Men > 75 years: inguinal hernia about 50%
  7. IIH: risk of incarceration 0.25-0.33% per year; lifetime strangulation 4-6%
  8. IIH right > left:
     - 8.1 Right testis descends into the scrotum later than the left
     - 8.2 On the left the sigmoid colon blocks content slipping down into the sac
- When can patients return to work after hernia surgery? Textbook answer: unknown — rest depends on repair technique:
  1. Open: rest about 6-8 wk.
  2. Tension free or prosthesis: about 1-3 wk.
  3. Lap.: about 1 wk.
- Many try to classify hernias, each claiming theirs is best — the author finds none useful, just a waste of memory; Schwartz edition 11 shows the Nyhus classification system 1993 for show, then never mentions it again.

- **Diagnosis**: history and examination are main; imaging is an adjunct when unsure.
  1. US: movement of content through the inguinal canal; sensitivity 86%, specificity 77%
  2. CT: similar to US; sensitivity 80%, specificity 65% (oddly, worse than US)
  3. MRI: sensitivity 95%, specificity 96% (best, but slow and very expensive)
- **Management**
  Figure: Groin hernia management algorithm, as steps —
  1. Clinical presentation → pain/discomfort, or groin mass.
  2. Pain/discomfort: palpable groin mass → surgical repair; no palpable mass → radiology (CT/US/MRI) → evidence of hernia → surgical repair; no evidence of hernia → conservative management.
  3. Groin mass: symptomatic → surgical repair; asymptomatic → discuss conservative management with surgeon → if it becomes symptomatic → surgical repair.
  4. Surgical repair: incarcerated or strangulated → open; unilateral → open vs laparoscopic; bilateral → laparoscopic; recurrence → approach hernia through virgin plane.
  1. **Observe**: for asymptomatic or very minimally symptomatic patients. A classic RCT following patients 4-5 years found incarceration in only 2/364 (0.5%) and no strangulation. With watchful waiting, explain the incarceration risk of about 0.4% per year; if pain develops, operate. Truss or elastic belt should not be used. Caution (ข้อควรระวัง): watchful waiting is forbidden for femoral hernia.
  2. **Surgical repair**: best, to prevent serious problems — incarceration and strangulation.
     - 2.1 Open repair
     - 2.2 Laparoscopic repair
  3. **Informed consent** — discuss before surgery:
     - 3.1 Recurrence after surgery, lap or open
     - 3.2 About 4-10%; in men chance of orchidectomy 1.4%; chance of chronic groin pain within 5 years (as printed)
     - 3.3 About 10%; chance of wound infection 1.5% (as printed)
     - PO. SSI is low — wound class 1 — so prophylactic antibiotic is not needed.

## Groin Hernia Repair
- 2 approaches: open repair and laparoscopic repair.
- Current trend favours tension free repair with mesh over tissue repair: less tension, less pain, lower recurrence.
- **Open repair**
  1. **Herniotomy**: cut the sac with high ligation of the sac only; suits children's hernias, whose floor muscles are still strong, so the posterior wall of the inguinal canal need not be sutured; e.g. Marcy repair.
  2. **Herniorrhaphy**: operation without mesh — narrowing the internal ring plus repairing the posterior wall of the inguinal floor from the internal ring to the pubic tubercle = tissue repair, e.g. Bassini, Mc Vay, Shouldice repair; the big hernia societies favour Shouldice most.
     - 2.1 **Bassini repair** (figure caption): (A) transversalis fascia opened from the internal inguinal ring to the pubic tubercle, exposing preperitoneal fat; (B) posterior wall reconstructed by suturing the transversalis fascia (TF), transversus abdominis muscle (TA) and internal oblique muscle (IO) — Bassini's famous "triple layer" — medially to the inguinal ligament (IL) laterally.
     - 2.2 **Mc Vay repair**: (figure) suture to Cooper's ligament.
  3. **Hernioplasty**: mesh used to reinforce the floor.
     - 3.1 Anterior floor reinforcement, e.g. Lichtenstein — the method most favoured by experts
     - 3.2 Posterior reinforcement behind the abdominal wall muscles and pelvis, e.g. Kugel and Stoppa-Rives repair
- **Laparoscopic repair** — experts favour TEP and TAPP:
  1. **Totally extraperitoneal repair (TEP)**: repair outside the abdomen — scope into the space between the back of the abdominal wall muscle layer and the peritoneum, expanded with a balloon, to place mesh. The most favoured method since the abdomen is not entered — fewer complications — but TEP needs a learning curve of 50-100 cases, so it is called "old-school skill" (เก๋าเกม).
  2. **Transabdominal preperitoneal repair (TAPP)**: port into the abdomen, open the peritoneum over the hernia, pull sac and content back, then repair with mesh outside the abdomen like TEP, close the peritoneum, remove the scope. Since TAPP enters the abdomen, there is risk of intraabdominal organ injury.
  3. **Intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM)**: enter the abdomen without pulling up the sac; push mesh into the sac to plug the hole; less favoured, since intra-abdominal mesh causes adhesions.
- Hernia doctors (who love to operate) got overexcited, playing with testicles and inventing many techniques — remembering them all would be abnormal. The author advises remembering only methods you have seen, assisted in or done, and are familiar with.
- Now lap. with small ports is popular, but lap. should not be used in acutely incarcerated hernia, or IIH in infants and young children, where simple high ligation is easy without a scope.
- **Lap. vs open** (Cameron 9th ed P. 568)
  - Lap. is better in:
    1. Faster recovery, less pain, shorter hospital stay
    2. Less long-term groin pain than open (9 : 18%)
    3. Suits recurrent cases or bilateral groin hernia
    4. Faster return to work
  - Disadvantages of Lap (ข้อเสียของ Lap):
    1. Long, boring operation — the operator enjoys it alone, the assistant is bored (as printed)
    2. More vessel injury
    3. Complications of lap such as respiratory and cardiac; more testicular pain than open
    4. Needs general anesthesia — riskier than block
- Lap recurrence is lower than open tissue repair, but equal to open repair with mesh.
- During lap, know the important areas where structures may be injured (figure from Schwartz 11th P. 1602):
  1. **Triangle of doom**: beware injury to external iliac artery and vein
  2. **Triangle of pain**: beware injury to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve
  3. **Circle of death** — formed by several vessels:
     - 3.1 Common iliac
     - 3.2 Internal iliac
     - 3.3 External iliac
     - 3.4 Obturator and aberrant obturator
     - 3.5 Inferior epigastric
- Robot-assisted inguinal hernia repair, promoted in the new Schwartz: seems suited to recurrent hernia like lap repair, but not yet popular because of cost and long operating time.
- **Relaxing incision**: reduces repair tension, so postoperative recurrence is lower.
  - **Technique**: divide (as printed "devide") the anterior rectus sheath from the pubic tubercle upward, curving laterally.
  - Suits tight open repairs such as Cooper ligament repair or Mc Vay, where it must be done every time; the author also uses it with tight Bassini, greatly reducing tension.

## Postoperative Complications of Open Repair
- **Urinary retention**: most common in early PO.; from anesthesia or spinal block.
- **Wound infection**: SSI risk is similar whether antibiotic is given and whatever the anesthesia — local, spinal or general. The debate is about prophylactic antibiotics: many studies say not needed. If SSI occurs → open drainage, local wound care with oral antibiotic; mesh must be removed if there is deep infection with pus draining as a sinus tract.
- **Seroma or hematoma**: Schwartz edition 11 — if no pain/tightness or activity limitation, advise and support only, since seroma can be absorbed; aspirate only if symptomatic. Many others advise aspiration because of infection risk (in theory no need to aspirate seroma, but in practice the author aspirates).
  - Try aspiration twice; if seroma recurs, place a drain; the author thinks Schwartz should be followed. Non-resolving hematoma → open and evacuate the clot.
- **Acute ischemic orchitis**: usually from trying to dissect out the distal sac, damaging the pampiniform venous plexus. Early: testis swollen and red from venous congestion; may resolve spontaneously → symptomatic treatment. Then at 6-12 wk., in some patients the testis atrophies; each time the patient feels his own testis, now a small piece like a shrivelled mung bean, he will curse the surgeon to death (as printed).
- Also injury to cord, testis, bladder, and mesh complications.
- Chronic groin pain from neuropathic pain is written about in more detail below.

## Chronic Groin Pain
- Pain lasting > 3 months after surgery; many causes: recurrent hernia, mesh complication, infection and nerve injury; pain from osteitis pubis is uncommon.
- Pain from recurrent hernia: usually with activity, eases on lying flat.
- Pain from mesh: vague dull pain on hip flexion, sitting, swimming.
- Pain from infection: groin pain with wound drainage and local inflammatory signs, fever, night sweat, fatigue, raised white count and C-reactive protein.
- Pain from nerve injury — from traction, entrapment, electrocartery (as printed), or cut nerve → neuropathic pain; tapping at ASIS gives a sudden pain like an electric shock.
- **Ilioinguinal nerve injury**: most common, as the nerve lies anteriorly, close to the external oblique, so it is often cut at the external ring. A cut nerve → loss of cremasteric reflex, numbness of the penile skin and that side of the scrotum, including a little of the inner thigh; the patient may want to kick the surgeon for leaving his "gun" numb (as printed).
- **Iliohypogastric nerve injury**: next most common; lies more medially, so injured during relaxing incision.
- **Genitofemoral nerve** — from L1-2:
  - Motor: supplies cremasteric muscle
  - Sensory: runs through the internal ring alongside the external spermatic vessels (remember: not inside the spermatic cord); sensory supply to scrotum and labia; nerve injury is usually with Shouldice repair.
- **Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury**: in lap. repair; injury causes numbness of the lateral thigh (meralgia parestica, as printed); luckily usually transient paresthesia that improves after a while.
- **Approach to chronic groin pain**
  - First rule out occult recurrent hernia by examination, possibly with US or MRI. Treatment always starts with conservative treatment: analgesics, NSAID, local nerve block, treat depression, reassurance; most improve within 2-3 weeks.
  - Usually allow conservative care about 1 year; if not better, explore the groin — very hard with many adhesions; with luck a neuroma is found → neurectomy. Warn the patient that the operative result may not be very good.
- Groin pain may be caused by mesh: mesh-related pain is not produced by percussion as neuropathic pain is. Start conservative; if pain persists, remove the mesh and convert to tissue repair.
- Mesh fixation by suture, stapling or tacking prosthesis — no difference in pain. Fibrin glue for mesh fixation reduces groin pain; long-term results and strength in large hernias are awaited; the drawback with glue is that it is very expensive.

## Incarcerated Inguinal Hernia
- The most serious complication.
- The hernia most often incarcerated is IIH.
- But the most serious incarcerated groin hernia is femoral hernia — hard to diagnose and with a narrow neck, so bowel slipping in easily dies.
- Highest incidence of incarcerated IIH: first few months of life and younger patients.
- **Incarcerated hernia**: abdominal organs slip down but can still be pushed back by hand, or the content returns by itself, with bowel not yet dead.
  1. Infants: 90% reducible
  2. Adults: about 4.5% become incarcerated within 2 years, mostly in the first 3 months after diagnosis
- **Obstructed hernia**: abdominal organs slip down and cannot be pushed back.
- **Strangulation**: usually at the neck → high venous pressure → venous infarction and finally dead bowel.
- **Findings suggesting strangulation**
  1. **Local signs**: swollen red or dusky skin, very painful
  2. **Abdominal findings**: continuous abdominal pain, diffuse tenderness, signs of peritonitis; film abdomen shows SBO or free air
  3. **Systemic signs** of sepsis: high fever, rapid HR, BP drop; CBC leucocytosis; ABG acidosis
- **Management**
  1. **No signs of strangulation** → start with gentle manual reduction first, with IV. sedation. In children raise the legs; ice packs not recommended. In adults lie flat, head slightly down, flex the thigh without abducting, then gently push the hernia back = **taxis**.
     - Another way (figure): **postural reduction** — lie flat head-down (trendelenburg) without pressing on the scrotum, letting the hernia slip back gradually within 1-2 hours.
     - Figure: Taxis technique — (A) pressing on the dome alone makes the sac mushroom over the neck; (B) one hand holds and elongates the neck while the other presses the dome, guiding content back through the neck.
     - After content returns, do not discharge — recurrence is high; operate within 48 hours. Laparoscopic approach is now found to be safe.
  2. **Signs of strangulation present** — usually > 6 hours: in practice no reduction into the abdomen, except reduction of sac and bowel together, still strangled by the neck of the sac, pushed back into the abdomen = **Reduction en masse**. Normally successful reduction improves symptoms, but with reduction en masse symptoms stay bad → urgent open operation.
- **Procedure**
  1. Pre op.: correct DM, heart disease; NG, Foley, IV antibiotic.
  2. Rush to OR.
  3. Anesthesia: GA preferred; spinal or epidural acceptable; if strangulation is suspected, no local.
  4. Incision: standard transverse inguinal incision.
  5. On reaching the hernia, open the sac and examine content, especially bowel loops proximal and distal to the obstruction, before pushing back. The constriction ring is often at the external ring → open the external oblique toward the pubic bone; if at the internal ring, cut the internal oblique at 12 o'clock to avoid the inferior epigastric vessels medially and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve and spermatic vessels laterally.
     - 5.1 Bowel viable — nice pink, with peristalsis → push back into the abdomen, dilating or cutting the neck if needed, then mesh repair as usual.
     - 5.2 Obvious gangrene — black, dead, foul smell → bowel must be resected; extend the incision upward like a Grid iron resection, or open a lower midline; resect dead bowel and anastomose.
     - 5.3 Questionable viability → wrap bowel in warm moist pads about 10 minutes; if colour returns and it is not dead, as 5.1; if dead, as 5.2.
  6. Bowel anastomosis at the groin incision if possible; if difficult, open a new low transverse transrectus incision on the right side; if the dead part is colon, open midline.
  7. Remember (จำไว้): even with strangulation, hernia repair at the groin is possible, but no mesh.
  8. Redivac drain 24-48 hours to reduce seroma and wound infection.
  9. If unlucky — during abdominal prep., or during surgery before opening the sac, the incarcerated content slips back into the abdomen — principle: find a way to assess all of the bowel.
  - Try to pull it out through the groin first; if not possible or unsure, 2 options: explore lap. or laparoscope. Schwartz 10th favours explore lap. more; the author leans to Schwartz — open the abdomen, otherwise you won't sleep.

## Femoral Hernia
- Hernia whose content passes through the femoral canal below the inguinal ligament, whereas IIH is above the inguinal ligament; 2-8% of all groin hernias.
- The canal is rigid and narrow; trapped bowel strangulates more often than other groin hernias.
- F > M; age 40-70 years; rare in children; some persist > 1 year without symptoms; about 30-50% present with incarceration.
- Beware in women with a tender groin mass medial to the femoral pulse who look sick, nauseated, vomiting, distended — always think of incarcerated femoral hernia; do not send home thinking it is lymphadenitis.
- **Clinical features: femoral vs inguinal hernia**

| | Femoral | Inguinal |
|---|---|---|
| Sex | Female | Male |
| Age | Elderly > 65 years | Younger — all ages |
| Children | Rare | Common |
| Males | 1 | 20 |
| Females | 1 | 5 |
| Cough impulse | Often absent | Usually present |
| Reducibility | Usually not | Often is |
| Shape | Globular | Pear shaped |
| Elective presentation (of groin hernia) in male | 1% | — |
| Elective presentation (of groin hernia) in female | 20% | — |
| Present | Persistent | Intermittent |
| Strangulation @ 3 mths | 22% | 3% |
| Strangulation @ 21 mths | 45% | 4.5% |

- If examination is uncertain, use US or CT to help diagnose.
- **Differential diagnosis**
  1. Inguinal lymphadenitis
  2. IIH or DIH
  3. Lipoma
  4. Saphenous varix
  5. Ectopic testis
- **Treatment**: once diagnosed it must be treated — risk of incarceration or Richter hernia; bowel dies quickly, possibly fatal.
  1. **Open approach** — 3 operations:
     - 1.1 **Low operation (Lockwood)**: infrainguinal approach via transverse incision; reduce the sac upward, then suture the inguinal ligament to the iliopectineal ligament to close the canal (as printed "inguinal canal"); young women like it — nice scar. (Figure caption: femoral approach to repair a right femoral hernia with suture — incision; dissection of the femoral orifice and canal; nonabsorbable sutures between inguinal ligament, fascia lata and pectineal fascia; completed repair.)
     - 1.2 **Transinguinal approach (Lotheissen)**: transverse incision above the inguinal ligament; reduce the sac, then Mc.Vay or Cooper ligament repair, with a transitional stitch to close the femoral canal; the repair is very tight, so a relaxing incision is needed.
     - 1.3 **High operation (Mc. Evedy)**: open retroperitoneal approach — vertical incision from above the inguinal ligament straight down below the femoral triangle (figure: incision through linea semilunaris), then repair as Lotheissen.
  2. **Laparoscopic approach**: hernia surgeons (who love to operate) strongly favour poking holes and peeking with a scope; besides lower recurrence than open repair, 37% of patients not known preoperatively to have femoral hernia are found incidentally to have one, which can be repaired at once — so lap. reduces complications a lot, and post op. lap has less pain than open.
     - Port the scope extraperitoneally or transabdominally, then mesh as a plug into the femoral canal. Remember a little: if strangulation is suspected, lap. should not be used.
- **Incarcerated femoral hernia**
  1. Recommended incision: transverse incision above the typical herniorrhaphy incision.
  2. In women the round ligament can be cut — no need to preserve it.
  3. Divide the inguinal ligament without needing to repair it later; find the hernia sac, push it up through the femoral canal, open the sac and check the organ; if bowel is not dead, push it back and repair the hernia. If bowel is not dead, mesh closing the myopectineal orifice is acceptable (as printed, "mother won't scold").
  4. If bowel is dead, enlarge the wound to control both proximal and distal ends of the involved bowel.
  5. Check viability; if not dead, return it to the abdomen and do Cooper ligament repair, or what we like to call Mc Vay.
  6. If bowel is dead, resect and anastomose right at the groin; in this case do not use mesh.
  7. Always do a relaxing incision.
- **What not to do** in incarcerated femoral hernia (สิ่งที่ไม่ควรทำ):
  1. Reduction — pushing dead bowel back into the abdomen is disastrous
  2. Lap. repair by TEP or TAPP — insufflated air loosens the femoral canal opening and may let the trapped bowel slip into the abdomen
  3. Repair by Marcy, Bassini, Lichtenstein, Shouldice

## Sliding Hernia
- Suspect in men > 70 years with a large groin hernia stuck around the internal ring and very hard to reduce.
- In sliding hernia, part of a visceral organ slides into the hernia sac — usually a retroperitoneal structure forming the posterior wall of the sac (not anterior): on the right usually cecum, on the left usually sigmoid colon; urinary bladder occasionally; in women tube or ovary more often than other organs.
- Small bowel and omentum are never the wall of a sliding hernia.
- **Treatment**: incision as for IIH or DIH, then simple reduction of that retroperitoneal structure above the internal ring is enough; take care during surgery not to perforate the colon.

## Groin Hernia in Infant and Children
- **Incidence**: common in premature infants; almost all IIH, from failed obliteration of the processus vaginalis — not from floor muscle weakness as in adults.
- **Diagnosis**: mother's history of a mass going in and out at the groin or beside the pubis; examination may feel the sac at the pubis; rolling it under the finger feels like silk = silk sign.
- After birth, about 6 months old, especially small infants coming to ER with an acute non-reducible mass in the right groin → beware bowel or gonad incarceration; the younger, the scarier; examination is hard — the baby just screams, abdomen distended, vomiting, can't tell what's going on.
- Young children with IIH have incarceration risk up to 20%, so all should be operated unless medically contraindicated.
- **Possible exam points**
  1. IIH in children is bilateral in 10-30%. Routine bilateral groin exploration was advised in the old days; now it is outdated. When bilateral is suspected, pediatric hernia surgeons advise US — may show a widened internal ring — or, for the more eager, laparoscopy.
  2. **Gonad found in the sac** — in boys usually testis, in girls ovary. Approach:
     - 2.1 Gonad looks abnormal → biopsy before reducing it back into the abdomen
     - 2.2 Boys: biopsy the testis in every case
     - 2.3 Girls: if only the ovary, biopsy the ovary first; if both tube and ovary, no biopsy — just push back into the abdomen
  - Why this is done — beyond standard knowledge; if curious, see Greenfield and Rush 6th ed.
- Repair is just Marcy repair: excise the sac with high ligation, then narrow the internal ring a little; Marcy is probably the simplest procedure suitable for children's hernia.

## Recurrent Groin Hernia
- Recurrence after hernia repair is generally about 5%. Since mesh-based modern tension free repair, recurrence fell dramatically, and mesh companies' shares rose higher than PTT and telecoms, becoming blue-chip stocks (as printed).
- **Risk higher in**
  1. PO repair of DIH > femoral > IIH
  2. Bilateral repair > unilateral repair
  3. Multiple operations
  4. Increased intraabdominal pressure — COPD, BPH, constipation, ascites, anorectal disease
- **Treatment**
  1. Re-repair through a virgin space not yet operated, depending on the original procedure:
     - 1.1 Previous open anterior repair → redo by lap. repair
     - 1.2 Previous lap. repair → redo by open anterior repair
  2. Mesh and relaxing incision reduce tension well.
  3. Generally laparoscopic approach is the best operation for recurrent or bilateral hernia.
- **Giant groin hernia**: instead of a good mood from a giant size, it brings misery, because big here means a hernia (as printed joke). The problem: on opening the sac and pushing bowel back into the abdomen, the bowel compresses the diaphragm, causing breathing difficulty.
  - In the past pre op. pneumoperitoneum was done — puncturing the abdomen and insufflating air to enlarge the peritoneal cavity; instead the scrotum enlarged, because the air passed through the hernia defect into the scrotal sac; now abandoned.
  - **Technique of repair for giant hernias**
    1. Relaxing midline incision
    2. Amputation of the scrotal sac
    3. If needed, resect some bowel
    4. Repair: choose among Mc Vay, Shouldice or GPRVS
- **Truss** (pressing pants over the hernia defect to keep content in): now considered useless and even squeezes both testes; except for some kinky types who enjoy it — counted as one way to release (as printed).

## Umbilical Hernia
- Hernia bulging straight out at the umbilicus; 10% of all hernias.
- **Congenital**: from birth; patent umbilical ring that fails to close, leaving a hole at the navel; about 10% of newborns; more common in premature and dark-skinned infants; incarceration is very rare (จำ).
- **Acquired**: common with high intraabdominal pressure, e.g. heavy ascites; acquired type can incarcerate, or the sac can tear and bowel spill out.
- **Treatment**
  1. Principle first — in children (congenital): as the child grows, the defect is reinforced by attachment of the ligamentum teres, median umbilical ligament and umbilical fascia, so spontaneous closure occurs within 2 years, not beyond 5 years — so wait comfortably. Features favouring easy closure: defect diameter < 2 cm with thick sharp edge; if diameter > 2 cm with thin edge (thin sharp edge, as printed), spontaneous closure is hard — no need to wait until 5 years; some wait only 3 years; watch a while, and if size is unchanged, consider early surgery.
  2. **Adult umbilical hernia**: if the patient is fit, repair is better than observation — but it is not life-or-death urgent; when patient and surgeon are ready, schedule surgery. If not operating, observe only truly selected cases — small and asymptomatic.
  3. **Procedure**: formerly sheath sutured in 2 overlapping layers = Mayo technique; actually Mayo is no better than simple repair — just fancier, with recurrence 25%. Now mesh repair is recommended — over the anterior rectus sheath (onlay) or in the preperitoneal space (sublay); mesh is recommended because there is less tension, especially when the defect is > 3cm. Hernia surgeons (who love to operate) push mesh because recurrence is up to 10 times lower than without.

## Hernia with Ascites
- **Umbilical hernia with ascites**
  1. Associated with **advanced liver disease**. Treatment (การรักษา):
     - 1st line: aggressive medical treatment — low salt with diuretic (read the HB liver chapter on ascites).
     - 2nd line: refractory → choose among TIPS, shunt, OLT; once ascites is controlled, repair the hernia. Le Veen shunt does not work.
  2. **Complicated situations**
     - 2.1 **Incarceration or strangulation**: cannot wait — immediate repair.
     - 2.2 **Rupture of sac**: as 2.1 — bowel out, cannot wait, operate at once.
     - 2.3 **Fluid leak at apex**: must remember, often examined — bed rest, IV and IV antibiotic, then repair during that admission; can wait a little, not as urgent as 2.1 and 2.2.
  - Note (หมายเหตุ): in 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3, after repair, continue to treat the ascites.
- **Groin hernia with ascites**
  - Femoral hernia should be repaired in every case — risk of incarceration.
  - IIH and DIH rarely incarcerate, and in men the scrotal skin is thick, so sac rupture is very rare — if asymptomatic, leave alone and just follow up.

## Epigastric Hernia
- Midline hernia between xyphoid (as printed) and umbilicus through the linea alba.
- M : F = 2 : 1.
- Content mostly preperitoneal fat.
- Usually small masses, possibly several.
- Some have subxyphoid pain mistaken for peptic ulcer.
- **Treatment**: repair as for umbilical hernia.

## Spigelian Hernia
- Hernia through a small defect below the semilunar line at the lateral edge of the rectus abdominis muscle, about 4-5 cm below the umbilicus.
- Some call the semilunar line the arcuate line (as printed) — the line here is both widest and weakest.
- Like femoral hernia, F > M: F : M = 1.5 : 1, age 50-60 years.
- Small, narrow neck; the hernia penetrates the transversus abdominis and internal oblique (intramural) and lies behind the external oblique, so the mass cannot be palpated and diagnosis is hard; incarceration is common. A hernia wedged between muscle layers like this is called an **interparietal hernia**.
- Patients may complain of localized pain or straining during exercise for years.
- Omentum or preperitoneal fat herniates behind the abdominal muscle.
- This hernia cannot be diagnosed by examination; if suspected, US or CT to confirm diagnosis.
- **Treatment**: overall open and lap. repair give similar results, but laparoscopic repair is now preferred; small hernias — scope, suture the defect; large ones — mesh to reduce tension.

## Ventral Incisional Hernia
- Hernia at an old operative wound; number 1 is the C/S wound.
- Most common in the 1st postoperative year; about 150,000 cases per year.
- **Risk factors**
  1. Wound infection — the most commonly associated factor
  2. Malnutrition
  3. Obesity
  4. Steroid or immunosuppressive drugs
  5. Chronic increased intraabdominal pressure
  5. (as printed) Vertical incision below the umbilicus
  7. Repeated operations, long operations, or large defects
- Incisional hernia has the highest recurrence after repair: 12% in 5 years, 20% in 10 years.
- Remember: women have more recurrence after surgery than men.
- Comparing continuous vs interrupted midline abdominal closure: risk of ventral hernia is no different; but fast-absorbing sutures carry higher ventral hernia risk than slow-absorbing sutures; bites should not be more than 1 cm from the wound edge.
- **Diagnosis** — remember: based on physical examination, not investigation; straining or coughing makes a bulge appear.
- **Technique**: open or lap.; laparoscopic mesh repair is now considered the gold standard.
- **Timing**: if able to wait, wait 6-12 months for maximum tensile strength.
- **Open repair**
  1. **Direct suture repair**: suturing alone is no longer OK — without mesh, recurrence at 3 years is 44%. For suture methods see Maingot 1980-1990:
     - 1.1 **Modified Mayo**: overlapping suture of the fascial edges
     - 1.2 **Keel**: relaxing incision at the lateral aspect of the anterior rectus sheath to reduce tension at the midline suture line
     - 1.3 **Nuttall**: transposition of the rectus muscle to strengthen the wound
  2. **Mesh**: now 85% are repaired with mesh, but mesh increases SSI risk.
- **Laparoscopic mesh repair**: strongly favoured; now considered the best operation — even wide defects can take a sublay mesh, with less SSI from mesh infection than open.
- **Contraindications for laparoscopic ventral hernia repair**
  1. Densely scarred abdomen
  2. Extremely large hernia — so big there is no space for a trocar
  3. Children
  4. Pregnancy
  5. Portal hypertension
  6. General contraindications to lap., e.g. severe COPD
  7. Strangulated bowel

## Pelvic Hernia
- Through the obturator foramen, anterior part of the pelvic bone = **obturator hernia**.
- Through the greater sciatic foramen, posterior part of the pelvic bone = **sciatic hernia**.
- Through a pelvic floor defect from surgery (iatrogenic), e.g. after APR or pelvic exenteration with PO. wound infection = **perineal hernia**.

## Obturator Hernia
- Hernia into the obturator canal, through which the obturator nerve and vessels run out of the pelvis to supply the thigh.
- The hernia compresses the obturator nerve → pain shooting down the medial thigh to the knee; worse with adduction + internal (medial) rotation of the thigh with the hip flexed = obturator nerve compression sign called **Howship-Romberg sign**, +ve in about 60% of obturator hernia.
- Obturator hernia: women > men, F : M = 5 : 1; about 90% present with SBO.
- **Classic presentation**: elderly thin female with SBO and no previous abdominal surgery; examination shows +ve Howship-Romberg sign.
- **PR**: tender mass palpable in 19%.
- **Diagnosis**: hard before surgery; suspect if plain film abdomen shows SBO with a gas shadow in the obturator foramenn (as printed) or below the superior pubic ramus; whether CT confirmation is necessary is still debated.
- **Treatment**
  1. Open explore lap. remains standard treatment + reduce the hernia, ligate the sac, then close the defect with local tissue repair or mesh, sutured interrupted to the pectineal ligament and the fascia above the pubic symphysis.
  2. When suturing, beware the inferior margin — the vessel may be hit.
  3. Don't forget to look at the other obturator canal too — bilateral in 50%.
  4. Laparoscopic repair is gaining popularity: port in, close the defect with synthetic plugs or sheets (IPOM or TAPP).

## Richter Hernia
- Hernia in which only part of the bowel wall is caught in the sac → that part of the wall becomes necrotic and perforates.
- Most common in femoral hernia; next laparoscopic trocar hernia.
- No suggestive gut obstruction symptoms, so diagnosis is difficult.

## Flank or Lumbar Hernia
- **Primary**: spontaneous; uncommon.
- **Secondary**: more common, e.g. after kidney surgery, or trauma with disruption of flank muscles.
- **Location**: superior lumbar space = Grynfeltt's hernia; more common at the inferior lumbar space = Petit hernia.
- Being in the retroperitoneal space, it rarely involves bowel (please remember).
- **Treatment**: repair with prosthetic mesh, by lap. or open; frequent operators say lap. has the problem of postoperative chronic neuralgia from nerve entrapment by subfascial sutures.

## Internal Hernia
- Types:
  1. **Acquired internal hernia**: usually after gastrojejunostomy, Whipple, ostomy; now common after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass for weight loss, with bowel slipping into a mesenteric defect.
  2. **Congenital internal hernia**: paraduodenal hernia, foramen of Winslow hernia.

## Left Paraduodenal Hernia
- Bowel slips into the Landzert fossa — a space in front of the IMV and ascending left colic artery present before gut rotation; within 3 months this space is normally obliterated.
- Presents with SBO.
- CT: cluster of small bowel at the LUQ with mass compression effect behind the stomach and transverse colon; loops between stomach & pancreas, behind pancreas, or between transverse colon & left adrenal gland.
- Treatment (การรักษา): operate, pull out the herniated bowel, close the defect with non-absorbable suture.

## Right Paraduodenal Hernia
- Bowel slips into the Waldeyer fossa — a space in front of the SMA and hepatic flexure of the right colon present before gut rotation; obliterated within 3 months.
- Presents with SBO.
- **CT finding**: cluster of small bowel at the RUQ with mass compression effect pushing the duodenum up, and pushing the ascending and transverse mesocolon forward; loops lateral & inferior to the descending duodenum.
- Treatment (การรักษา): operate, pull out the herniated bowel, close the defect with non-absorbable suture.

## Foramen of Winslow Hernia
- Associated with a petulous (as printed) foramen, letting bowel slip in and get stuck in the lesser sac.
- Fairly rare; patients usually present with SBO.
- CT: cluster of small bowel behind the stomach, pushing the stomach forward; bowel loops between IVC and liver hilum into the lesser sac.
- **Treatment**
  1. Open explore lap.
  2. Gently reduce the bowel (as printed, "แหงละ" — of course).
  3. Whether to close the foramen is still debated: formerly not closed; now the trend favours closing the defect with interrupted sutures to the retroperitoneum, or pexy of omentum to plug the hole.

## Foramen of Morgagni Hernia
- Hernia through a defect in the triangular space of Larrey, where costal and tendinous fibres normally fuse into the central tendon of the diaphragm.
- Defect on the right 90%, left 9%; few bilateral; has a sac — unlike Bochdalek, which is usually on the left and has no sac.
- F : M = 4 : 1; mostly diagnosed in childhood; fewer in adults; mostly asymptomatic.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): from non specific to gut obstruction, especially with sudden increase in intraabdominal pressure.
- **Film chest, abdomen**: may show air fluid bowel loops above the Rt. hemidiaphragm. CT is for confirming diagnosis, but not sending CT is fine — in paper exams they seem to want plain film only.
- Thoracoscopy or bronchoscopy: unnecessary and should not be done — may perforate bowel.
- **Treatment**: because incarceration can occur, repair every case.
  1. By lap. or open
  2. Via chest or abdomen

## Special Problems
- **Hernias with odd names** — good for teasing professors:
  1. **Littre's hernia**: hernia whose content is a Meckel diverticulum stuck in it; if not inflamed, leave it; if inflamed, remove it through the incision.
  2. **Amyand hernia**: hernia with the appendix as content. Asked whether a normal appendix must be removed — answer: not necessary, unless the appendix is strangled, swollen, red, inflamed — then remove it.
  3. **Maydl's hernia**: bowel lies in the sac in a W shape; on examining bowel in the sac you may see only the viable part, while the dead part is pushed up inside the abdomen.
- **Bilateral repair**: patients with bilateral groin hernias can have both repaired together, as long as it is tension free repair; very suitable for laparoscopic repair.
- **Simultaneous prostatectomy**: hernia into the scrotum plus a large prostate — operate on both together, but treat UTI until it subsides first.
- **Groin hernia in pregnancy**: the enlarging uterus helps plug the hernia, so incarceration is very rare, except in the very unlucky; advice: expectant management, then repair after delivery.

## Conditions that May Mimic Hernia
- Residents should know about 3.
- **Diastasis recti** (rectus abdominis diastasis): separation of the rectus abdominis muscles; on valsalva — straining or hard coughing — the area bulges, mistaken for ventral hernia; usually above the umbilicus, about 2 cm; never incarcerates or strangulates.
  - In obese patients with big bellies, the elderly, and especially women with many pregnancies, CT may be needed to distinguish it from ventral hernia.
  - Treatment (การรักษา) is cosmetic; if the patient is not bothered, the doctor should not meddle.
- **Sportman hernia** (as printed): in athletes, especially fake-injury footballers who use it as an excuse not to play, but once paid more it usually disappears (as printed). Symptom: groin pain without specific surgically correctable lesion.
  - Treatment (การรักษา): rest, analgesics with NSAID, PT is enough; operating on a sportman hernia is very likely wrong; if a footballer, try raising their pay — the pain disappears in every case (as printed).
- **Lipoma of the cord**: excessive preperitoneal fatty tissue near the cord, giving symptoms and findings much like hernia. Hernia surgeons (who love to operate) believe it should be operated if possible — in future the enlarging fat mass gradually widens the internal ring and finally a true hernia develops. Leaving it alone is therefore probably not correct.
- **Giant groin hernia**: found ones so huge (as printed, "starting to get bored") — how to repair is to be written for reading in the next edition.
