## Abdominal Wall
- Boundaries: costal margin above, symphysis pubis and pelvic bone below, vertebral column behind.
- **Blood supply** of the anterior abdominal wall muscles runs posterior to the rectus muscle and sends branches into it:
  1. **Superior epigastric artery** — from the internal thoracic artery
  2. **Inferior epigastric artery** — from the external iliac artery; larger than the superior epigastric artery (จำ); if torn, may cause shock
- **Lymphatic drainage**
  1. Above the umbilicus — more precisely above the arcuate line (semilunar line of Douglas), at the level of the anterior iliac spine — drains to the axillary node basin
  2. Below the umbilicus → inguinal nodal basin
- **Nerve**
  1. Skin: afferent branches of T4-L1
  2. Muscle: motor nerves from T6-T12
  3. Umbilicus: sensation from T10
- **Rectus sheath**
  1. Anterior: complete
  2. Posterior: below the semicircularis there is no (posterior) rectus sheath

## Rectus Sheath Hematoma
- From tearing of an epigastric vessel, artery or vein — not from muscle tear (จำ).
- More common in women than men, about 3 : 1 (จำ); rare in children.
- **Etiology**
  1. Trauma — most common (จำ): from being kicked, or strong rectus contraction such as coughing, sneezing, straining at stool or urine, lifting heavy weights
  2. Typhoid
  3. Collagen disease with vasculitis
  4. Anticoagulant
  5. Hematologic disease, e.g. ITP
  6. Pregnancy or just after delivery
  7. Tumor — possible but very rare
- So there are many causes, not only trauma.
- **Clinical manifestations**
  - Typical: sudden abdominal pain on one side; if pain is at the RLQ it may be confused with appendicitis. Pain is exacerbated by movement or rectus contraction.
  - Bleeding below the arcuate line → hematoma can cross the midline to the other side → bilateral lower quadrant pain; heavy bleeding may cause shock.
- **Physical examination**
  - Skin may show ecchymosis; with a desmoid the skin is normal. Sometimes a tender mass is felt; when the patient tenses the abdominal wall by contracting the rectus and the mass is still palpable, it is in the abdominal wall = +ve Fothergill's sign.
  - If the mass disappears when the abdomen is tensed, it is inside the peritoneal cavity.
- **Diagnosis**
  1. **US**: screening; tells cystic vs solid
  2. **CT**: the most definitive diagnostic study; besides diagnosing rectus sheath hematoma it excludes other causes such as appendicitis. Schwartz recommends abdominal CT in every suspected case.
- **Management**
  1. Small hematoma and stable patient → observe as OPD case; anticoagulation can be given if needed.
  2. Large hematoma, still stable → treat as in 1 but observe as IPD case; do not discharge.
  3. Expanding hematoma, or patient not stable (BP not good, rapid pulse) → admit, volume resuscitation, transfuse; if on anticoagulation, reverse first; then reassess:
     - 3.1 Becomes stable and hematoma stops growing → continue observation
     - 3.2 Becomes stable but hematoma keeps growing → angiographic embolization
     - 3.3 Unstable → OR to stop bleeding: remove blood clot, suture or ligate the torn vessel, without entering the peritoneal cavity
- Summary: mostly conservative; surgery is rarely needed.
- Figure: Rectus sheath hematoma algorithm, as steps —
  1. Suspected rectus sheath hematoma → abdominal CT scan.
  2. Small + stable → ± anticoagulation, observe as outpatient.
  3. Large + stable → hold anticoagulation, check CBC, observe as inpatient.
  4. Unstable or expanding → reverse anticoagulation, volume resuscitation, transfuse if required; then stable → continued observation; enlarging → angiographic embolization; unstable → to OR (or OR for evacuation of hematoma).

## Omentum
- Greater and lesser omentum: support, cover and protect organs in the peritoneal cavity.
- **Blood supply**: right and left gastroepiploic A.; venous drainage follows the arteries into the portal system.

## Omental Infarction
- Mostly adults; rare in children.
- **Primary torsion**: very rare; cause unclear; some believe a redundant omentum twists.
- **Secondary torsion**: more common than primary; due to causes such as tumor or band acting as a fixed point.
- **Pain** can be anywhere; may have low-grade fever; little nausea, vomiting or intestinal symptoms, and appetite is not lost as in appendicitis.
- **Physical examination**: may feel a tender mass.
- **Diagnosis**: fairly difficult; most are diagnosed at surgery for presumed appendicitis. If unclear, US or CT may show a localized inflammatory mass of fat density, but only in some cases. The text recommends laparoscopy if truly stuck.
- **Treatment** (การรักษา)
  1. In the past, if diagnosed before surgery, observe with analgesics since it is benign and resolves in 2-3 months; few now agree with observation.
  2. Now, non-operated patients were found to develop complications such as abscess and adhesion formation → laparoscopic resection is recommended in every case.
  3. If operated for presumed appendicitis and omental infarction is found → resect the infarcted part.

## Omental Cysts
- Believed to arise from peritoneal inclusions or degeneration of lymphatic structures.
- Mostly asymptomatic, found incidentally, especially at laparotomy for other diseases; some have mild intermittent pain, or present with torsion, infarction or rupture.
- Less common than mesenteric cyst.
- On palpation an omental cyst is freely mobile in every direction, unlike a mesenteric cyst, which moves only left-right = +ve Tillaux's sign.
- **US or CT**: well circumscribed cystic mass with internal septation arising from the greater omentum.
- Surgical drainage or FNA for fluid study should not be done — if the diagnosis is wrong, e.g. a cancer, puncturing spreads tumor through the abdomen (จำ).
- **Treatment**: benign lesion; should be excised, especially if symptomatic; lap. resection is now popular. Do not unroof — recurrence is frequent.

## Omental Neoplasms
- Uncommon.
- Benign, e.g. lipoma.
- Malignant: secondary metastasis more common than primary; secondary = metastatic tumor, especially from ovarian cancer, next GI and KUB.
- Primary malignancy: occasionally, especially GIST.
- Interesting: cancer spread to the omentum normally means extensive disease and should not be operated, but ovarian cancer to the omentum is an exception — omentectomy is beneficial, especially in reducing ascites very well.

## Mesentery
- Attaches the foregut, midgut and hindgut to the posterior abdominal wall, and carries the arteries, veins, lymphatics and nerves to the gut — knowing this much is enough.

## Sclerosing Mesenteritis
- Many names: mesenteric panniculitis, mesenteric lipodystrophy, retractile mesenteritis, sclerosing lipogranulomatosis.
- Chronic inflammatory process involving the mesentery → reaction in mesenteric fat followed by fibrosis; cause unknown; very rare.
- Age > 50 years; men more than women.
- Most common presenting symptom: abdominal pain.
- Associated symptoms: low-grade fever, fatigue, chronic abdominal pain.
- Over time a mass may be palpable, from small up to 40 cm; the larger the mass, the more likely it compresses the bowel to obstruction.
- Often diagnosed incidentally on abdominal CT: mesenteric mass denser than normal mesenteric tissue, to be distinguished from metastatic cancer or lymphoma.
- Figure caption (as printed): idiopathic, though some suggest an association with certain malignancies; suggested on imaging (CT or MRI) and confirmed at laparotomy or laparoscopy; surgical resection only if it causes intestinal obstruction; patients may be asymptomatic or have fever, abdominal pain, anorexia, weight loss and altered bowel habits.
- **CT**: ill-defined mass involving the mesentery without true encasement of vessels; still hard to distinguish from tumors such as lymphoma or metastatic cancer.
- So tissue diagnosis is very important — confirm by lap. or open biopsy; histology shows fibrosis and chronic inflammation without evidence of malignancy.
- Self-limited disease; symptoms often improve by themselves → symptomatic treatment; for severe pain, steroid may speed improvement. Surgery is kept for diagnostic problems and intestinal obstruction.

## Mesenteric Cysts
- Benign tumor.
- May or may not be symptomatic; examination finds an abdominal mass.
- **Tillaux's sign +ve**: the mass moves only left-right (not up-down).
- **CT or US**: cystic lesion in the central abdomen without solid component.
- **Indications for surgery**
  1. Symptomatic
  2. Cannot be distinguished from a cystic tumor
- **Surgery** (การผ่าตัด): laparoscopic excision before open excision; very large masses may need bowel resection and anastomosis.
- Do not do cyst unroofing or marsupialization — recurrence is certain (จำ).

## Mesenteric Tumors
- Benign and primary malignancy are uncommon; among these, benign (lipoma, desmoid, lymphangioma) are more frequent; malignant is usually lymphoma.
- Most common: **metastatic cancer**, especially metastatic lymph nodes at the root of the mesentery — an enlarging mass there should first suggest metastatic cancer.

## Retroperitoneum
- Boundaries: superior — diaphragm; inferior — down to the levator ani muscle (pelvic floor muscles); posterior — spinal column and iliopsoas muscle (deep back muscles); lateral — abdominal muscles (figure label).
- The retroperitoneal space is very large, so a collection or mass is usually fairly big by the time it causes symptoms.
- Retroperitoneal masses: see the soft tissue sarcoma chapter (retroperitoneal sarcoma). Retroperitoneal infection usually comes from GI or KUB infection — not written here.

## Retroperitoneal Fibrosis
- Common in middle-aged men; disordered, excessive proliferation of fibrous tissue.
- The fibrotic process usually starts below the renal artery, then spreads to encase the ureter, IVC, aorta and duodenum.
- 67% bilateral involvement.
- Types:
  1. **Primary**: unknown cause or idiopathic = Ormond disease
  2. **Secondary**, associated with:
     - 2.1 **Drug**: methyl sergide (จำ), betablocker, thiazide, methyldopa
     - 2.2 **Inflammatory process**: SLE, ankylosing spondilitis (as printed), polyarteritis nodosa, AAA, pancreatitis, histoplasmosis, TB
     - 2.3 **Malignancy**: prostate cancer, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, retroperitoneal sarcoma, carcinoid, stomach
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): none early; later, symptoms from compression of tubular organs:
  1. Ureter — number 1 (จำ)
  2. Next IVC and duodenum
- Figure caption (as printed): slowly progressive condition of unknown etiology with fibrous tissue in the retroperitoneum compressing ureters, great vessels, bile duct and other structures; with AAA it may be called chronic periaortitis or inflammatory perianeurysmal fibrosis.
- **Investigation**
  1. ESR raised in every case
  2. Imaging: previously IVP; now CT or MRI, much better than IVP — shows ureters plus bowel and vessels; then CT-guided core biopsy to distinguish fibrosis from sarcoma
- **Management**
  1. **Rule out** cancer, autoimmune, infection or drug causes first; if a cause is known, treat it and stop the drug.
  2. **Medication**: mainstay is steroid (จำ) — prednisolone 60 gm (as printed) per day for 2 months, then taper until it can finally be stopped; when to stop depends on symptoms and ESR together with response on CT. If steroid fails, try tamoxifen or cyclosporin.
  3. **Surgical treatment** for complications:
     - 3.1 **Ureter**: moderate or severe hydronephrosis → laparoscopic ureterolysis, or stent, or both
     - 3.2 **Duodenum**: usually needs bypass
  4. **Iliocaval thrombosis** → anticoagulation; duration not yet agreed, but not less than 6 months; if due to cancer, lifelong.
