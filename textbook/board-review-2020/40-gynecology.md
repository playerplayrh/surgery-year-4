# Gynecology 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 946-961 (PDF pages 2044-2059)

## Introduction
- Gyne disease a surgery resident should know is mainly infection and tumors, especially ovarian tumors. Year-3 residents may meet about 3-5 MCQ items; Year-4 residents can skip this chapter.

## Anatomy
- **Ligament**
  1. Round ligament — keeps the uterus in anteversion
  2. Broad ligament — contains the uterine vessels
  3. Infundibular ligament — contains ovarian artery, vein and nerve
  4. Cardinal ligament — helps hold cervix and vagina
- **Ovarian artery**: branch of the aorta.
- **Ovarian vein**
  1. Lt. drains into the Lt. renal vein
  2. Rt. drains into the IVC
- **Lymphatic drainage of vagina**
  1. Upper 2/3 → common iliac and hypogastric nodes
  2. Lower 1/3 → femoral nodes in the femoral triangle

## Surgical Disease in Pregnancy
- **Appendicitis**: most common cause of acute abdomen in pregnancy; confirmed by US, or MRI if unsure. Operate urgently — rupture brings fetal loss 36% and premature labor.
- **Cholecystitis**: 2nd most common after appendicitis; operate in the second trimester.
- **Adnexal torsion**: more common in pregnancy than non-pregnant. Rx: open, detorsion and check viability first; oophorectomy only if the ovary is necrotic.
- **Graves disease in pregnancy**: best Rx is PTU; if surgery is needed, do it in the second trimester; RAI is forbidden.

## Ectopic Pregnancy
- Suspect in a woman of reproductive age with missed period, intermittent vaginal spotting, lower abdominal pain without the migratory pain of appendicitis, no fever; some have a past history of sexually transmitted disease.
- Pain is sudden, with hypovolemia from intraperitoneal bleeding; internal exam may show blood at the cervical ostium; in some, PV resembles PID — cervical motion tenderness or palpable adnexal mass.
- Risk factor: common in patients with repeated PID or previous ectopic; age is not a risk factor.
- **Investigation**
  1. Hematocrit falls, white count normal, urine preg test +ve
  2. Serum beta HCG > 1,500 mIU/ml
  3. Transvaginal US +ve 90%, transabdominal US +ve 70% — no gestational sac in the uterine cavity
  4. Laparoscopy — best diagnostic test
- **Treatment**
  1. Early case — tube not ruptured, serum beta HCG < 1000 and ectopic size < 3.5 cm → methotrexate to terminate the pregnancy; the couple can then try again.
     - Contraindications of methotrexate:
       1. Fetal heart beat present
       2. Tube ruptured and bleeding
       3. Liver, kidney or lung disease, or bleeding tendency
  2. Advanced case or bleeding → surgery (laparoscopy acceptable):
     - 2.1 Try not to remove the tube — the current era favours tube preservation
     - 2.2 Salpingostomy and suction of the conception product (2 figures)
     - 2.3 If the tube is badly damaged → salpingectomy
- So salpingectomy is no longer the treatment of choice for ectopic pregnancy.
- Figure: Salpingostomy — 2 cm incision on the anti-mesosalpingeal border over the ectopic, then aspiration and detachment of the conception product.

## Adnexal Torsion
- Common in reproductive age, especially pregnancy; patients usually have an ovarian cyst. Malignant tumors or endometrioma rarely twist because of adhesions.
- **Ovarian cyst** that twists is usually > 5 cm; Warner's study found no torsion in cysts < 5 cm.
- **Symptoms**: acute lower abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, little fever, normal menses; PV palpable adnexal mass in 25%; no vaginal discharge.
- **Lab**: usually normal; WBC may be mildly raised. If unsure, color doppler US confirms: an enlarged edematous ovary with decreased or absent doppler blood flow (ลูกศรสีแดง, red arrows).
- Doppler slide caption: absence of blood flow demonstrates ovarian torsion; diagnosis rests on ovarian enlargement (normal ovarian volume up to approximately 15 cc); other suggestive findings are multiple peripherally based follicles.
- **Treatment**
  1. Black, necrotic ovary, or not looking like a simple cyst → oophorectomy; send for pathology
  2. Ovary not black after untwisting, a cyst, young patient wanting children → cystectomy with oophoropexy to prevent recurrent torsion

## Endometriosis
- Endometrial tissue outside the uterus.
- Suspect in a sexually active woman with severe dysmenorrhea, infertility, dyspareunia, pelvic mass and tenderness at the broad ligament; about 20% are found incidentally at laparoscopy, e.g. during LC.
- Endometrial glands can also disseminate to the uterus, tube, ovary, bowel, even into the chest.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Chronic pelvic pain 80%
  2. Dysmenorrhea
  3. Infertility 20-50%
  4. Dyspareunia
  5. No fever, no vaginal discharge
- Pain does not relate to cyst size; some small cysts hurt.
- **Diagnosis**: above symptoms > 3 months, after ruling out other causes of abdominal pain.
- **Investigation**
  1. Hematocrit and white count usually normal
  2. US: ground glass echogenicity of the cyst fluid or loculated hypoechoic cyst at the adnexa
  3. Gold standard: laparoscopy — brown cyst on the ovary and supporting ligament
- **Treatment**
  1. **Asymptomatic** → expectant Mx with periodic follow-up; no Rx yet
  2. **Mild symptom** → pill + analgesic, or NSAID
  3. **Moderate symptom** → oral metroxyprogesterone (as printed) acetate 10-20 mg, or medroxyprogesterone 150 mg IM every 3 months; do not use this group > 2 years (osteoporosis)
  4. **Severe symptom** → Danazol or gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist to stop menses
  5. **Surgical treatment**
     - 5.1 Refractory, recurrent or extensive disease → best operation is TAH+BSO
     - 5.2 Conservative surgery: lysis of adhesions with ablation of endometrial implants by CO2 laser or electrical cauterization, or excise the cyst
- **Problems surgeons must face and manage**
  1. Pre-op appendicitis; at surgery normal appendix with endometriosis → appendectomy + biopsy of endometriosis; TAH+BSO only after counseling the patient
  2. Intestinal endometriosis — resolution of implant cysts on the bowel serosa causes fibrosis that constricts the bowel; even partial obstruction needs resection of the involved bowel segment. Then decide whether the uterus is still needed:
     - 2.1 Premenopause → keep uterus and ovaries
     - 2.2 Postmenopause → TAH + BSO

## Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)
- Spectrum of inflammatory disorders of the female upper genital tract: cervicitis, salpingitis, endometritis, tuboovarian abscess (TOA) and plvic (as printed) peritonitis.
- **Organism**
  1. Chlamydia trachomatis — most common
  2. N. gonorrhea — No. 2
  3. Mycoplasma
- Repeated PID narrows the tubes → higher risk of infertility and ectopic.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Fever, bilateral lower abdominal pain
  2. PV: swollen red cervix with discharge, cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, and bilateral adnexal tenderness
- **Diagnosis**: discharge gm stain and culture. Gold standard is laparoscopy, but not routine — only when diagnosis is unclear.
- **Differential diagnosis**: lower abdominal pain, e.g. appendicitis, diverticulitis, UTI, and gyne disease such as twisted ovarian cyst.
- **Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (FHC)**: complication of PID — RUQ pain, perihepatitis, PID; diagnosed clinically and confirmed with US.
- **Treatment**
  1. Broad spectrum antibiotic — obstetricians like cefotetan 2 gm IV q 12 h or cefoxin (as printed) 2 gm IV q 6 h, with oral or IV doxycycline 100 mg q 12 h; reassess at 48 h; if not better and TOA suspected → US
  2. Admit:
     - 2.1 PID in pregnancy
     - 2.2 FHC syndrome
     - 2.3 To rule out appendicitis
     - 2.4 Worsening, no response to oral antibiotic
  3. After treatment, counsel about common sequelae: chronic pelvic pain, infertility (10-15%) and risk of ectopic pregnancy

## Tuboovarian Abscess (TOA)
- Follows PID with virulent organisms or debilitated patients, e.g. HIV; usually polymicrobial aerobes mixed with anaerobes.
- **Treatment** (จำ)
  1. Full-dose IV antibiotic, adding metronidazole; reassess at 72 h
  2. Not improving, or TOA > 4-6 cm:
     - 2.1 US guided drainage or laparoscopic drainage with catheter left in
     - 2.2 Worsening → laparotomy:
       - 2.2.1 Unilateral TOA → unilateral SO
       - 2.2.2 Bilateral TOA, ruptured, or necrotic uterus → TAH+BSO, wash out, drain, close leaving skin open for delayed 1° suture

## Bartholin Abscess
- **Bartholin glands**: 2 glands at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions; secrete mucus to moisten the vaginal mucosa.
- Duct obstruction → cyst or inflammation of the gland: fever, swollen red painful labia minora with tender mass; usually polymicrobial and sexually transmitted organisms.
- **Treatment**
  1. First step: abscess → I & D + ATBs
  2. Chronic or recurrent abscess, or Bartholin cyst even at first presentation → marsupialization: open the cyst and suture the wall edges to the wound edge, leaving the cavity open to heal (see figure)

## Genital Ulcer
- Surgery residents need only 3 of these — keep them in mind (as printed, a joke); LGV and Donovanosis need not be known.

| Disease | Incubation period | % of STI-related GUD | Spontaneous healing time | On treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary syphilis | 9-90 days (avg 3 weeks) | > 1% | 3-8 weeks; never exceeds 3m | 1-2 weeks |
| Chancroid | 1-14 days | < 1% | Self limiting but may persist for yrs | 1-2 weeks |
| Herpes genitalis | 5-21 days | 80-95% | 14-21 days | 6-12 days |
| LGV | 3-30 days | < 1% | 2-5 days | - |
| Donovanosis | 1-180 days | < 1% | No tendency for healing | 3 weeks |

1. Genital herpes simplex: small shallow ulcers with clustered vesicles, very painful. Rx: acyclovir 400 mg three times daily for 7-10 days.
2. Chancroid: shallow, very painful ulcer, often with inguinal lymphadenitis (ฝีมะม่วง). Rx: ceftriazone (as printed) 250 mg IM single dose or azithromycin 1 gm orally single dose.
3. Primary syphilis: raised-edge ulcer, not very painful, groin nodes not enlarged, VDRL +ve. Rx: benzathin penicillin G 2.4 million units IM once.

## Uterine Leiomyoma (Fibroid)
- Most common benign tumor; growth of uterine smooth muscle cells (myometrium).
- Hormone sensitive tumor, so not seen in girls before menarche.
- After menopause the mass may shrink from degeneration and calcification.
- Classified by location: intramural, submucosal, subserosal (figure).
- Mostly asymptomatic; malignant degeneration of fibroid is very rare (< 5%).
- **Diagnosis**: dilation & curettage and US.
- The commonest reason for surgery is menorrhagia to the point of pallor; size and malignant risk are not indications for surgery.
- **Treatment**: no bleeding → nothing yet, follow up in 3 months; heavy bleeding or postmenopausal → TAH.

## Uterine Adenomyosis
- Endometrial tissue within the myometrium; exam finds irregular firm nodules on the uterine surface.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Abnormal uterine bleeding
  2. Dysmenorrhea
- **Treatment**: TAH.

## Endometrial Cancer
- **Risk factor**
  1. Nulliparity or late menopause
  2. Obesity, DM, HT
  3. Tamoxifen, unopposed estrogen
  4. Complex hyperplasia with atypia
  5. HNPCC
- Smoking is not a risk factor.
- **Histologic subtype**
  1. Endometrioid adenocarcinoma — most common
  2. Next: papillary, serous, squamous, clear cell, mucinous
- **Symptoms**: always think of it in postmenopausal women with vaginal bleeding.
- **Workup**
  1. Initial workup: transvaginal US and endometrial sampling biopsy; one best answer: dilation & curettage
  2. Hysteroscopy
  3. Suspected HNPCC → add mammogram (as printed)
- Single most important prognostic indicator: staging.
- Prognosis is much better than ovarian cancer, since vaginal bleeding brings patients in early.
- **Treatment**
  1. One best: surgery — TAH+BSO + pelvic LND + omentectomy and pelvic washing
  2. RT for high surgical risk patients

## Cervical Cancer
- **Risk factor**
  1. HPV — strongest risk factor, HPV 16 and 18
  2. Multiple sexual partners
  3. Early age at 1st sexual intercourse
  4. Herpes simplex
  5. Smoking
  6. Immunosuppressive drugs
- Not a risk factor: family history — it depends on use, not genetics (as printed, joke wording).
- **Symptoms**
  1. Most common presenting complaint: abnormal vaginal bleeding or menorrhagia 80-90%
  2. Invasion symptoms, e.g. into bladder → hematuria; into rectum → rectovaginal fistula
- Figure: cervical cancer stages — early and late stage IB: carcinoma only in (still limited to) the cervix; stage IIB: the cancer spreads outside the cervix.
- **Histologic subtype**
  1. SCC 80%
  2. Adenocarcinoma 15%
  3. Adenosquamous 5%
- **Metastasis**: first node is the obturator node.
- **Investigation**
  1. Pap smear — cancer screening: first 3 years after starting sexual activity, then yearly until age 70; pap sensitivity 60%
  2. Abnormal pap → calposcopy (as printed) and biopsy; diagnostic in 99%
  3. Cancer → CT pelvis to see whether confined to the pelvis or spread further
- **Treatment**
  1. Early cancer — confined to the cervix, no lymphovascular invasion, ≤ 3 cm and depth ≤ 5 mm → radical TAH+BSO + pelvic LND first; if the patient still wants children, may consider "co…" (as printed, text breaks off at the page end; the next page shows cold cone biopsy: a large area of tissue around the cervix excised for examination).
  2. Beyond the vagina but confined to the pelvis → chemo RT

## Ovarian Cancer
- Most common cause of death among gyne cancers.
- At diagnosis 65% have already spread; most common cause of death is bowel obstruction.
- **Risk factor**
  1. Nulliparity, early menarche & late menopause
  2. BRCA 1,2 mutation
  3. HNPCC
  4. Endometriosis
- Pill, breast feeding and tubal ligation are not risk factors (as printed).
- **Krukenberg tumor**: gastric cancer metastatic to the ovary; pathology shows signet ring cells.
- **Meige's syndrome** (as printed): ovarian fibroma (benign) causing ascites and pleural effusion; oophorectomy cures it.
- **Symptoms**
  1. 80% present with abdominal swelling 2° to ascites
  2. Next: non-localized vaque (as printed) abdominal pain, fever, night sweat, wt. loss
- **Type**
  1. **Epithelial cancer** — most common type (95%); most common subtype papillary serous carcinoma; worst prognosis clear cell type
  2. **Germ cell tumor** — 5%, e.g. yolk sac, dysgerminoma, immature teratoma; often secrete hormones and tumor markers
  3. **Sex cord stromal tumor** — e.g. granulosa cell tumor
- **Investigation**
  1. Tumor marker: CA 125 — unsuitable for screening (raised in only 50% of early cases, and also in benign conditions); used more for monitoring treatment
  2. Transvaginal US — single most effective and efficient modality to assess pelvic anatomy & pathology
  3. MRI gives no more detail than US, so not the initial workup
  4. CT chest, abdomen & pelvis — good for metastasis
- **US features associated malignancy**
  1. Solid component: nodular or papillary projection
  2. Septum thick > 2 mm
  3. Color or power doppler: increased flow in the solid component
  4. Neovascularization
  5. Bilaterality
  6. Ascites
  - Example caption: a complex 12 × 10-cm mass in a premenopausal woman with multiple septations and solid elements with marked vascular flow was correctly identified as malignant (Stage III papillary adenocarcinoma).
- **Treatment**
  1. Low malignant potential or borderline tumor → SO of that side only; postmenopausal → TAH+BSO
  2. Epithelial invasive cancer → cytoreductive surgery gives tissue diagnosis, staging, and is the most effective Rx.
     - Procedure:
       1. Peritoneal washing for cytology
       2. TAH+BSO
       3. Omentectomy
       4. Biopsy:
          - 4.1 Peritoneum of diaphragm, bilateral paracolic gutters, bilateral pelvis & cul de sac
          - 4.2 Lymph nodes: paraaortic at the renal hilum and pelvic lymph nodes
       5. Resection of all visible disease, as much as possible
       6. Remember: no random liver biopsy
- Even with carcinomatosis, cytoreduction can still give long-term survival.
- **Incidental ovarian mass found at surgery for another disease**
  1. Postmenopausal woman → oophorectomy first and await permanent section; some gynecologists send frozen section and proceed if cancer.
  2. Premenopausal woman → partial oophorectomy with frozen section; if cancer, do SO only first, then await permanent section; TAH should be a 2nd stage.
- **Incidental ovarian cyst found at surgery for another disease**
  1. Use all findings to decide functional vs neoplastic cyst:

| Functional cyst | Neoplastic cyst |
|---|---|
| Thinner cyst wall | Thicker cyst wall |
| Less vascularization on ovarian surface | Increased vascularization on ovarian surface |
| Yellowish colored content of cyst | Clear, dark brown or dermoid-like content of cyst |

  - 1.1 Functional cyst > 5 cm → cystectomy (risk of bleeding and torsion); < 5 cm → leave it
  - 1.2 Dermoid (teratoma) or cystadenoma → treat regardless of size
  2. Dermoid or teratoma — classic finding: calcium in the cyst. Rx: cystectomy or SO.
     - US slide labels for bilateral ovarian dermoids: cystic with linear strands, solid echogenic mass, predominantly cystic.
  3. Cystadenoma → SO, as it can turn malignant.
- **Unexpected carcinomatosis found at surgery for another disease** → by appearance, the primary is likely ovarian. Rx: biopsy the mass, close, await permanent section.

## Hydatidiform Mole
- Abnormal pregnancy: placental villi swollen with excess trophoblast.
- **Cause**: unclear; not cancer, but may develop into placental cancer.
- Usually diagnosed in the 1st trimester: abnormal vaginal bleeding 84% and uterus larger than dates (36%).
- **Investigation**
  1. Very high serum beta HCG
  2. US: swollen placenta, classic snow storm pattern
- **Treatment**
  1. Terminate pregnancy by suction, then curettage to make sure the mole is completely removed
  2. No further children wanted → hysterectomy
  3. Malignant → chemo Rx
