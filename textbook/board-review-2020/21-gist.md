# Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST) 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 1055-1076 (PDF pages 1059-1080)

- Intro: GIST is the most common mesenchymal tumor of the GI tract; it can occur in any organ, most at the stomach (50%) and 25% at the small bowel, the rest at colon, rectum, esophagus, retroperitoneum; among all GI cancers GIST is under 0.5%, which is very little; the author notes that every year GIST is a standard topic at international oncology seminars and conferences.
- GIST is a good example of research into diagnosis and treatment down to the molecular level; in the past, tumors thought to be leiomyosarcoma that spread to the liver should be re-checked; if the patient turns out to have GIST with many liver metastases, drug treatment is still given, and if the liver mass is large, it can be cut, with IOUS as a helper (like peeling a durian [as printed]).
- Exam tip: if a submucosal tumor is found, review the CT film and the pathology slide; GIST must always be in the differential diagnosis.
- GIST is a rare tumor of the GI tract arising from mesenchymal tissue.
- Sarcoma sites: 1. extremities 50%; 2. gastrointestinal 25%; 3. retroperitoneal 15%; 4. head and neck 9%.
- Gastrointestinal sarcoma has 2 different types: 1. GIST: 80-90%; 2. non-GIST: about 10-20%.
- History: in the past most GIST were diagnosed as leiomyoma and leiomyosarcoma by HE stain on the pathology slide only; GIST and smooth muscle tumor are both usually spindle cells, hard to separate.
- 1983 Mazur said the mass is not a smooth muscle tumor but did not know where to put it, so named it "stromal tumor".
- After 15 years of doubt, in 1998 Hirota found the tumor arises from mutation of the C-kit proto-oncogene, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor target; from this point GIST is found more and more; masses in the past thought to be smooth muscle tumors were mostly GIST; with more understanding of tumor biology, molecular targeted therapy works very well.

## Epidemiology
- M slightly > F; average age 50-60; a rare tumor, only 0.1% of GI tumors.

## Cell of origin
- From the interstitial cell of Cajal (ICC), like a junction connecting the intramural neuron and smooth muscle, located around the myenteric plexus, acting as the pacemaker of GI motility. Figure: stain of cells of Cajal vs GIST (skip).

## Pathogenesis
- Normally, proliferation and anti-apoptosis of the cell depend on stem cell factor binding to the KIT receptor, which is in the tyrosine kinase receptor group; when two bind in pairs, a signal activates the intracellular tyrosine kinase domain so the cell proliferates and resists apoptosis. Figure: KIT signaling (skip).
- In GIST patients, there is an abnormality in the KIT receptor that activates the domain all the time, without needing stem cell factor, making the cell divide quickly into a GIST mass; so a drug to treat GIST must block tyrosine kinase.
- The cause of the KIT receptor abnormality is mutation of: 1. C-kit protooncogene, 85-90%; 2. PDGFRA gene (platelet derived growth factor receptor alpha gene), 5-10%.

## Location
1. GI-GIST:
   - 1.1 Stomach: number 1, 50-60%; usually at the proximal part near the fundus.
   - 1.2 Small bowel: number 2; jejunum and ileum together 25%; duodenum only 5%.
   - 1.3 Colon, rectum and anus: 4%.
   - 1.4 Esophagus and appendix: under 1%.
2. E-GIST (extra GI GIST): GIST at omentum, mesentery, retroperitoneum, abdominal wall; most E-GIST are metastases from a primary GI-GIST.
- Wild type GIST: GIST with no mutation of either C-kit or PDGFRA gene; shows that GIST probably has mutation of other genes, needing high-tech labs, e.g. DOG 1 (not a pet name; it comes from "Discovered On GIST").
- NCCN V.2 2019 divides GIST into 4 groups: 1. small or microscopic GIST: size under 2 cm, usually found incidentally, e.g. EGD for other reasons, with the usual site the proximal stomach, if biopsy is taken from the gastric specimen; 2. localized or potentially resectable GIST; 3. locally advanced GIST; 4. unresectable or metastatic GIST.

## Clinical Manifestations
- Depends on size and location of the GIST.
- 70% have nonspecific symptoms: GI bleeding, vague abdominal pain, abdominal mass, fatigue, early satiety, anorexia; large masses give pressure symptoms; obstruction is possible.
- 20% no symptoms: incidental finding from workup, e.g. endoscopy or an operation for other causes; tissue taken then found; conclusion: most of the cases diagnosed as GIST are found by chance.
- 10% are known as GIST at autopsy.
- Size at first presentation ranges from 1-35 cm, average 5 cm.
- Metastasis: hematogenous spread to the liver is the most common, next locoregional metastasis to the peritoneum; spread to lung and bone is also possible but less; if GIST is found in lung or bone, the case is very advanced; spread to lymph nodes is very rare.
- Differential diagnosis (mesenchymal tumor): 1. GIST; 2. smooth muscle tumor: leiomyoma and leiomyosarcoma; 3. peripheral nerve sheath tumor: schwannoma; 4. intraabdominal fibromatosis.

## Evaluation and Workup
- History and physical examination.
- CT scan: usually CT abdomen and pelvis with 1 cm cuts; chest needs only a plain film at first, since lung metastasis is rare.
  - Use CT with IV and oral contrast as the initial imaging modality; if unclear may add MRI, but MRI is not sent as routine.
  - Advantages of CT: 1. tells size and extent of the GIST, especially metastasis in the abdominal cavity; 2. assists operative planning; 3. helps assess response to targeted therapy.
  - Disadvantage: cannot separate inflammatory adhesion from malignant involvement of adjacent organs.
  - CT features: 1. a solid hyperdense enhancing submucosal mass; aggressive feature: a big mass, especially size over 1 cm, appears as a complex mass with necrosis, ulceration, cavitation from degeneration, local tissue infiltration, metastasis; 2. usually exophytic growth with cavitation and necrosis; 3. lymph nodes are not enlarged; if many nodes enlarge, think of other diseases.
- PET scan: used with CT in assessing response to targeted therapy, to decide whether to adjust the drug dose or change the drug; for use as a baseline before treatment it may not be necessary.
- Endoscopy: whether gastroscopy or colonoscopy, the GIST seen is a single well circumscribed submucosal mass with fairly smooth mucosa; some may have ulceration. Figure: endoscopic photo (skip).
  - If gastric GIST, use EUS as well, as a guide for biopsy; if an ulcer is seen, edge not smooth, mass growing fast, EUS shows a hypoechoic mass arising from the 4th layer (muscularis propria); if a heterogeneous pattern, the tendency is high malignant potential.
- Histology (HE stain), 3 patterns: 1. spindle cell: 70%; 2. epithelioid cell: 9%; 3. mixed or pleomorphic: 21%.
  - Histologic features cannot separate GIST, smooth muscle tumor and schwannoma, so immunophenotype is needed to help separate.
- Criteria requiring immunophenotype examination: 1. primary mesenchymal tumor of the GI, except where it is clearly esophageal leiomyoma, small polypoid leiomyoma, schwannoma; 2. histologic features cannot separate GIST, leiomyoma and schwannoma; 3. mass at the abdominal wall or in the abdominal cavity, without knowing which mesenchymal tumor; 4. spindle cell tumor of the liver.
- Tissue for IHC: 1. paraffin embedded tumor block; 2. fresh tissue.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Leiomyoma or SA | - | 10-15% | 100% | 100% | - |

- Because GIST expresses C-kit protein, immunohistochemistry shows GIST cells stain with C-kit antibody (CD 117), seen as brown; C-kit positive: think GIST, and helps separate from leiomyosarcoma. Figure: GIST KIT(+) vs leiomyosarcoma KIT(-) (skip).
- SMA, Desmin positive: think smooth muscle tumor.
- S-100 positive: think schwannoma.
- Once more: if the shape of the mass, the skin and everything look truly like GIST but C-kit is negative, test for PDGFRA gene mutation next; if PDGFRA positive, it is a KIT-negative GIST.
- Genotype or kinase genotype: testing whether the KIT mutation is in exon 11 or 9, or is wild type; NCCN V.2 2016 advises testing every case, including PDGFRA mutation; knowing the genotype helps GIST doctors know what drug and what dose to give. Figure: KIT overall mutation frequency 85-90%: exon 9 (18.1%), exon 11 (66.9%), exon 13 (1.6%), exon 17 (1.6%); PDGFRA 5%.
  1. KIT mutation at exon 11 gets a good result with imatinib.
  2. KIT mutation at exon 9 responds less to imatinib; if it must be given, give double dose.
  3. Wild type GIST group, both KIT and PDGFRA negative: wild type does not respond to imatinib.
  - Residents only need this much; for our country molecular pathology down to exon level is not yet available [as printed].

## Tissue Biopsy
- In the past preoperative biopsy was not needed if the tumor looked resectable entirely; go straight to operate; but now if tissue can be obtained by EUS-FNA, do it that way.
- Principles of biopsy:
  1. GIST is a hypervascular tumor; percutaneous biopsy may cause tumor hemorrhage and dissemination.
  2. Indications everyone accepts: 2.1 planning neoadjuvant therapy; 2.2 suspicion of lymphoma; 2.3 other primary or metastatic malignancies.
  3. EUS biopsy, if possible, should be the first choice; avoid percutaneous biopsy.
  4. If a choice is available, core biopsy samples are preferred over FNA.
  5. Do not forget to request immunohistochemistry.

## Treatment of Patients with GIST
- Surgery is the primary treatment and the only chance for cure.
- Targeted therapy is a very good helper.
- Chemotherapy and RT do not work.
- In general, all resectable non-metastatic GIST in operative candidates should be resected entirely, whether open or lap.
- For very small and localized resectable GIST, no targeted therapy is needed before surgery; but for locally advanced GIST, give neoadjuvant therapy for at least 6 months before operating.
- Very small gastric GIST (size under 2 cm) without high risk features from EUS, i.e. irregular border, cystic spaces, ulceration, echogenic foci and heterogenicity: do endoscopic surveillance every 6 months for 5 years; but if there are high risk features, resect.
- Marginal resectable or locally advanced primary GIST:
  - Especially large masses pressing on vital structures; if cut en bloc there may be significant morbidity; if possible send EUS and biopsy first; if it is truly GIST, give imatinib and monitor response with baseline CT together with subsequent PET scan at 2-4 weeks, to assess therapeutic effect (for our country PET is not yet available [as printed]).
  - In cases given the drug with a good response, continue imatinib; maximum response takes about 6 months; then use CT to assess whether it can be operated; if so, take it out and give imatinib post op, continuing.
  - If given the drug without response, or progressive disease, increase the dose or change to a second line drug.
- Advanced, unresectable, recurrence or metastatic GIST:
  - Treatment: give targeted therapy, then assess response, continuing treatment about 3 months, with CT and PET; if it turns into resectable GIST, then resect, then PO imatinib continues.
  - If given and still unresectable, increase the dose or change drug.
  - Cytoreductive surgery with the aim of debulking tumor without complete resection is no better than using the drug.
  - Role of palliative resection: 1. large mass with significant symptoms, e.g. much pain; 2. complications from the mass, e.g. bleeding, abscess, fistula.
- Liver metastasis:
  - If after giving imatinib the liver mass is stable disease and an R0 resection is possible, resect it out.
  - But if cannot operate or patient not fit, two options: 1. RFA: the mass should be smaller than 4 cm; total masses under 5; and the mass should be away from the central bile duct and major vascular structures; 2. HACE or hepatic arterial chemoembolization.

## Principles of Operative Treatment
- When exploring, the surgeon must be meticulous: look at the liver and peritoneum; do not forget the lesser sac and the pouch in the pelvis.
- GIST mass is rather soft and bleeds a lot, ruptures easily; the operator must handle it gently; if we manipulate the mass until it ruptures, prognosis is poor [as printed].
- The operation should aim for R0; a wedge resection is enough; during operation do not tear the pseudocapsule, and the margin should be more than 2 cm; however, if the margin is under 2 cm or microscopically positive, it does not affect survival.
- Overall, open surgery looks to have better results than laparoscopic, since there is fear of rupture of the mass; in 2013 laparoscopic resection became stronger: in the past it was advised for masses smaller than 4 cm with a bag to deliver; but nowadays even if the mass is larger than 5 cm, lap can still be used while still giving an excellent cancer outcome.
- If adherent or attached to an adjacent organ, most can be separated; but if invasion into the adjacent organ is certain, or the mass is firmly stuck to that structure, trying to dissect it apart usually causes bleeding and rupture of the mass; so such cases should be resected en bloc.
- Because GIST rarely spreads to lymph nodes, LND is not needed; seen in the exam papers before; except in cases with grossly positive nodes, then take those nodes out.
- Older recommendation (NCNN 2007 [as printed]): in a case operated for R0 but the permanent pathology says microscopic positive margin (R1), three options were left: 1. re-exploration and wider resection if the surgeon thinks it will not cause severe functional morbidity (most people do not agree with this); 2. give targeted therapy by risk criteria of recurrence; 3. watchful waiting, which got the most support in 2007.
- Currently, NCCN 2019 concludes no need to re-operate; give the drug as best, not even R1: whatever the permanent report says, R0 resection; there are many trials, especially phase 3 ACOSOG Z9001, which says for a tumor with intermediate or high risk of recurrence, a large mass (5 cm or more) with mitotic rate 5/50 HF or more, give imatinib as adjuvant treatment straight away; our country cannot give it yet, since the drug is very expensive; if the pathology comes out low risk GIST, no adjuvant treatment is needed; observation with serial CT scan is enough.

### Gastric GIST
- Most gastric GIST grow slowly; masses smaller than 2 cm are usually benign; for operation, do wedge resection to obtain a 2 cm free margin, trying to preserve the EGJ and pylorus; except if the mass is right there, avoid it, and anatomical resection or gastrectomy is needed; LND is not needed; for endoscopic resection by EGD (endoscopic submucosal resection), use it for small masses, not over 2 cm.
- EGJ GIST: from Sabiston 2017, advises choosing esophageal-sparing by giving imatinib first; when the mass shrinks, do just a wedge resection; do not do esophagogastrectomy.
- Difference in operation between gastric GIST and gastric adenocarcinoma:

| | GIST | Adenocarcinoma |
|---|---|---|
| Margins | wide margins not required | for clear margins, need a 4-cm distance from tumor; need 10-cm margins for diffuse-type tumors |
| Gastrectomy | wedge or segmental resection often sufficient; formal gastrectomy for large proximal gastric GISTs | total gastrectomy for tumors in the proximal third of the stomach |
| Lymphadenectomy | unnecessary | for staging and therapeutic purposes |

### Minimally invasive approaches for gastric GIST
- Currently laparoscopic surgery is accepted more for resecting gastric GIST, including jejunum and ileum; the way depends on the location of the tumor; in the stomach, the sites suitable for lap are the greater curvature and anterior wall, and the mass should be not larger than 5 cm; the cut mass should be put in a bag first, to prevent spillage and seeding at the port site. Figure: laparoscopic wedge resection of gastric wall (skip).
- RCT by Karakkussis, reported in Ann Surg 2011, compared open vs lap resection in gastric GIST, 40 cases each side, similar size not over 8 cm: no difference in operating time and in margin clearance or recurrence; the lap group was better: less blood loss (25 vs 100 cc) and shorter hospital stay (4 vs 7 days).
- Intragastric resection was presented by Privette, reported in Surg Endosc 2008, who divided gastric GIST into 3 types (Figure: stomach diagram and transgastric resection of GIST on lesser curvature with simultaneous endoscopy):
  - Type 1: tumor at the fundus and greater curve; treatment: lap partial gastrectomy.
  - Type 2: tumor at the antrum and prepyloric region; treatment: lap distal gastrectomy.
  - Type 3: tumor at the lesser curve near the GEJ; treatment: lap transgastric resection by cutting the tumor via the gastric wall guided by intraoperative endoscopy.
- Today MIS is strong: reports of success in resecting gastric GIST with single port, NOTES and robotic surgery are coming out continually; we keep following new technology.

### Periampullary GIST
- GIST at the duodenum is found in 4%, but right at the periampullary area is rather rare.
- Patients present with progressive jaundice, anorexia, weight loss, followed by fever and abdominal pain.
- May need PD followed by adjuvant imatinib. Figure: CECT 8.3 x 8.1 cm periampullary tumor (skip).

### Small bowel and large bowel GIST
- Found often at the jejunum; small bowel GIST is much more aggressive than stomach; treatment is segmental resection to get a margin of 2-3 cm together with adjuvant imatinib.

### Rectal GIST
- Found less; usually an extraluminal lesion; the pelvis is narrow, so it is hard to operate; rectal GIST behaves very aggressively, with high mitotic rate, recurs after resection and metastasizes, even when the mass is smaller than 2 cm. Figures: endoscopic ulcerative lesion, EUS mass from muscularis propria, CT (skip).
- A small mass can be resected transanally, but recurrence will be high; currently it is advised to do LAR straight away, avoiding APR, except for very low lesions where the margin is not free after cutting, which may need APR.

## Primary GIST Following Complete Resection
- Assessing the outcome of resection of GIST is rather difficult, because GIST has behavior of many kinds, benign or aggressive, uncertain.
- Some groups tried to set criteria for the aggressiveness of clinical behavior of GIST, and the chance of relapse or metastasis, how much.
- Because the teacher often organizes GIST meetings, many criteria appear; the author lists them briefly:
  1. NIH criteria (Fletcher 2002): classifies into very low, low, intermediate and high risk, using the size of the primary tumor (the largest mass) and the mitotic index per HPFs.
  2. Modified NIH criteria (Haung 2007): classifies the high risk group of NIH 2002 into a new group called very high risk, adding up.
- De Matteo from Sloan Kettering says the NIH criteria seem to miss tumor location: he found small bowel GIST has more aggressive behavior than gastric GIST.
- That is the origin of two more groups setting criteria adding tumor location together with size and mitotic activity:
  3. AFIP criteria (Miettinen with Losota, published 2006): mixes NIH with tumor location; valued a lot at first; (Goh from Singapore then mixed NIH with AFIP, said better, but also criticized; most say it does not work).
  4. Joensuu (his name is admired by GIST lovers; remember it): set criteria in 2008, taking the AFIP 2006 criteria and re-summarizing; Joensuu uses tumor size and site together with mitotic count.

- Besides Joensuu's criteria, there are criteria of the ESMO group, from Japan, and many more trying to set up; GIST fans may keep going; residents who are lazy about GIST are asked to beware the stack.
- Remember: assessing the risk of recurrence is done only at primary resection; for those who got imatinib first and then operated, it is hard to assess risk of recurrence because mitotic index cannot be assessed.
- If there is recurrence after resection, 50% recur within 2 years.
- Summary: risk of GIST recurrence rises with: 1. mitotic rate over 5/50 HPF; 2. size over 5 cm; 3. intestinal origin; 4. tumor rupture.

## Targeted Therapy
- Drugs that block the target tyrosine kinase; considered the main treatment in advanced GIST patients.
- There are many drugs in this group; the key one to know is imatinib; for the others just get an idea.

### Imatinib mesylate
- Trade name in the USA: Gleevec; outside the USA: Glivec.
- FDA approved in 2002 as the 1st line treatment.
- In patients who respond well, tumor volume may shrink within 3-4 months:
  1. Partial response (PR): 54%. 2. Stable disease (SD): 28%. 3. Complete response (CR): under 5%. 4. Progressive disease (PD): 14%.
- Response criteria: summarized for reading later.
- Response to imatinib: 1. KIT exon 11 mutation responds best; 2. exon 9 mutation gets less benefit; wild type GIST responds poorly; but anyway the first drug used should still be imatinib.
- Dose given: 400 mg/day; maximum dose not over 800 mg/day, since giving a dose higher than 800 mg/day gives more side effects than can be tolerated.
- Give a double dose in: 1. cases given 400 mg/day that still have progressive disease; 2. exon 9 mutation should start with double dose straight away.
- In the past the drug was given about 1 year, but when stopped, 80% got PD again; so the current concept is to give it continuously until intolerance or resistance occurs (ESMO 2010).
- From the ESMO 2010 guideline:
  1. Adjuvant imatinib: they advise it for the high risk group; NCCN 2019 only says if intermediate risk, give straight away; even if R0 can be resected, give for not less than 3 years.
  2. For locally advanced, unresectable or metastatic GIST by ESMO 2010 and NCCN 2016: give imatinib continuously with no set limit; stop only when there is drug resistance or progressive disease.
  3. In cases initially unresectable, but after giving imatinib the response looks good enough to resect: they advise taking it out, then postop continue imatinib.
- In 2014, ACOSOG Z9001 reported an RCT comparing giving the drug 1 year vs 3 years in grossly negative resection, together with a separate trial of Joensuu 2014: the studies concluded that giving for 3 years is better than 1 year; but to give longer than that, follow his RCT further (Sabiston 2017 p.770).

### Drug resistant
1. Primary resistant: 1.1 no response at all (seen in 1-2 months); 1.2 mass had response but recurs within 6 months; 1.3 PD within 6 months.
2. Secondary resistant: the mass that had been controlled with the drug and recurs after 6 months, further divided into partial and multifocal resistant.

### Adverse drug reaction
- Symptoms improve after treatment of the symptoms, without stopping the drug: 1. edema from fluid retention; 2. nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fatigue; 3. muscle cramp; 4. skin rash, dermatitis; 5. anemia.
- Discontinuation: must stop the drug if: 1. liver impairment; 2. leucopenia.

### Sunitinib malate
- Also called SU 11248; trade name Sutent.
- An oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor that inhibits KIT, PDGFRA, VEGFR (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor) and many others; so it suits those who are secondary resistant to imatinib.
- FDA approved in 2006 for imatinib-resistant GIST, and also for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and pancreatic NET.
- Dose: 50 mg/day continuously for 4 weeks, rest 2 weeks, then continue the same way.
- PR 8%; SD 58%.
- Advised to be used as the 2nd line treatment.
- Increases survival from 6.4 weeks to 27.3 weeks.
- Since the drug is very expensive and in America insurance companies do not pay, doctors do not often prescribe it.
- Adverse drug reactions of sunitinib: 1. fever, headache, fatigue; 2. diarrhea; 3. skin reaction at hands and feet; 4. hypothyroidism; 5. fluid retention; 6. anemia; 7. elevated liver function test.

### Nilotinib
- Trade name Tasigna; usually used in chronic myeloid leukemia; later used to treat GIST.
- A 2nd generation drug, suits imatinib-resistant cases, because the drug level in the cell is higher than imatinib.
- Dose 400 mg bid; found that 75% of patients benefit.
- Should be used as 3rd line treatment, i.e. GIST that is resistant to both imatinib and sunitinib.

### Developing targeted GIST therapies
- Many new drugs are coming for those resistant to imatinib, currently in trials, e.g. Recentin or AZD 2171 (an oral VEGFR blocker), sorafenib, dasatinib, motesanib, PTK 787, flavopiridol, RAD 001, perifosine, PKC 112.
- A new drug FDA approved: regorafenib, used in cases resistant to imatinib and sunitinib, with data supporting that taking it is better than doing nothing.
- The reason for writing about new drugs is to let residents know the GIST field has a future and goes far.

## Evaluation of Targeted Treatment Response
- GIST is a tumor group where chemo does not work well, unlike the expensive targeted drugs, which look to have a big future.
- The tumor's response to imatinib or another targeted drug should have criteria set for measurement: those who respond keep getting the drug; those who don't need dose-up or a change of drug.
- In the past we assessed by WHO and RECIST criteria, which were later found a bit weak; now mostly the Choi criteria are favored.
- A summary (if lost in a symposium on GIST, you will hear the experts discuss).
- WHO response criteria (1979): see response by the longest 2 dimensions of the mass, multiplied together to measure area:
  - Complete response (CR): GIST mass disappears.
  - Partial response (PR): GIST mass shrinks by 50% or more; small masses must not enlarge more than 25%.
  - Progressive disease (PD): a large mass grows while being treated by 25% or more, or small masses growing, or new masses appearing that never existed.
  - Stable disease (SD): masses that do not fit any of the above groups.
  - WHO criteria did not work because they did not define the imaging to be used, and if there are many masses, they did not say which to measure and how to measure small masses.
- RECIST criteria (2000): rules:
  1. The imaging to measure GIST size is CT or MRI.
  2. Measure the largest mass, by length.
  3. The mass measured must be larger than 1 cm.
  4. Each organ: measure not more than 5 masses; in total, not more than 10 masses.
  - RECIST groups responses into 4 like WHO: CR: all GIST masses gone; PR: the sum of measured GIST masses (several masses added) shrinks by 30% or more; PD: the sum of GIST masses grows by 20% or more, or new masses arise; SD: those not fitting any.
  - Disadvantages of RECIST (unreliable at times): 1. a mass getting larger may result from hemorrhage or tumor necrosis, which are features of those that respond (good), not bad; 2. appearance of a new lesion may result from GIST responding well, where a lesion seen is due to change from heterogeneous hyperdense to hypodense.
- Modified Choi's CT criteria (2007): Choi's criteria (mentioned most and look more advanced than others): Choi assesses not by mass size alone but also by tumor density; those that respond well show tumor necrosis with cystic degeneration; NCCN V.2 2016 also advises using Choi's:
  - CR: GIST mass disappears completely and no new lesion.
  - PR: size decreases 10% or more and tumor density decreases 15% or more in HU, without new lesion.
  - PD: GIST mass grows 10% or more and the density does not enter the criteria for PR group, or new lesion, or new intratumoral nodule, or an existing intratumoral nodule that enlarges, or from hypo- to homo- back to hyperdensity with heterogeneous lesion again.
  - SD (Choi): those not meeting CR, PR, or PD criteria.
- Table (assessing response on tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy), Choi vs RECIST:

| Response | Choi criteria | RECIST criteria |
|---|---|---|
| CR | (I) disappearance of all lesions; (II) no new lesions | same |
| PR | (I) decrease in size of 10% or more, or decrease in tumor density (HU) of 15% or more on CT; (II) no new lesions; (III) no obvious progression of non-measurable disease | at least 30% decrease in the sum of diameter of target lesions |
| SD | (I) does not meet criteria for CR, PR or progression; (II) no symptomatic deterioration attributed to tumor progression | same as (I) |
| PD | (I) increase in tumor size of 10% or more and does not meet criteria of PR by HU on CT; (II) new lesions; (III) new intratumoral nodules or increase in size of existing intratumoral nodules | (I) at least 20% increase in the sum of diameter of target lesions, along with an absolute increase of at least 5 mm; (II) new lesions |

- After targeted therapy 3 months, send CT to assess response of the GIST mass to the drug; if responding well, we see (Figure: pre-imatinib vs good response CT):
  1. Tumor density decreases, becoming a homogeneous hypodense mass, measured by HU (Hounsfield unit).
  2. Absence of tumor vessel and solid enhancing nodule.
  3. If not tumor hemorrhage, the GIST mass must be smaller.
- PET: look at FDG uptake; if uptake decreases, it shows good response; PET is not used as routine; use PET in cases where CT is unclear or does not match the patient's symptoms. Figure: CT and PET pre-imatinib vs 4-week post imatinib (skip).

## Follow Up
- How often to see the patient depends on risk of recurrence.
- Very low and low risk: CT every 6 months for 5 years.
- Intermediate and high risk: CT every 3 months for 3 years, then CT every 6 months until completing 5 years.

## Survival Rate
1. Size: 1.1 under 5 cm: 5-yr 60%; 1.2 over 10 cm: 5-yr 20%.
2. Mitotic rate: 2.1 under 5/50 HPF: metastasis 20%; 2.2 over 10/50 HPF: metastasis 60%.
3. R0 resection: 5-yr 40-55%.
4. R1 resection: 5-yr 5%.
- Prognosis is poor if: 1. large mass over 10 cm; 2. mitotic rate over 10/50 HF; 3. location at small bowel and rectum; 4. high risk GIST; 5. R1 or R2 resection, or the mass ruptured during operation (spillage).

## Summary guideline for GIST treatment (Schwartz)
