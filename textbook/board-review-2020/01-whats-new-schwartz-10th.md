## What's New in Schwartz's Eleventh Edition 2019 (history of the textbook)
- Before 1960 several surgery textbooks were sold (e.g. Rhoads, Moyer, Hardy); the author liked Hardy most for its key-point summaries, but such books lasted only 1-2 editions.
- 1967: four young surgeons led by Seymour I. Schwartz, with Richard C. Lillehei, G. Thomas Shires and David Hume, formed a team to write a textbook of surgery; 1st edition published 1969, as a counterpart of Harrison's Textbook of Medicine.
- Seymour I. Schwartz served as Editor-in-Chief; the book was easy to read and understand, making it the "bible" every surgeon should own; now in its 11th edition.
- **Five eras of Schwartz (as divided by the author)**
  1. Pioneer era (ยุคบุกเบิก) — editions 1 and 2. In 2527 (1984) the author, as chief resident at Phramongkutklao, took an elective at Police General Hospital; อาจารย์ ชุมศักดิ์ พฤกษาพงษ์ lent him edition 1 used during his US residency; conclusion: an easy-to-read textbook.
  2. Golden era (ยุคทอง) — editions 3 and 4, written by the "4S" (Schwartz, Shires, Spencer, Storer). Then a popular saying: internal medicine trainees carry Harrison, surgical trainees carry Schwartz.
     - Schwartz — excellent at everything, especially HBP; Shires — trauma (RIP); Spencer — CVT and vascular (RIP); Storer — everything, especially upper and lower GI (RIP).
     - อาจารย์ ราชา ตะเภาพงษ์ (Fort Suranari Hospital, Nakhon Ratchasima) gave the author Schwartz edition 3 before his residency; reading Schwartz with Sabiston felt like flying first class at Board exam time (as printed).
  3. Declining era (ยุคเสื่อม) — after the death of Rodney Maingot, Schwartz was invited to rescue Maingot's Abdominal Operation (editions 9 and 10), leaving no time for his own book. Edition 5 (1989) had many famous contributors (King, Greenfield, Townsend, Goldberg ...) but the writing lost coherence and lacked summaries; readers dropped; Greenfield and Townsend went on to own/lead other books (as printed: Townsend took over Sabiston). Edition 6 worse; edition 7 still poor; meanwhile Sabiston, Cameron and Greenfield gained popularity.
  4. Present era (ยุคปัจจุบัน) — Seymour Schwartz removed the old editorial group and invited Charles Brunicardi (then Chief of Surgery in Houston, Texas) as Editor-in-Chief with 6 new associate editors = a "model change". Edition 8 still had some errors but was acceptable; editions 9 and 10 improved a lot.
  5. Digital era — web-based education and implications of social media: Schwartz edition 11 (mid-2019), a minor change from edition 10.
- The book had to adapt because learners (students, residents, even staff) now find knowledge on the internet, read online textbooks, load videos, use surgical simulators.
- Teachers are often asked whether textbooks are still needed — heavy and less up-to-date than the internet; this drove the minor change to Schwartz for a new generation.
- Seymour I. Schwartz's foreword: from the first edition (1969) to 2019 the book is 50 years old; he hopes surgical residents will keep it.
- Cover history: 1st ed 1969 (pioneer era); 4th ed 1984 (golden era) — cover with ancient Greek/Roman surgeons, used up to edition 7; 11th ed 2019 (digital era) — cover style changed from edition 9 to show MIS, signalling that surgery has moved into MIS, lap and robot.
- Celebration of 50 years of Schwartz organized by the American College of Surgeons.
- Two key people: F. Charles Brunicardi (Editor-in-Chief from edition 8 to present) and Lillian S. Kao (called เจ๊เกา; new associate editor from edition 11, restructuring the book's direction).
- F. Charles Brunicardi: born 1954; Chief Department of Surgery in Houston, Texas at edition 8; at edition 10 at UCLA; now Chief Department of Surgery at U. Toledo, Ohio. ## Hemostasis, Surgical Bleeding, and Transfusion
- Residents years 2-3: read **coagulopathy in liver disease** and hemostasis in trauma, and replacement therapy — frozen RBC, leukocyte-washed RBC, platelet concentrates, plasma, tranexamic acid (TXA), much discussed in trauma.
- **Topical hemostatic agents** for oozing bleeding: besides gelfoam and surgicel/avitene, newer products such as fibrin sealant (floseal, as printed "flocel") and platelet sealant (vitagel) work well, especially for raw-surface bleeding after hepatectomy. Caution with large amounts — leakage into vessels may increase risk of DIC. Topical agents are not a substitute for meticulous surgical technique; use only as an adjunct.

## Shock
- Little updated; read septic shock in the surgical infections chapter instead.
- New set of sepsis definitions:
  1. **Sepsis** = systemic signs of inflammation, e.g. fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis
  2. **Severe sepsis** = sepsis with hypotension
  3. **Septic shock** = severe sepsis with evidence of tissue hypoperfusion
- International guidelines for managing severe sepsis and septic shock list what must be done within 3 h and 6 h; following them increases survival. See p. 176 in surgical infections.

## Surgical Infections
- Better written than shock; includes the 3rd International consensus definition of sepsis and shock (2016, published 2017) — as printed, new but just a definition needing a meeting.
- Figure: SIRS / sepsis definitions —
  - SIRS = two or more of: (1) heart rate > 90; (2) temp > 100.4°F (38°C) or < 96.8°F (36°C); (3) respiratory rate > 20 or PaCO2 < 32 mmHg; (4) WBC > 12,000/mm3 or < 4,000/mm3, or > 10% band forms

  - Sepsis + lactic acidosis, or SBP < 90 or SBP drop ≥ 40 of normal, or organ dysfunction/hypotension/hypoperfusion → Severe sepsis
  - Severe sepsis + hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation → Septic shock
- **CDC prevention guideline for SSI 2017**: keep blood glucose ≤ 200 mg/dL in the perioperative period.
- **Surgical Infection Society revised guidelines on the management of intraabdominal infection 2017** — duration of antibiotics:
  1. Antibiotic ≤ 24 h in:
     - 1.1 Traumatic bowel perforation operated within 12 h
     - 1.2 PU perforation operated within 24 h

- **Surviving sepsis campaign international guidelines for management of sepsis and septic shock 2017** (p. 176) — Schwartz summarizes the consensus treatment recommendations well but at length; the author substitutes a ready-made summary:

| Item | Surviving Sepsis Guidelines 2017 |
|---|---|
| Definition | Sepsis: life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Septic shock: subset of sepsis with circulatory and cellular/metabolic dysfunction associated with a higher risk of mortality. Deemphasize "severe sepsis" and "sepsis criteria" |
| Fluids | At least 30 cc/kg in first 3 h, even in patients with ESRD and CHF; use crystalloids, consider albumin, avoid starch solutions |
| Vasopressors | Use norepinephrine (NE), avoid dopamine; add epinephrine if NE inadequate, add vasopressin to taper NE |
| Steroid | No steroid, unless shock refractory to adequate fluids and vasopressors |
| Antibiotics | Broad-spectrum (e.g. Vancomycin + Tapimycin); avoid double coverage |

- The surviving sepsis campaign met again in 2018 and updated management in the first hour; Schwartz 11th ed missed it (text closed earlier), so the author adds it from the internet (as printed: so you do not look clueless before the surgical infection "gods").
- **Surviving Sepsis Campaign Bundle 2018 update (first hour bundle)**
  1. Starts by defining time zero / time of presentation = time of triage, or time of diagnosis at the primary hospital before referral, or the time sepsis/septic shock information is complete on chart review
  2. Measure lactate level; if > 2 mmol/L, re-measure
  3. Send hemoculture before antibiotic
  4. Give broad spectrum antibiotics to cover first
  5. If BP drops or lactate > 4 mmol/L → crystalloid fluid 30 cc/kg
  6. If BP still poor after IV fluid → add vasopressor, maintain MAP > 65 mmHg
- Figure: Surviving Sepsis Campaign Hour-1 Bundle 2018 Update, as steps —
  1. Measure initial lactate level; if > 2 mmol/L, re-measure.
  2. Obtain blood cultures prior to administration of antibiotics.
  3. Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  4. Begin rapid administration of 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥ 4 mmol/L.
  5. Give vasopressors if hypotensive during or after fluid resuscitation for MAP ≥ 65 mmHg.
  - The 3-h and 6-h bundles have been combined into the 1-h bundle to begin resuscitation and management immediately. "Time zero"/"time of presentation" = time of ED triage or earliest chart annotation consistent with all elements of sepsis or septic shock (Levy MM, et al. Intensive Care Med 2018;44:925-928).

## Burns
- Written by a resident with staff supervision; well summarized; mostly follows the American Burn Association, concepts largely as in ATLS 10th edition.
- **Electrical burn**: beware cardiac arrhythmia, compartment syndrome and rhabdomyolysis; baseline ECG recommended in every electrical burn patient.
- **Resuscitation formula**: follow the American Burn Association and ATLS = 2 cc/kg/% burn, not the Parkland formula 4 cc/kg/% burn.
- **Inhalation injury** is well written and updated: best diagnosis is clinical presentation plus bronchoscopic evaluation. Bronchoscopy assesses airway injury severity, graded by the **Abbreviated Injury Score (AIS)** for inhalation injury, 0-4; higher score = more severe injury, more ARDS and more often tube + ventilator. Bronchoscopy also helps identify the causative organism so antibiotics can be chosen accurately.

| Grade (Abbreviated Injury Score) | Bronchoscopic findings |
|---|---|
| 0 (No injury) | Absence of carbonaceous deposits, erythema, edema, bronchorrhea, or obstruction |
| 1 (Mild injury) | Minor or patchy areas of erythema, carbonaceous deposits in proximal or distal bronchi [any or combination] |
| 2 (Moderate injury) | Moderate degree of erythema, carbonaceous deposits, bronchorrhea, with or without compromise of the bronchi [any or combination] |
| 3 (Severe injury) | Severe inflammation with friability, copious carbonaceous deposits, bronchorrhea, bronchial obstruction [any or combination] |
| 4 (Massive injury) | Evidence of mucosal sloughing, necrosis, endoluminal obliteration [any or combination] |

- Burn wound topical agents: nothing new — silver sulfadiazine, mafenide, silver nitrate as before.
- Energy requirement in burn patients: use the Curreri formula.
- Future of burn care: smart devices to assess burn size more accurately than the rule of 9 or the patient's palm = 1% burn.

- Discusses personalized cancer treatment: serum markers, molecular biology, and especially new imaging for earlier cancer detection, to reach more curable stages in the near future.
- **Novel radiosensitizers**: several chemotherapy drugs sensitize tumor cells to radiation, e.g. 5-FU, actinomycin D, gemcitabine, taxane, but with unwanted side effects; nano-technology drug production is being researched for safer agents.
- **Cancer gene therapy** is being studied: modify genes in certain viruses into suicide genes, put them into the patient to stimulate immune response and destroy cancer cells; hoped to become another systemic treatment option.
- **Trend in oncology**: more conservative resection with individualized systemic therapy.

## Transplantation
- 4 authors; the famous one is David L. Dunn, 1 of the 7 associate editors.
- New operative techniques and new immunosuppressive drugs in recent years, but the update is small (under 5%) and repetitive. New: monoclonal antibodies for humoral rejection — rituximab (printed "retuximab"), bortezomib, eculizumab, FDA approved.
- **Kidney transplantation**: success of living-donor kidney transplant with 1-year graft survival up to 98%. Immunologic evaluation has moved beyond ABO blood typing and HLA typing to new technology for anti-HLA antibody with greater specificity, helping match donor and recipient best.
- **Liver transplantation**
  1. Previously: liver transplant results in cholangiocarcinoma at Mayo Clinic — very strict patient selection, neoadjuvant treatment, then transplant → 5-year survival up to 82%. Edition 11 recommends that cholangiocarcinoma for transplant must be localized cancer with negative nodes.
  2. Contraindications: HIV, normally a contraindication for donor and recipient, is now only a relative contraindication in the new Schwartz (p. 382).
- **Pancreas transplantation**
  1. About 35,000 pancreas transplants worldwide so far, 75% in America.
  2. Islet cell transplantation: results better than before but only early on; one islet cell transplantation needs pancreases from up to 4 donors to extract islet cells for a single recipient.
- **Intestine and multivisceral transplantation** (p. 386-388) — the most updated part. Previously poor results because the intestine has much lymph node and Peyer's patch tissue causing an immune response against the graft (printed: "graft versus host rejection").
  - Now results are much better; survival is better than patients on chronic TPN. Notably, patients needing intestinal transplant often have evidence of chronic liver damage with portal hypertension, and some have dysmotility of the remaining stomach and bowel.
  - So intestinal transplant often requires transplanting other organs too = **multivisceral transplantation** (several organs in one operation), sometimes liver, pancreas, stomach and duodenum together as a big package — long operations, hard to train surgeons for (as printed).
- **Xenotransplantation** (p. 391-392): organs from animals, usually pigs, into humans; not yet successful because of rapid rejection. With long waiting lists, an International Xenotransplantation Association was formed. Interesting research: genetically engineered pigs to reduce rejection in humans, plus specific immunosuppression protocols; pig organs tested in non-pig animals now reach graft survival of 250 days.
- **United Network of Organ Sharing (UNOS)**: in the US about 105,000 people wait each year, but only 28,000 organs are available — hence interest in xenotransplantation via genetic engineering, tissue engineering and newer immunosuppressive drugs.
- Heart and lung transplant: 1st-year residents need not read unless very free.

## Minimally Invasive Surgery
- Written by John G. Hunter ("father of laparoscopic holes"); residents should read it to understand MIS principles, instruments, their use and many techniques.
- Besides holes made from outside into chest and abdomen, cameras now go into every natural orifice (mouth, anus, vagina) — no longer only for staff; residents can do it (as printed, jokes).
- Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, appendectomy, groin hernia repair and enteral access are basic lap — residents must do them themselves. Advanced procedures (esophagus, stomach, bariatric) — do not do alone; hold the camera first; to do them, become a fellow.
- Multiple abdominal port holes look ugly, so ports were combined into one hole using special instruments with multiple trocars = **single incision laparoscopic surgery**, leaving only one abdominal scar.
- Figure: single incision laparoscopic surgery — (A) single port accommodates multiple trocars; (B) multiple trocars through single skin incision.
- **Laparoscopic access** update removing the scar of single incision access: operating through natural orifices (mouth, anus, vagina) to remove bowel or gallbladder without any abdominal scar = **natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (NOTES)** — e.g. transoral cholecystectomy, transrectal cholecystectomy (as printed, "หยะแหยง" — yuck).
- Even retroperitoneal organs: adrenalectomy can be done through the vagina or anus.
- Endoscopic surgery via the mouth for peroral esophageal myotomy (POEM) in achalasia, and removing tumors in the esophagus or stomach, also count as NOTES.
- Thoracic surgery once using VATS can now enter the chest via natural orifices. Mediastinoscopy was traditionally done through a skin hole above the sternal notch, tunnelling into the mediastinal space — first with a rigid metal mediastinoscope, later fiberoptic; disadvantage: visible, ugly neck scar. (Figures: rigid and fiberoptic mediastinoscopy; anterior mediastinotomy = Chamberlain procedure.)
- Later: hole through the esophagus (transmediastinal mediastinoscopy, as printed), widened with a balloon, scope inserted to biopsy tissue and nodes — easy, no scar (p. 462).
- Figure: transesophageal mediastinal access, as steps — A: mucosa/submucosa needle knife puncture; B: saline injection into submucosa; C: creation of submucosal space (blunt dissection or dilation), then off-site needle knife penetration of muscularis propria; D: scope into mediastinum (right-sided needle knife incision into mediastinum, at level of aortic arch); E: closure of mucosal defect with clips (mucosal flap 10-15 cm).
- Also covers the role of MIS in intervention, and MIS in children, pregnant women and the elderly.
- **MIS robotic surgery** is increasingly done: instead of multiple holes with mechanical arms, the surgeon and fellow sit and control the instruments comfortably; now there is also single incision robotic surgery (figures: position of surgeon and assistant for robotic surgery p. 468 & 473; single incision robotic surgery p. 473).
- Closing advice from the author of the MIS chapter to young surgeons eager for new things — before using MIS ask:
  1. Is it safe for the patient? If yes →
  2. Is there an indication for MIS? If yes →
  3. Is MIS better than the conventional method? If yes →
  4. Assess yourself honestly: do you have enough MIS skill to get results as good as the experts? If not, choose the method safe for the patient, then go train as an MIS fellow (as printed, joke).

## Molecular Biology, Atomic Theory of Disease, and Precision Surgery
- Goes down to molecular level: basic molecules, DNA, gene regulation controlling signal pathways for cell cycle, growth and death; molecular/gene abnormalities causing cell dysfunction and disease.
- Now goes deeper to the **atom**: each cell is up to 99% oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium and phosphorus; atom-level abnormality makes molecules and genes abnormal → wrong signals → cells multiply → disease.
- From atom-level knowledge came study of specific disease-causing genes: in medicine = **precision medicine**; in surgery = **precision surgery**.
- Genes used to help treatment decisions are called **precision genes**, e.g. finding a BRCA gene guides thyroidectomy (as printed), and an EHD1 gene mutation guides gastrectomy (as printed).
- In the 21st century, **gene therapy** is expected to grow in importance; good surgeons should partner with a genomic scientist to research the genomics of disease and improve surgical outcomes.
- Much research on stem cells, cloning, and targeted genome with the CRISPR-Cas 9 system. As printed: the author wonders why surgeons need genome/molecule/atom; Thai surgeons already work to exhaustion.
- Most of the chapter is still animal experiments — read when it reaches humans.
- Then the clinical part — basic sciences are 500 pages (as printed: mild teasing of Kao).

## Skin and Subcutaneous Tissue
- Written by plastic surgeons from Georgetown University, Washington, DC.
- **Benign diseases**, especially skin and subcutaneous infection, are summarized well; NF summary is usable. But some topics, e.g. infections of the hair follicle (folliculitis, furuncle, carbuncle), lack detail — read other books.
- Tumors (benign and malignant) are readable but not detailed enough; residents should add Greenfield, Cameron and NCCN.
- New: research on why **UVB radiation** raises skin cancer risk — UV damages the function of Langerhans cells, the immune cells of the epidermis.
- **Hidradenitis suppurativa** arises from follicular occlusion of apocrine glands.
- **Treatment** (items 1 and 2 as in the previous edition)
  1. Early case → antibiotic
  2. Chronic case → as before, WLE removing all infected skin and subcutaneous tissue
  3. New in edition 11: disease classified into 3 classes (as printed, newer updates also add subclasses) = **Hurley staging**, describing extent of inflammation
  4. Treatment by Hurley staging: stage 1 and early stage 2 → drugs, prefer clinda, may need I & D too; advanced stage 2 and stage 3 → radical excision then cover with skin graft or local flap

|---|---|
| Stage I | Abscess formation (single or multiple) without sinus tracts and cicatrization |

- **Infantile hemangioma** (p. 527): vascular tumor growing fast in the first year (proliferative phase), then involution phase with slow shrinkage. If it does not shrink, grows fast, or obstructs airway, vision or feeding → treat; first-line = propranolol (printed "propanolol"); if not responding consider corticosteroid, interferon-alpha, laser; surgery is a last option.
- **Melanoma** — written well
  1. Role of SLN remains for lesions 1-4 mm; if SLN +ve, immediate lymphadenectomy increases disease-free survival by 7% vs waiting for nodes to appear (delayed lymphadenectomy)
  2. Surgical margin: cites a British trial 2016, but nothing differs from before
  3. Metastatic disease: new drug high dose interleukin-2 (printed "intetleukin-2") gives survival benefit (p. 534)
- **Merkel cell carcinoma**: aggressive neuroendocrine tumor; at diagnosis, nodal spread in 30% and metastasis in 50%, to liver, lung, bone and brain.
  - **Treatment**
    1. WLE down to fascia with margin 1-3 cm
    2. Node +ve → LND; node not palpable → SLN
- **AIDS-associated Kaposi sarcoma** (printed "Kapsi"): antiviral agents give dramatic response; if no response, other treatments such as RT or cryosurgery. Surgery's role now is only biopsy for diagnosis.

## Acquired Heart Disease
- Author: cardiothoracic surgeon from Washington U., St Louis, Missouri (the author does not know who).
- Overall about 95% of content is the same as before.
- New point 1: **cardiac risk assessment** in patients with **underlying heart disease** — **coronary artery disease** and **valvular heart disease** — using **guidelines** of the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association **(ACC/AHA)**.
- **ACC/AHA guideline 1**: **perioperative cardiovascular evaluation and management** (2014, p. 805) for patients with **coronary artery disease** risk factors undergoing noncardiac procedures.   1. Left main coronary artery
  2. 3-vessel +/- proximal LAD
  3. 2-vessel + proximal LAD
- **PCI vs CABG**: long-term outcome of CABG is better than PCI; CABG remains standard for left main occlusion and multivessel coronary artery disease in diabetics.
- **Extracorporeal perfusion** (the pump) and its 3 adverse effects:
  1. Hemostasis — anticoagulation needed while on pump
  2. Humoral and cellular immune response on pump — inflammatory cells release reactive oxidants (cytotoxic) → cardiovascular effects: vasodilation and hypotension
  3. End-organ function effects — nervous system, respiratory system and kidney
- **Off-pump coronary artery bypass**: bypass without pump while the heart beats (needs a "god-level" surgeon) to avoid cardiopulmonary bypass complications, using an epicardial stabilizing device during coronary anastomosis. Off-pump has lower mortality, stroke and MI than conventional on-pump CABG, but long-term survival is better with on-pump CABG with cardioplegic arrest — perhaps because anastomosis on a still heart is better.
- **Minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass (MIDCAB)**: off-pump bypass via mini-anterolateral thoracotomy, mobilizing the left internal thoracic artery to the left anterior descending artery; suits single vessel disease.
- **Total endoscopic coronary artery bypass (TECAB)** is now possible (from 2012) with robot assistance, but numbers are small due to a long learning curve — as printed, by the time one is good, the surgeon's key organs may have deteriorated (joke).
- Studies now compare minimally invasive direct coronary artery bypass with PCI, to decide who should get PCI and who CABG.
- Valvular disease: follow ACC/AHA guidelines as before; residents need not read unless free.
- Residents year 3: read surgery for pericardial disease and cardiac neoplasm, especially myxoma.

## Thoracic Aneurysms and Aortic Dissection
- Written by the Michael E. DeBakey Department of Cardiovascular Injury (as printed), St. Luke's Medical Center, Houston, Texas — the DeBakey name guarantees quality.
- Anecdote on DeBakey, called the father of cardiovascular surgery; served as a military doctor (rank of colonel) during the world war (photos 1945).
- DeBakey's many famous works: coronary bypass, carotid endarterectomy, artificial heart; he introduced dacron graft for AAA, and later had his own AAA repaired with a dacron graft. He received the (Congressional) gold medal award from President George W. Bush; died aged 99 years 10 months. His name is on surgical instruments, a classification of aortic dissection, a museum, cardiac surgery institutes and more (e.g. Michael E. DeBakey VA Medical Center). The new Schwartz cites him under leadership in surgery.
- **Causes and pathogenesis of aneurysm**: research focuses on molecular mechanisms of aortic wall degeneration and dilatation, especially imbalance of matrix metalloproteinase (a proteolytic enzyme), hoping molecular targeted drugs can rebalance proteolytic enzymes and slow wall degeneration.
- **Familial thoracic aneurysms and aortic dissection**: genes causing aortic wall degeneration are being sought — 10 found so far.
- **Expansion rate**: ascending aortic aneurysm about 0.07 cm/year on average; descending about 0.19 cm/year.
- **Strong predictor of rupture** = diameter (as with AAA):
  1. Ascending aortic aneurysm diameter > 6 cm → rupture chance 31%
  2. Descending aortic aneurysm diameter > 7 cm → rupture chance 43%
- Ascending aortic aneurysm > 5 cm diameter often causes aortic valve annular dilatation with incompetent valve, often needing valve replacement; when replacing the valve, check the ascending aorta — if > 4.5 cm, replace the ascending aorta at the same time.
- **Diagnostic evaluation**: **contrast-enhanced CT** is the most useful study — location, extent, aortic lumen, intraluminal thrombus, aortic calcification, relation to major branch vessels — and guides treatment. Major disadvantage stressed in this edition: contrast-induced acute renal failure; if it occurs, postpone surgery until renal function returns to normal.
- **Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA)**: about as effective as contrast-enhanced CT; advantages — no ionizing contrast exposure, clearer branch-vessel detail; limitations — more expensive, takes longer, unsuitable for very sick patients.
- Indications for surgery in asymptomatic patients still depend on size (residents beware):
  1. General population: treat if ascending aorta > 5.5 cm or descending aorta > 6 cm
  2. Marfan patients: ascending aorta > 5 cm, or "…scending aorta < 6 cm" [unclear] (as printed) → already treat
- Besides Marfan, surgical indications are given for 2 other **connective tissue disorders**: **Loeys-Dietz syndrome** and **Ehlers-Danlos syndrome** — as printed, residents need not know them; it will not make them smarter.
- **Women with Marfan** planning pregnancy: treat a thoracic aneurysm of only 4 cm.
- **Endovascular treatment** is accepted for descending thoracic aneurysm; for ascending and thoracoabdominal it is still experimental. Those who benefit are very old, with many comorbidities, unfit — open repair would mean a 4-day stay in the funeral pavilion (as printed).
- Marfan patients: endovascular repair is not recommended — progressive vessel dilatation causes stent migration and endoleak.
- Surgery is summarized readably; the hard part is aortic arch aneurysm surgery — varied, complex techniques, especially cardiopulmonary bypass perfusion strategies for the brain while the heart is stopped, and brain protection so the patient does not end up a "vegetable" (as printed). Personal opinion: unless going on to a CVT fellowship, just skim.
- **Aortic dissection**: first change concerns classification — long-known DeBakey classification and Stanford classification.
- Figure: Aortic dissection classification — DeBakey I and II = Stanford A (ascending, arch and descending; or ascending only); Stanford B = arch and descending (as drawn); DeBakey IIIa and IIIb = Stanford B (descending; descending penetrating ulcer; iatrogenic).

## Arterial Disease
- 4 authors, 2 of them vascular experts at Michael E. DeBakey Medical Center — quality assured; well written, though little changed from the previous edition; mostly extends intervention results, especially endovascular treatment in AAA and nonatherosclerotic disorders.
- **General approach** to arterial disease: basics suited to medical students and 1st-year residents — history, physical exam, noninvasive diagnostic evaluation: ABI, segmental limb pressure, US, CTA, MRA and diagnostic angiography.
- **Vascular stent**: now the era of drug eluting stents (DES) — nitinol plus anti-inflammatory drug; DES slowly releases drug into the arterial wall it contacts, preventing graft (as printed) stenosis. Besides coronary use, DES is now used in PAD for stenotic lesions.
- **Carotid occlusive disease**
  1. **MRA vs CTA**: the authors favour MRA — 3D MRA in coronal and sagittal planes on newer machines grades stenosis very accurately. If CTA is used, it should be multidetector CTA; data on whether CTA grades stenosis as well as MRA are awaited.
  2. **Treatment**: nothing new; carotid intervention for:
     - 2.1 Symptomatic case with ICA stenosis ≥ 60%
     - 2.2 Asymptomatic case with ICA stenosis ≥ 80%; 60-79% still controversial — personal opinion: follow Rutherford
  3. **Carotid endarterectomy** remains first-line: lower stroke rate and better long-term outcome than stent.
  4. **Stent** is reserved for patients unfit for surgery — anatomic problems such as high carotid bifurcation above C2, previous surgery or radiation to the neck; or medical reasons such as age over [number missing] years (as printed), left ventricular ejection fraction < 30%, unstable angina or recent MI. Table 23-4, p. 912 summarizes indications well.
     - Stent problem: higher stroke risk. Still debated: timing of stenting in acute stroke — latest (retrospective) report: stenting within 2 weeks has higher mortality than after 2 weeks.
- **Carotid body tumor**: tumor of neuroectodermal neural crest origin; mostly benign, malignancy about 5-7%.
  - Usually presents as asymptomatic lateral neck mass.
  - Diagnosis needs CT or MRI — widening of the carotid bifurcation.
  - New: **Shamblin classification** by tumor extent into 3 types; whatever the type, treatment is surgical resection.
  - Figure: Shamblin types I-III, drawn with increasing tumor extent around the internal and external carotid arteries and nerves (hypoglossal, superior laryngeal, vagus).
- **AAA**
  1. EVAR now has lower morbidity and mortality than open repair, so use EVAR when anatomic morphology suits stent graft placement (table 23-9, p. 924).
  2. Much-discussed: EVAR needs a proximal landing zone of 10-15 mm (Schwartz uses > 15 mm). When the neck is too short — suprarenal or juxtarenal AAA — the stent graft attached to the aortic wall covers the renal artery or SMA orifice, affecting blood flow to bowel and kidney. Schwartz recommends the **chimney technique** (p. 923) to allow EVAR, or a **fenestrated graft** with holes matching the renal arteries and SMA so blood reaches kidneys and bowel.
  - Chimney EVAR (CHEVAR) (from figure): in juxta-renal AAA with no infrarenal landing zone, a covered balloon-expandable stent-graft is deployed in the renal arteries and extended proximally into the supra-renal aorta, allowing the aortic stent graft to extend proximally and increase the landing zone. Literature: technical success 91%, peri-operative major morbidity 17%, mortality 5%, early type 1 endoleaks 13% (Vascular Journal, October 2012).
- **Mesenteric occlusive disease**
  1. **Celiac artery compression syndrome**: laparoscopic release of the arcuate ligament compressing the proximal celiac artery gives results no different from open release, so laparoscopic release is now standard.
  2. **Endovascular treatment** suits **chronic mesenteric ischemia**: lower morbidity than open, but long-term results and patency still not as good as open.
- **Renal artery occlusive disease**: suspect in diastolic hypertension > 125 mmHg, rapidly rising BP, or BP hard to control with drugs. Renal artery revascularization if BP is uncontrolled by drugs or renal function worsens, with CTA showing renal artery stenosis > 70%. Surgical repair is still better than endovascular treatment.
- **AIOD and PAD** (from above to below the knee): still use **Trans-Atlantic Inter-Society Consensus (TASC) 2** (since 2007); every standard textbook still uses TASC 2 and the same treatment approach.
  - But beware: vascular experts are preparing TASC 3 based on scientific evidence. The author heard (from a TASC chairman) that anatomical lesions will likely stay 4 types, but treatment modality will change because endovascular methods have developed rapidly with better stents and more skilled vascular surgeons. The next TASC meeting is likely June 2020; if agreed, consensus will be announced in 1-2 years — residents in year 1 now may be the first to use TASC 3 (จำ).
- Updated arterial diseases: **nonatherosclerotic disorders** at the end of the chapter — very rare; this book lists 13 diseases; only updated ones are written here:
  1. **Behcet's disease**: large vessel arteritis; in Middle-Eastern men; new — 36% of patients are Japanese (new to the author). Thought to be due to gene abnormality causing inflammation of mouth, anus (as printed, "จู๋ จิ๋ม" — genitals), eyes and vessels; vascular inflammation usually in large vessels such as aorta and pulmonary artery → aneurysmal dilatation and rupture. Treated with steroid and immunosuppressive drugs.
  2. **Polyarteritis nodosa**: systemic inflammatory process of medium and small-sized arteries → end-organ ischemia. New: 60% have nerve inflammation from ischemia of the nerve, and 50% have GI complications. Treated with steroid and immunosuppressive drugs.
  3. **Raynaud's syndrome**: intermittent vasospastic disorder after exposure to stimuli — cold, smoking, stress. New: suspected cause — on follow-up 11-65% progress to connective tissue disorder. Not curable; treatment = avoid vasoconstricting triggers, vasodilators usually calcium channel blockers. Surgery: debridement of dead tissue and amputation; sympathectomy relieves symptoms in 60-70%, but 60% recur within 10 years.
  4. **Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD)**: vasculopathy of medium-sized arteries, cause unknown; segmental arterial involvement; classic bead appearance (like a string of rosary beads). Subgroups by which layer of the wall is involved; update: commonest subgroup is medial hypoplasia (as printed).
     - Figure: FMD subgroups — intimal fibroplasia (1-2%); medial fibromuscular dysplasia (95% +; focal, multilocal or tubular stenosis, with or without aneurysm and dissection); periadventitial fibrosis (1-2%).
     - 85% in the renal artery, next internal carotid artery; main treatment = balloon angioplasty.
  5. **Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans)**: new is a retrospective report from Mayo Clinic — of young patients with lower limb ischemia, 24% had Buerger's disease; otherwise nothing new.

- **GERD**
  1. Previously esophagitis was graded by severity using the **LA classification** into 4 grades (grade 4 most severe, with stricture, likely to need surgery); the new edition dropped it.
  2. **Barrett esophagus**: how to approach each grade of dysplasia — not discussed.
  3. Treatment: first line still PPI; surgical indications are coarse, unchanged. Slightly better: **laparoscopic anti-reflux** techniques — Nissen, Toupet, Dor, Collis — briefly but clearly explained (the authors are laparoscopic experts).

Pending question from 5 years ago — non-absorbable synthetic mesh or biologic mesh (porcine skin)? The new edition concludes biologic mesh is no better than tissue repair, so the authors favour non-absorbable synthetic mesh.
  - Whether to add an antireflux procedure: most surgeons recommend it, but the authors say if PEH patients had no reflux before surgery, why add antireflux. The author notes: reduction of the hernia requires enlarging the hiatus and possibly cutting the phrenoesophageal membrane, weakening the LES — so he agrees with most surgeons to add antireflux.
- **Scleroderma**: same as before, nothing new.
- **Management of Schatzki's ring**: also same as before:
  1. No acid reflux → dilation
  2. Acid reflux → antireflux procedure
- **Eosinophilic esophagitis (EE)**: same as the last edition; summary (seems promoted a lot):
  - **Cause**: unclear, leaning to autoimmune.
  - **Symptoms**: postprandial chest pain; some present with dyspepsia (as printed), usually with solid food.
  - **Investigation**
    1. Barium swallow: classic ringed esophagus or falcine esophagus — esophagus appears as stacked rings
    2. Esophagoscopy: series of stacked mucosal rings, or ring-like mucosa
    3. Biopsy: eosinophils > 15 per HF, with eosinophils at the base of the epithelium
  - **Treatment**
    1. First test which foods the patient is allergic to; avoid them
    2. Then start steroid
    3. If steroid fails, dilate for dysphagia (printed "dyspepsia") — dilate with great care: the esophagus is friable and perforates easily, which is where surgeons get involved
  - Note: as printed, the author again wonders why this is in Schwartz — seems a medical disease.
- **Esophageal diverticulum**
  1. **Zenker diverticulum**
     - Diagnosis: barium swallow; hard to diagnose by scope.
     - Treatment: endoscopic cricopharyngotomy (transoral endoscopic cricopharyngotomy) seems favoured, especially symptomatic cases with diverticulum ≥ 2 cm.
  2. **Midesophageal diverticulum**: as before. Treatment: surgery only if symptomatic; as with Zenker, diverticulum suspension with myotomy; if motor abnormality, myotomy is required.
  3. **Epiphrenic diverticulum** — treatment:
     - 3.1 Laparoscopic diverticulectomy
     - 3.2 Opposite esophageal myotomy
     - 3.3 Partial fundoplication — Toupet or Dor

  1. **End-staged achalasia**: lap. Heller should be the initial surgical treatment first, but Schwartz 2019 still recommends esophagectomy as before.
  2. Another weakness: no mention of the **Chicago classification** by high resolution manometry (HRM), used worldwide, now Chicago version 3 / 2015. Knowing only Schwartz, one would look clueless before the UGIST society (as printed).

## Esophageal cancer
- **TNM staging**: new 2017 version, 3 groups — **confined to esophagus** (T1-T2), subdivided into very early or intramucosal cancer (T in situ, T1a); **locally advanced** (T1-3 N1); **disseminated** (any T N M1). Actually SCC and adeno have separate staging since their adjuvant treatments differ.
- **Investigation**
  1. CT chest, abdomen and pelvis
  2. EUS in every case if possible
  3. PET scan only when CT suggests metastasis; recommended in locally advanced cancer; not needed for confined lesions
  4. Thoracoscopy and laparoscopy only when:
     - 4.1 T4b suspected
     - 4.2 Nature of enlarged lymph node is uncertain
     - 4.3 PET scan not available
- **Surgical management**
  1. Tis, T1a → EMR
  2. T1B-T2 N0 = confined disease → resection only
  3. T1-T3 N1 = locally advanced disease → neoadjuvant chemo first, then esophagectomy
  4. Any T, any N, M1 = disseminated disease → stent or PDT
  5. Cervical esophageal cancer → stereotactic RT (printed "sterotactic") with concomitant chemotherapy
- **Clinical decision** for curative vs palliative treatment — approach used since 1997 (as printed, "30 years").
- Figure: Clinical decision in esophageal cancer, as steps (each step → palliation if criteria met; otherwise continue) —
  1. Age: palliation if > 75 years.
  2. Physiologic fitness: palliation if FEV1 < 1.25, ejection fraction < 40%.
  3. Clinical staging: palliation if recurrent nerve paralysis, Horner's syndrome, persistent spinal pain, paralysis of diaphragm, fistula formation, malignant pleural effusion, endoscopic tumor length > 9 cm, abnormal esophageal axis, multiple enlarged nodes or distant organ metastasis on CT, more than 20% weight loss, loss of appetite (relative).
  4. Endoscopic ultrasound: palliation if transmural tumors with > 4 enlarged nodes.
  5. Intraoperative staging: palliation if unresectable primary, cavitary spread, distant metastasis, extension through mediastinal wall, multiple gross lymph node metastases, microscopic nodal metastasis at margins of the en bloc dissection.
  6. Otherwise → curative en bloc resection.
  - Palliation group with palliative symptoms (dysphagia, obstruction, pain of ulceration, bleeding, infection, anxiety) → requirements for palliative transhiatal resection: free of distant organ metastases and complete excision of primary tumor possible; otherwise → nonsurgical palliation. (*Could include combined Rx and chemo neoadjuvant therapy prior to resection to increase resectability and potential survival in patients 75 or under.)
- **Palliative treatment** changes: age cut-off from > 75 to > 80 years; clinical staging contraindications — tumor > 9 cm, abnormal esophageal axis, multiple lymph node +ve (> 4 nodes), weight loss > 20%. Now with neoadjuvant chemo RT, these contraindications probably no longer hold as the authors say (their references are old and not updated).
- **Surgical technique**: nothing new; for node dissection the authors seem not to like three-field lymphadenectomy.
- Residents should see the management guideline on p. 1081 — a bit old in places but acceptable. The author dislikes the early-disease approach: no reason to include a lymph-node count, and if tumor goes through the wall or N+, do not operate straight away — give neoadjuvant first.
- Figure: Esophageal cancer management guideline (p. 1081), as steps —
  1. Barium swallow, endoscopy → clinical evaluation → tumor staging (CT chest and abdomen, endoscopic ultrasonography).
  2. Late disease or significant comorbidity (distant organ metastasis; imminent cardiac, pulmonary or hepatic failure) → chemoradiation.
  3. Early disease (tumor suspected not to be through the wall and/or less than 8 lymph nodes involved) → curative en bloc resection.

## Stomach
- Schwartz 2019 changed only the junior author; the mentor is still Dempsey of U. Pennsylvania (old author), a conservative, so new material is scarce; most new content is dull basics, roughly ten topics; a short summary:
  1. **Vagus nerve**: besides acid secretion, motor function, mucosal blood flow and cytoprotection, it also controls appetite and mucosal immunity.
  2. **Gastric acid**: besides inhibiting swallowed pathogens, it helps maintain healthy normal flora.
  3. **Gastric mucosal barrier**: many components — mucous barrier, bicarbonate secretion, prostaglandin, nitric oxide and more — the stomach's mucosal defence.
  4. **Ghrelin**: from P/D1 (printed "P/ DI") endocrine cells in gastric oxyntic glands; stimulates appetite. Removing the stomach lowers ghrelin → less eating → weight loss; sleeve gastrectomy's weight loss is thought partly due to lower ghrelin.
  5. Mechanism of **somatostatin** acting on parietal cells' acid secretion (as printed, "stimulates" acid secretion).
- That's enough basics — 15 pages of them (as printed: the author is about to gag).
- EGD diagnoses gastric symptoms so well that barium upper GI study is looked down on; still, barium is better than EGD for gastric diverticulum, gastric fistula, stricture location, and size or morphology of hiatal hernia.
- **Testing for H. pylori**: the authors seem to favour urea breath test for both initial diagnosis and follow-up.
- **Peptic ulcer disease**: surgical options no different from 2009; indications for surgery in order: bleeding, perforation and obstruction.

  1. Requiring multiple transfusions after two attempts at endoscopic control
  2. Limited availability of blood for transfusion
  3. Lack of availability of therapeutic endoscopist

  5. Concurrent indications for surgery, e.g. perforation or obstruction with bleeding

- Figure: Bleeding peptic ulcer guideline (Schwartz edition 11, p. 1134), as steps —
  1. Hospital admission, bleeding peptic ulcer → resuscitate, continuous IV PPI drip, EGD.

- Operation in bleeding peptic ulcer: one consideration in choosing the procedure is BMI, cut-off 21 — not mentioned in other books. BMI < 21 → do little, just suture to stop the bleeding point; BMI > 21 → a definitive procedure can be done. Reason: thin patients are often malnourished and frail, so do not do much.

  - Add lifelong PPI.

- Figure: Gastric lymphoma guideline (p. 1150), as steps —
  - **Low-grade (indolent) MALT**
    1. Confined to gastric wall, no t(11;18) translocation → H. pylori eradication therapy, re-evaluate at 12 months.
    2. Lymph node involvement, t(11;18) translocation → H. pylori eradication therapy, re-evaluate at 3-6 months.

- **Gastric neuroendocrine tumor**: still 3 types; treatment as before.

  1. Peptic ulcer: read Schwartz + Shackelford
  2. Cancer: read NCCN + the new Japanese guideline as the main course; Schwartz is a side dish

## Surgical Management of Obesity
- This Schwartz promotes obesity surgery too much — longer than the liver chapter. Those planning an MIS fellowship earning from obese patients should read it.
- Severe obesity: over the last 10 years non-surgical treatment (diet control, drugs) made little progress; short- and long-term data show surgery gives better weight loss and corrects metabolic disorders, with very low mortality.
- Chapter author: MIS bariatric surgeon **Anita P. Courcoulas**, professor at U. Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania; currently director of SAGES (laparoscopic surgeons' society; printed "SAGE"), whose data are often cited in GI surgery, especially fundoplication. (Non-clinical anecdotes about the authors omitted.)
- Co-author Philip R. Schauer (Director of the Cleveland Clinic Bariatric and Metabolic Institute) — lead author in the previous edition (as printed, jokes).
- Anita opens the chapter: approach to obese patients for surgery should be shared decision-making between doctor and patient, with the patient fully understanding risk and benefit. In 2014 nearly 600,000 bariatric operations: sleeve gastrectomy (SG) 46%, RYBG 40%, LAGB 7%.

- Institutions doing bariatric surgery need prepared staff and infrastructure: large rooms, large beds, operating tables bearing at least 363 kg, and instruments/scopes large and long enough to reach the abdomen.
- **Operative techniques**: written very well; read when a case comes.
- New points in **surgical management**:
  1. Patients with recent venous thromboembolism (printed "thromboemlism") on anticoagulant: stop drugs 5 days before surgery — off warfarin and switch to subcutaneous LMWH. The previous edition advised a preoperative temporary IVC filter for strong VTE risk factors, removed ~3-6 weeks after surgery; the new edition says do not use a filter — it does not prevent PE and adds morbidity.
  2. Most popular techniques now: **laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG)** and **LRYGB**. BPD and DS, once used for super-obese patients, are now done very rarely (< 1%) — technically hard with too many complications.
  3. Open vs lap: choose lap; studies are comparing lap vs robot.
- This edition promotes **enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) 2016** before, during and after surgery.
- **New/experimental techniques** — investigational bariatric procedures, awaiting results in the last edition:
  1. Endoscopic procedures
     - 1.1 Intragastric balloon placement
     - 1.2 Endoscopic stapled or sutured gastric pouch, e.g. transoral gastroplasty (TOGP)
  2. Duodenojejunal bypass and ileal transposition — both still investigational
  - Current conclusion: these experimental procedures are not great — weight loss only 10%, slightly better than drugs.
- This edition compares common operations using results from Swedish (Europe), Utah, Veteran (USA) groups and more — as said, if not planning to make a living this way, no need to read; tiring.
- **Future important question**: Anita emphasizes **metabolic surgery** — the role of surgery in treating diabetes.

## Small Intestine
- Written by experts from Harvard Medical School, Boston; mentor Prof. Michael J. Zinner, editor-in-chief (printed "edittor-in-chief") of Maingot's Abdominal Operation.
- Advantage of famous authors: credibility for paying readers; downside: experts have little time and must entertain life too (as printed).
- Overall, small bowel in edition 11 is hardly updated — about 5% change.
- **Small bowel obstruction**
  1. The new edition favours CT with water soluble contrast — better sensitivity and specificity than plain film, and better at detecting strangulation; limitation: detecting low-grade or partial SBO.
  2. Lap vs open: lap causes up to 4 times less adhesion; this edition favours lap — faster discharge, fewer overall complications; disadvantages: longer operation, conversion rate 17-33%.
  3. The guideline for adhesive SBO used since editions 9 and 10 changed. The old one split partial vs complete obstruction: partial without signs/symptoms of ischemia → conservative as long as etiology and surgeon allow; complete → conservative about 24 h but not > 48 h, then operate if not improving. - **Prevention of adhesion**: good surgical technique is the best prevention (author agrees). Synthetic hyaluronan barriers placed to stop fibrous adhesions to bowel still lack clear evidence of benefit (as before); a downside found with barriers is higher leakage.
- **Ileus**: new definition. Previously: bowel not working > 5 days after surgery. New — postoperative ileus means:
  1. Nausea/vomiting at least 2 times
  2. Inability to tolerate oral diet over 24 hours; absence of flatus over 24 hours
  3. Abdominal distension
  4. Radiologic confirmation occurring on or after day 4 postoperatively without prior resolution of postoperative ileus
- **Analgesia**: avoid morphine (slows bowel); Schwartz 8 and 9 recommended ketorolac (NSAID) instead, or thoracic epidural anesthesia by anesthetists.
- **Prokinetic agents**: 2009 recommended alvimopan; 2015 said alvimopan not OK, needs more study; 2019 recommends alvimopan again (p. 1235) — as printed, the reader's misfortune.
- **Early Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathway** — protocol reducing postoperative ileus, from before, during and after surgery; new chapter 50 in this Schwartz.

  3. Major bleeding (persistent):
     - Stable → tagged RBC scan → positive: angiography and treatment; negative and patient stable → enteroclysis/enteroscopy/capsule endoscopy (step 2).
     - Unstable → operating room → source uncertain: localize bleeding by serial clamping or intraoperative enteroscopy followed by resection; source of bleeding identified: treat source, e.g. small bowel resection.

## Colon, Rectum and Anus
- New author: Kelli Bullard Dunn (as printed "เจ๊กวาน (Kwaan)") from U. California LA — moderately known, mentoring; the famous previous-edition author David A. Rothenberger left the book.
- **Investigation and imaging** — 4 additions since 2015:
  1. Fecal occult blood testing (FOBT) vs fecal immunohistochemistry testing (FIT): FIT has higher sensitivity and specificity for real blood in stool; any positive result must still go to colonoscopy.
  2. Stool DNA testing used for early detection of colorectal cancer.
  3. Preoperative CEA used for prognosis, though not very specific; new tumor markers such as ornithine decarboxylase are no better than CEA.
  4. Scans for lower GI bleeding: tagged RBC scan — patient must be reasonably stable since it takes at least 4 h, and bleeding rate must be about 0.05-2 cc/minute to detect (as printed).
- **Guideline treatment of colorectal bleeding** still from 1999 — old but usable (p. 1268).
- Figure: Acute colonic bleeding guideline (p. 1268), as steps —
  1. Volume resuscitation plus blood transfusion → NG aspirate.
     - NG aspirate positive → gastroduodenoscopy + endoscopic treatment.
     - NG aspirate negative → proctoscopy (rule out anorectal bleeding) → step 2.
  2. Bleeding stopped or slowed down → elective colonoscopy: positive → endoscopic treatment (rebleeding → segmental resection); negative → observe (rebleeding → see moderate or massive bleeding).
  3. Massive life-threatening bleeding → mesenteric arteriography: positive → vasopressin or emboli (fail → segmental resection); negative → explore, intraoperative endoscopy (positive → segmental resection; negative → total colectomy).
  4. Moderate bleeding continued → either:
     - 99mTc RBC scintigraphy: positive → mesenteric arteriography (positive → vasopressin or emboli, fail → segmental resection; negative → explore, intraoperative endoscopy → segmental resection or total colectomy); negative → explore, intraoperative endoscopy (positive → segmental resection; negative → total colectomy); or
     - Urgent colonoscopy: positive → colonoscopic treatment or explore, segmental resection; negative → explore, intraoperative endoscopy (positive → segmental resection; negative → total colectomy).
- **Treatment of anal incontinence**: new options to tighten the anus, e.g. inserting an RFA probe to burn the anal canal (SECCA procedure), but no long-term follow-up yet.
- The author adds another option: **FENIX system** (magnetic anal sphincter) — titanium beads like a rosary with magnetic cores, placed around the anal sphincter to add pressure, similar to the Linx reflux system around the LES for GERD. As printed: the author has not followed FENIX results; doubts it works — the patient may be bothered when it sticks, and passing a stool bolus may be hard.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease** — new:
  1. As chronic immune dysregulation, infection becomes a problem; a new organism thought related to inflammation and fibrosis — **Veillonella** genus.
  2. Restorative proctocolectomy with IPAA in UC: pouchitis in 30-55%; cause unknown, thought due to fecal stasis in the pouch. Treatment: responds well to metronidazole and ciprofloxacin; frequent/chronic cases → salicylate and corticosteroid enema. Targeted anti-TNFa (printed "TNFa") drugs — infliximab, vedolizumab (printed "vedulizumab"), ustekinumab — are being tried; awaiting results.
- **Diverticular disease** — debated point is **indication for surgery**; as printed, Thai residents have long known what the author calls "new":
  1. Previously sigmoid colectomy after the second episode, or first episode in young patients; 2015 says not OK — complication and recurrence risk is not as high as thought; 2019 data unchanged.
  2. Previously favoured surgery after > 2 episodes; the new edition no longer treats this as an indication.
  3. First episode in immunocompromised host → operate (as before).
  4. Emergency surgery in a sick-looking (unstable) patient or with severe adhesive inflammation: previously open surgery + proximal diversion and drainage without resection to reduce mortality and morbidity; if doing only that, laparoscopic lavage + local drainage without bowel resection is favoured.
- **Bowel preparation**: RCTs in the last edition said mechanical prep does not prevent SSI; 2019 cites SCIP-1 — without mechanical bowel prep, oral antibiotic (drug unspecified) reduces postoperative wound infection.
- **Polyp**: more colonoscopy → about 130,000 new cases/year.
- **Polypectomy of sessile polyps** challenges endoscopists; techniques include saline lift and piecemeal snare excision. Piecemeal removal makes depth of invasion hard to interpret; tattoo the site of sessile polyp removal to guide later surgery.
- **FAP**: after surgery desmoid tumor may occur; tamoxifen and NSAIDs give mixed results. The new Schwartz favours GIST drugs — imatinib — citing a recent report at the 2017 sarcoma meeting in Germany that it works.
- **HNPCC**: colon surgery as before = total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis. When planning colectomy in a woman who has completed her family, do prophylactic hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy at the same time.
- **Peutz-Jeghers syndrome** (printed "Peuta-Jeghers"): residents year 3, review the features — pigmented black spots on the lips, family history of colorectal cancer, and colicky pain like SBO from hamartomatous polyps acting as lead points for intussusception.
- **Screening guidelines for colorectal cancer**: p. 1295 summarizes the 2013 guideline, usable; now the 2017 version — see the Oncology chapter, read with NCCN.
- Screening by method: FOBT/FIT yearly; stool DNA should be combined with FIT, every 3 years (NCCN).
- **Cancer of colon & rectum** — mostly as before; new:
  1. **TNM staging** edition 8 / 2017 — similar to 7, T and N as before
  2. **Pathogenesis of colorectal cancer** emphasized too much, dull
  3. Low rectal cancer surgery we call ultralow anterior resection; Schwartz calls it extended low anterior resection
  4. **Abdominoperineal resection**: when resecting from below, as the rectum is freed from the levator ani, avoid entering the space between tumor and levator ani = extralevator APR (ELAPE; the chapter author abbreviates APE). Suits only low locally advanced rectal cancer; not routine for all rectal cancer since it does not improve cancer outcome.
- **Treatment of colon and rectal cancer**: residents read p. 1296-1302 — treatment by stage summarized well; remember Schwartz's recommendations, then attend the colorectal society conference to get it easily.
- **Guideline management for rectal cancer**: unchanged but well summarized (p. 1300); compare with colon cancer treatment.
- Figure: Rectal adenocarcinoma guideline (p. 1300), as steps —
  1. Staging evaluation: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, endorectal U/S, +/- MRI, colonoscopy/CT colonography (if possible).
  2. Stage I (T1-2, N0, M0): high risk or refuses radical resection → transanal excision +/- neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemoradiation; low risk and accepts radical resection → radical resection.
  3. Stage II (T3-4, N0, M0) and stage III (Tany, N1-3, M0) → neoadjuvant chemoradiation → restage → no metastases → radical resection.
  4. Stage IV (Tany, Nany, M1): asymptomatic → chemotherapy → restage → unresectable/multiple metastatic sites: continue chemotherapy; resectable/single metastatic site: consider resection. Symptomatic → chemoradiation vs palliative procedure (stent, laser ablation, stoma, resection).
- **MIS for resection of colon cancer**: early results poor — frequent port site recurrence, doubts about oncologic completeness; with more skilled surgeons and many studies (COST study group, COLOR study group, CLASICC trial (printed "CLASSICC"), ACOSOG Z 6051, AlaCaRT trial, COREAN trial, ROLARR trial; p. 1302-1303), conclusion:
  1. **Colon cancer** in well-trained hands: recurrence, survival and quality of life make laparoscopic colectomy appropriate; laparoscopy with hand-assisted laparoscopy (HAL) gives results as good as open with less pain, faster bowel recovery and better short-term quality of life.
  2. **Rectal cancer**: role of laparoscopy still debated — for TME, lap had higher +ve CRM and more inadequate TME than open, though not statistically significant; still uneasy. To reduce this, robots are being used for pelvic and rectal resection; early studies show robot resection about as good as laparoscopic HAL resection; with more experienced surgeons, robot vs open comparison will follow (wait 5 years for Schwartz edition 12).
- **Rare neoplasms**: 2 tumors updated — lipoma and lymphoma.
- **Lipoma**: small asymptomatic → leave; indications for colonoscopic or colotomy resection: complications such as bleeding, obstruction, intussusception, size > 2 cm.
- **Lymphoma** of colon and rectum: usually bleeding, obstruction, abdominal pain; hard to distinguish from adenocarcinoma; oncologic resection plus adjuvant chemotherapy recommended.
- **Rectorectal or presacral tumor** — as before:
  1. Most common = chordoma, a malignant tumor from the notochord
  2. Previously no biopsy for fear of infection and needle tract seeding; now biopsy of solid lesions shows seeding is not as frequent as feared, and knowing tumor type beforehand allows neoadjuvant treatment to shrink it before surgery. Cystic lesions should not be biopsied — if meningocele, serious infection may follow.
- **Anal cancer**: nothing new, brief but well organized; read more in NCCN and Cameron, especially anal intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN), rather complicated.
- **Hemorrhoid, fissure, FIA**: as before, for residents year 1. Schwartz 2019 accepts LIFT as a procedure for FIA — early report good but lacking long-term follow-up; also cites 3 papers without mentioning อ.อรุณ (the originator, from Chula).

## Liver
- Schwartz 2019 liver chapter is by the same group (not internationally famous); no new data from the past 3-5 years; many points written are probably no longer correct, e.g. serology in ALA, drainage of PLA, treatment of simple cyst.
- New: the word liver comes from Greek "hepaomai" meaning repairable (as printed, a sigh).
- **Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)**: how it causes cirrhosis and then cancer (as printed, a sigh).
- **Evaluation of incidental liver mass** found on imaging (e.g. US for suspected GS): history, physical exam, labs, etc. — like teaching medical students.
- Figure: Incidental liver mass evaluation, as steps —
  1. Mass identified incidentally by US or CT.
  2. History and physical exam: abdominal pain/weight loss; liver disease/cirrhosis/alcohol use; hepatitis/blood transfusion/tattoos; OCP/hormone use/cancer history; jaundice/scleral icterus; palpable mass/hepatomegaly; stigmata of portal HTN.
  3. Laboratory tests: CBC, platelet count; lytes/BUN/creat/glu/albumin; liver function tests/ammonia; coagulation studies; hepatitis screen; tumor markers (CEA, AFP, CA 19-9).
  4. Additional imaging studies: US, CT or MRI scan, nuclear med., angiogram.
  5. Liver biopsy if needed (percutaneous or laparoscopic) → occult primary evaluation: EGD, colonoscopy, mammogram, Gyn/PAP smear.
  6. Diagnosis.
- Not mentioned in Schwartz but readers want to know: **screening and surveillance program for HCC** from the American Association for the Study of Liver Disease (AASLD), latest version 2018 — practice guidelines cited by most texts (except Schwartz). Criteria: adults with cirrhosis at highest HCC risk should enter screening and surveillance to be cost-effective:
  1. **Hepatitis B carrier**
     - 1.1 With cirrhosis
     - 1.2 Asian male age ≥ 40 years; Asian female ≥ 50 years
     - 1.3 African age > 20 years
     - 1.4 Family history of HCC
  2. **Hepatitis C** with cirrhosis or bridging fibrosis
  3. **Cirrhosis** due to:
     - 3.1 Alcohol
     - 3.2 Hemochromatosis
  4. **Surveillance benefit uncertain**
     - 4.1 Hepatitis carriers aged < 40 (men) or < 50 (women)
     - 4.2 Hepatitis C and stage 3 fibrosis; NAFLD without cirrhosis
  5. No surveillance in Child C cirrhosis not on a transplant waiting list
- **AASLD 2018**: screening & surveillance by US alone every 6-12 months; AFP optional — AFP in cirrhosis has low sensitivity and specificity.
- AASLD is researching (phase 3, 4) new biomarkers to replace AFP. Screening by CT and MRI is too expensive — use only when US is inconclusive. When US finds a mass > 1 cm, proceed to diagnostic study; overall AASLD has not changed much.
- Figure: AASLD algorithm for a mass on surveillance ultrasound in a cirrhotic liver or with chronic HBV, as steps —
  1. < 1 cm → repeat US at 3-month intervals → stable over 18-24 months: return to standard surveillance; enlarging: proceed according to lesion size.
  2. > 1 cm → 4-phase MDCT/dynamic MR → arterial hypervascularization and venous or delayed-phase washout?
     - Positive → treat as hepatocellular carcinoma.
     - Negative → other imaging modality (CT or MRI) → arterial hypervascularization and washout? Positive → treat as HCC; negative → biopsy.

## Hepatocellular Carcinoma
- New: HCC is now the 3rd cause of cancer death worldwide.
- In America more than 7,000 liver transplants per year; at the time of writing, 14,463 people waiting. Indications for liver transplant in cancer still follow Milan criteria, though many groups have expanded them; even the Milan group expanded its indication in 2009:
  1. **Rule of 7**: size of the largest tumor (cm) + number of tumors must total ≤ 7. E.g. largest 6 cm (beyond old Milan — not done before) plus one more 1 cm tumor = 7 → transplant possible.
  2. No microvascular invasion
  3. No metastasis
  4. Patient fit to tolerate surgery
- Schwartz edition 11 on **follow-up of RFA** in HCC: 10-year survival about 23.4%.
- **Guideline for management of HCC**: same guideline since edition 9 (blue background), recoloured in edition 10.
- Figure: HCC management guideline, as steps —
  1. HCC identified → resection candidate? transplant candidate?
  2. Resection: non-cirrhotic/Child's A, single lesion, no metastasis.
  3. OLTx evaluation: 1 lesion ≤ 5 cm or 3 lesions ≤ 3 cm, Child's A/B/C, no gross vascular invasion, no metastasis → LDLT? (suitable donor) → OLTx; or UNOS list (cadaver) → MELD score (wait > 3 mos?) → No: OLTx; Yes: neoadjuvant therapy (RFA/TACE/90 Yttrium) → OLTx.
  4. Not Tx candidate (co-morbid factors, ≥ 4 lesions, gross vascular invasion, LN (+) or metastasis) → sorafenib; perc/lap RFA (single lesion < 5 cm, Child's A/B); TACE/90 Yttrium (multi-focal > 5 cm, Child's A/B/C, bili < 3); clinical trials; palliative care (Child's C, bili ≥ 3); new agents.
- In brief: HCC without cirrhosis, or cirrhosis Child A without portal hypertension → resection; otherwise the authors favour OLT.
- Most people use Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging, but Schwartz mentions it in only ~5 lines, and only in relation to liver transplantation.
- **BCLC staging** is now version 2018:
- Figure: BCLC 2018, as steps —
  1. Very early stage (0): single ≤ 2 cm, preserved liver function, ECOG PS 0 → potential candidate for liver transplantation? No → ablation; Yes → portal pressure/bilirubin (as for solitary early stage).
  2. Early stage (A): single or up to 3 nodules ≤ 3 cm, preserved liver function, ECOG PS 0 →
     - Solitary → portal pressure, bilirubin: normal → resection; increased → associated diseases?
     - Up to 3 nodules (≤ 3 cm) → associated diseases?
     - Associated diseases: No → transplantation; Yes → ablation.
  3. Intermediate stage (B): multinodular, preserved liver function, ECOG PS 0 → chemoembolisation.
  4. Advanced stage (C): portal invasion, extrahepatic spread, preserved liver function, ECOG PS 1-2 → systemic therapy.
  5. Terminal stage (D): end-stage liver function, ECOG PS 3-4 → best supportive care.
  - Survival: stages 0-A (ablation/resection/transplantation) > 5 years; B > 2-5 years; C > 1 year; D 3 months. Treatments for 0-C = effective treatments with impact on survival.
- Contentious points are BCLC-B and BCLC-C, where the BCLC guideline does not operate: BCLC-B → chemoembolization; BCLC-C → systemic treatment, no longer specifying sorafenib (printed "soarafenib") as before.
- Some surgeons disagree for BCLC-B and C, since they see many large tumors; operating on these patients gave better survival than TACE, so groups formed their own treatment approaches for BCLC-B and C — the most influential likely the Hong Kong group, which dropped BCLC and issued its own guideline.
- Patients who survive surgery have better survival and live longer — most people accept that; the frequent question is whether operating on BCLC-B and C carries high operative mortality.
- Large reports totalling about 2,600 patients: mean mortality only 1.1-3.1% (very low); especially Torzilli G. et al., with 1,034 BCLC B/C cases and mortality only 3% — so they issued a guideline specifically for BCLC B/C, included in Maingot edition 13 (where Schwartz himself was once editor-in-chief), adding credibility.
- Figure: Maingot 13th ed algorithm for HCC BCLC B/C, as steps —
  1. HCC intermediate stage (B), large/multinodular, PS 0 → Child-Pugh: A → resection; B → stage B → chemoembolization.
  2. HCC advanced stage (C):
     - TT +, Child-Pugh A, PS-0 → resection? Yes → resect; No → stage B (chemoembolization) or stage C (medical treatment).
     - M1, N1, Child-Pugh B, PS 1-2 → medical treatment.
- **Metastatic liver tumors** in the new Schwartz: if many tumors, bilobar, they often cannot all be removed at once because FLR is insufficient; 2 methods to increase remaining liver:
  1. **Two-stage hepatectomy**: first non-anatomical resection in the left lobe, then PVE on the right, so the remaining left liver hypertrophies; second operation = major right hepatectomy.
  2. **Classic Associating Liver Partition and Portal Vein Ligation for Staged Hepatectomy (ALPPS)**: increases FLR faster than PVE; started 2012. Takes only 7-10 days before resection, but the patient still undergoes 2 operations, like two-stage hepatectomy.
     - First operation: ligate the PV on the tumor side to divert portal flow to the remaining liver, plus split the liver along the umbilical fissure; after about 10 days reassess remaining liver volume; if enough, resect the tumor side (figure: ALPPS steps — first step; after 1-2 weeks; second step).
  - New in Schwartz, but Thai HBP surgeons have discussed ALPPS for years; reports show ALPPS morbidity and mortality higher than staged hepatectomy — not impressive to the author.
- Overall, in the last 3 years there is much new on the liver, especially liver tumors: molecular subclasses of hepatic adenoma; approach to incidental liver mass from AASLD 2018; role of imaging with LI-RADS categories (mimicking BI-RADS of breast and TI-RADS of thyroid); controversies on BCLC-B/C; and the future of surgery for portal hypertension — whether surgeons will still do shunts.
- Author's view: this chapter suits residents year 1; for years 2, 3, 4 knowing only this would be shaky at exams.

| Strength | Predictors |
|---|---|

|---|---|

| Presence of both strong predictors | High |
| No predictor present | Low |

|---|---|---|

| Intermediate | Abnormal LFTs, or age > 55, or dilated common bile duct (> 6 mm with GB in situ) | EUS, MRCP, laparoscopic IOC, or intraoperative US |

## Pancreas
- Same team, overseen by Brunicardi (editor-in-chief). Pancreas is well written in Schwartz 2019 — easy, not complicated — but over-emphasizes grassroots basics; important topics often asked by teachers in conference are thin, e.g. management of infected pancreatic necrosis; management of branch duct IPMN not updated; postoperative complications such as fistula and bleeding, and the growing role of MIS, are coarse.
- Advice: read the author's summary first, then Shackelford and Cameron, finishing with Schwartz.
- **Acute pancreatitis (AP)** — 10 new points; read table 33-10, p. 1449, which summarizes AP treatment very well:
  1. Alcohol causes 25-30% of AP; mechanism thought to be reduced pancreatic blood flow. 2019: alcohol stimulates pancreatic stellate cells to produce proinflammatory mediators and cytokines → pancreatitis.
  2. Severity classification still Revised Atlanta Classification of AP 2013; there is also an effort to promote determinant based classification (DBC) 2012 (as printed: DBC is no good).
  3. Several other severity scores:
     - 3.1 **BISAP score** is shown in Schwartz — no need to get excited; it is old, from Cameron; BISAP is no better than old criteria.
     - 3.2 Assess systemic response with **SOFA score** to predict organ failure more accurately: organ failure in at least 2 organs → score ≥ 2 = very severe AP. The SOFA part (p. 1444) is dull; residents should know most people use the Marshall score.
  4. CT scan in diagnosis: only to confirm when unsure whether it is AP.
  5. **Indications for pancreatic protocol CT scan** (rarely in first week):
     - 5.1 Significant clinical deterioration and elevated CRP
     - 5.2 Suspicion of local pancreatic complications
     - 5.3 Suspected bowel ischemia
     - 5.4 Acute bleeding → CTA to find the bleeding point; if found → angiographic embolization
     - 5.5 Suspected abdominal compartment syndrome
  6. **Fluid resuscitation** in AP: LRS reduces SIR(S) better than NSS in the first 24 h.
  7. **Nutrition**: start enteral feeding within 24 h once initial resuscitation is done; if after 48-72 h the patient still cannot eat by mouth and has no gastric stasis → NG feeding; if NG not possible → NJ; increase enteral volume gradually over 2-3 days.
  8. **Early ERCP**: no benefit unless cholangitis or impacted stone.
  9. **Antibiotics** in AP do not reduce pancreatic infection complications, risk of surgical intervention or death.
  10. **Intervention** for infected pancreatic necrosis: they favour the step up approach; if it fails → open necrosectomy.

## International Consensus Guideline 2017 for BD-IPMN
- The 2012 consensus was revised at Fukuoka 2017 with minor changes.
- Basics: MD-IPMN and mixed type IPMN have malignancy rates of 67% and 45% respectively → recommend resection for both types.
- BD-IPMN malignancy about 20% (less than main duct type); do not rush to surgery — patients are often elderly with many risks. Before operating, image with pancreatic protocol CT or Gd-enhanced MRI/MRCP: size and character of the lesion, presence of nodule, and whether CT shows high risk stigmata (likely cancer) or only worrisome features (Fukuoka guideline 2017).
- Figure: Fukuoka 2017 BD-IPMN algorithm, as steps —
  1. High-risk stigmata? (i) obstructive jaundice with cystic lesion of the pancreatic head; (ii) enhancing mural nodule ≥ 5 mm; (iii) main pancreatic duct ≥ 10 mm. — Yes → consider surgery if clinically appropriate. No → step 2.
  2. Worrisome features? Clinical: pancreatitis. Imaging: (i) cyst ≥ 3 cm; (ii) enhancing mural nodule < 5 mm; (iii) thickened/enhancing cyst walls; (iv) main duct size 5-9 mm; (v) abrupt change in caliber of pancreatic duct with distal pancreatic atrophy; (vi) lymphadenopathy; (vii) increased serum level of CA19-9; (viii) cyst growth rate ≥ 5 mm / 2 years. — If yes → EUS → step 3. No → step 4.
  3. EUS: (i) definite mural nodule(s) ≥ 5 mm; (ii) main duct features suspicious for involvement; (iii) cytology suspicious or positive for malignancy. — Yes → consider surgery. No or inconclusive → step 4.
  4. Size of largest cyst: < 1 cm → CT/MRI in 6 months, then every 2 years if no change; 1-2 cm → CT/MRI 6 months × 1 year, yearly × 2 years, then lengthen interval up to 2 years if no change; 2-3 cm → EUS in 3-6 months, then lengthen interval up to 1 year, alternating MRI with EUS as appropriate; consider surgery in young, fit patients with need for prolonged surveillance; > 3 cm → close surveillance alternating MRI with EUS every 3-6 months; strongly consider surgery in young, fit patients.
- Explanation:
  1. **High risk stigmata** (HRS, Fukuoka 2017) — high cancer risk:
     - 1.1 Obstructive jaundice from a mass at the pancreatic head (simply: jaundice → operate)
     - 1.2 Enhancing mural nodule ≥ 5 mm (as printed "> 5 mm") in the cyst (2012 gave no nodule size)
     - 1.3 Main PD size ≥ 1 cm
     - **Treatment**: surgical resection with free margin — head lesion → PD; body & tail lesion → distal pancreatectomy.
  2. No HRS → more detailed imaging for worrisome features. **Worrisome feature (WF) criteria** (as printed: 9 items):
     - 2.1 Cystic tumor size ≥ 3 cm
     - 2.2 Thickened enhancing cyst wall
     - 2.3 Main PD 5-9 mm
     - 2.4 Enhancing mural nodule < 5 mm in the cyst (2012 gave no size)
     - 2.5 Abrupt change in caliber of PD with distal pancreatic atrophy
     - 2.6 Lymphadenopathy
     - 2.7 Clinical pancreatitis
     - 2.8 New: raised serum CEA (level not stated) (as printed; figure says CA19-9)
     - 2.9 New: growth rate > 5 mm per 2 years
     - With worrisome features → Doppler EUS to see the blood supply of a mural nodule ± FNA; accuracy > 95%.
  3. On EUS, any one of these → operate:
     - 3.1 Definite mural nodule ≥ 5 mm
     - 3.2 Main PD involvement present or suspected, e.g. thick cyst wall, nodule or mucin in the duct
     - 3.3 FNA of cyst wall shows cancer cells, or suspicious cells → operate
     - **Mural nodule features on EUS**
       1. Nodule does not move with position change
       2. Doppler flow in the nodule
       3. FNA yields tumor
  4. EUS of BD-IPMN without the features in item 3 — **treatment** by size of the largest cyst:
     - 4.1 Size < 1 cm → CT or MRI at 6 months; if unchanged, repeat CT/MRI every 2 years
     - 4.2 Size 1-2 cm (often asked) → CT or MRI every 6 months in the first year; if unchanged, yearly for 2 years; if still unchanged, CT/MRI every 2 years
     - 4.3 Size 2-3 cm — 2 options:
       - 4.3.1 Elderly (> 65 years) → EUS with MRI every 3-6 months for 1 year; if changed, consider surgery
       - 4.3.2 Young, fit, not wanting long follow-up → consider surgery
     - 4.4 Size > 3 cm — 2 options:
       - 4.4.1 Elderly → lifelong surveillance with MRI plus EUS every 3-6 months; if changed, consider surgery
       - 4.4.2 Young → strongly consider surgery rather than long-term follow-up
  5. EUS of BD-IPMN with item-3 features only borderline (= inconclusive) → follow item 4.4.
- Summary: the Pancreas chapter in Schwartz 2019 is usable; to excel, read Shackelford too.

## Spleen
- Formerly written by Schwartz himself, a true master; now main authors are Eduardo M. Targarona and Carlos Rodrigues of Barcelona, Spain, plus a newcomer in America — as printed, the author does not know them (only knows Barcelona for Messi).
- Blood diseases needing splenectomy are things we already know; for residents, read (selectively) treatment and role of splenectomy in: hereditary spherocytosis (best result of splenectomy); G-6-PD (splenectomy has no role); thalassemia (splenectomy when indicated); staging laparotomy for Hodgkin's lymphoma (no longer done); splenic abscess (best treatment is splenectomy).
- **ITP**
  1. First line still oral prednisolone; if platelets < 5,000 or internal bleeding → add IV immunoglobulin for about 2-3 days
  2. New drugs for ITP: rituximab and thrombopoietin (printed "thrombopoitin") receptor agonist
  3. Refractory ITP → splenectomy
- **Hairy cell leukemia (HCL)**: previously splenomegaly with hypersplenism → splenectomy; since rituximab, pentostatin, cladribine (printed "cladripine") are very effective, splenectomy in HCL is now rare.
- **G-6-PD**: main treatment is avoiding drugs or substances predisposing to hemolysis. The new edition reports 6 G6PD patients with severe hemolysis who benefited from splenectomy, yet concludes overall that splenectomy has no role in this disease (as printed, the author asks why tell the 6 cases).
- **Staging laparotomy in Hodgkin's lymphoma**: since CT and PET imaging, rarely used (Shackelford: "rarely used today"). Remaining indications:
  1. Clinical suspicion of lymphoma without evidence of peripheral disease
  2. Patient requiring restaging for failure after chemotherapy
- **Splenic cancer** (p. 1539): new — a Taiwanese paper found splenectomized patients, besides OPSI risk, have higher future cancer risk in several organs: esophagus, stomach, liver, head and neck, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma and leukemia. Exact mechanism remains unclear; hypothesized impaired immune defence (as printed, a sigh).
- **OPSI**: nothing new; as before, carry antibiotics and take them at once with fever or sore throat.
- **Vaccination**: new 2017 recommendation — H. flu (printed "H.flu") vaccine yearly (as printed), pneumococcal vaccine with revaccination every 5 years.

## Soft Tissue Sarcomas
- Only one author changed — an expert from Florida; mentor still the master Raphael E. Pollock, on the editorial board; credible names, but let us see how updated the content is.
- **Incidence**: cites the American Cancer Society, August 2018 — about 11,280 new soft tissue sarcoma cases per year and 3,900 deaths per year; oddly the numbers match the 2007 report (as printed).
- **Radiation induced sarcoma**: new; commonest types osteogenic sarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, angiosarcoma and lymphangiosarcoma. Lymphangiosarcoma occurs in patients with breast cancer or lymphoma who had RT; radiation induced sarcoma is aggressive, recurs fast, dies fast.
- **Molecular pathogenesis**: many oncogenes related to carcinogenesis found, aiming to explain causation and find targeted therapy.
- **Imaging studies**: CT and MRI roles as before; 2019 does not recommend PET/CT for initial staging, but uses PET-CT to detect metastasis and assess chemotherapy response.
- **Staging**: AJCC edition 8, revised — the old TNM was one system for all sites; the new one changes:
  1. Separate TNM by site — head neck one, abdomen another, extremities another…
  2. Slightly better: old T staging classified Ta superficial lesion (above superficial fascia) and Tb deep lesion (below superficial fascia, or in mediastinum, abdomen or retroperitoneum); now depth does not matter — only T, no Ta and Tb.
  3. Sarcoma usually does not go to nodes; N +ve means very advanced — old system stage 3; new system all stage 4.
- **Surgery for soft tissue sarcomas**: margin at least 1-2 cm; isolated limb perfusion with TNF alpha + interferon + melphalan (printed "melphalan") increases limb salvage up to 82%. Read table 36-3, p. 1575 a bit to know when to give adjuvant RT; in brief, year-3 residents should know:
  1. No RT if pathology is low grade and T stage only T1 (size ≤ 5 cm)
  2. RT if pathology is intermediate or high grade and T stage T2 (size > 5 cm)
- **Brachytherapy** with implanted iridium radioactive substance vs external radiation: implants improve local control, faster wound healing, less radiation-associated toxicity.
- **New chemotherapy** promoted: trabectedin and palifosfamide for advanced soft tissue sarcoma, called novel chemotherapeutic agents — as printed, the same drugs written in the last edition; the author sees nothing exciting.
- **Targeted therapy** accepted as beneficial in advanced soft tissue sarcoma: pazopanib (printed "pazopinib"); new drug in phase 1 study: olaratumab.
- **Post-treatment surveillance**: follow NCCN guideline.
- Many sarcoma types are written; residents must know GIST — but disappointing: little on immunohistochemistry (printed "immuohistochwmistry") to separate GIST from other sarcomas, or treatment of GIST by organ. Knowing only this, residents at conference or oral exam will be "อาจารย์ตื๊บจนไส้แตก" (beaten badly by examiners).
- Overall acceptable, but download NCCN soft tissue sarcoma and GIST for more.

## Inguinal Hernias
- Mentor: editor-in-chief Brunicardi — expected to be top-tier, but the real writers were swapped; only 27 pages, with 7 writers including the mentor; as printed, the writers all appear to be his former and current juniors.
- 75% of **abdominal wall hernia** is in the groin; now almost 40% is ambulatory surgery; even high-risk patients can be repaired under local anesthesia.
- Lifetime risk of groin hernia: men 27%, women only 3%.
- **Pathophysiology** of indirect inguinal hernia (printed "indirect indirect"): thought due to patent processus vaginalis (PPV); for adults, PPV is likely to become IIH mostly in infancy rather than other ages.
- **Imaging adjunct to history & physical examination**: nothing new:
  1. US: sensitivity 86%, specificity 77%, seeing movement of content through the inguinal canal
  2. CT: sensitivity 80%, specificity 65% — oddly CT cannot beat US
  3. MRI: highest sensitivity and specificity, up to 95%; problem: very expensive, unsuitable for routine use
- **Treatment** as before:
  1. **Elective repair**: 3 approaches — open, laparoscopic, and the rising robotic approach (costly and longer operation)
  2. **Indications for laparoscopic inguinal repair** as for open repair; most surgeons agree bilateral, recurrent inguinal hernia, or repair together with prostatectomy is better laparoscopic than open. Contraindications to lap: unfit for anesthesia, cannot insufflate the abdomen, large incarcerated inguinal hernia
  3. **Robot-assisted inguinal hernia repair**: suits recurrent hernia, like lap repair
  4. **Preoperative antibiotic**: this edition still has no conclusion whether it must be given; other textbooks lean to not giving
  5. **Prosthesis**: current conclusion — use light weight mesh; compared with heavy weight mesh, recurrence is no higher, with much less postoperative pain
  6. **Biologic mesh**: reserve for contamination; disadvantages — rapid loss of tissue strength and recurrence
  7. **Fixation technique** (suture, stapling or tacking) causes chronic pain; fibrin glue to fix mesh reduces pain, but costly and lacking long-term outcome
- **Nonoperative strategy** (no surgery) is safe but only for minimally symptomatic inguinal hernia.
- **In summary**: the only really new thing is robot-assisted inguinal hernia repair; knowledge for residents is just right — pass.

## Understanding, Evaluating, and Using Evidence for Surgical Practice
- Modern surgeons must use **evidence-based** practice in patient care, because surgical knowledge changes constantly; new knowledge should come from evidence-based data.
- Data used must not be biased, especially cognitive bias (from one person's experience); no pre-set results; data from standard research processes. Data types are ranked as a pyramid — RCT most credible; lowest (floor) is expert experience/opinion, from the guesses of old people (as printed).
- Figure: Evidence pyramid (Schwartz 11e p. 2140), top to bottom — RCT; cohort study; case control study; case series; case reports; animal research; in-vitro research; expert experience/opinion.
- Ratings of data then guide use, by tiers; different groups rate the same topic differently — e.g. VTE prevention in hip or knee surgery: American Association of Orthopedic Surgeon (AAOS) rates it no higher than level B or level 2, mostly level C or level 3, whereas American College of Chest Physician (ACCP) rates all of it level A or level 1.
- To standardize ratings, the writers favour **Oxford Center for Evidence Based Medicine (CEBM)** (p. 2141), 5 levels:

| Is this diagnostic or monitoring test accurate? (Diagnosis) | Systematic review of cross-sectional studies with consistently applied reference standard and blinding | Individual cross-sectional studies with consistently applied reference standard and blinding | Nonconsecutive studies, or studies without consistently applied reference standards | Case-control studies, or poor or nonindependent reference standard | Mechanism-based reasoning |
| What will happen if we do not add a therapy? (Prognosis) | Systematic review of inception cohort studies | Inception cohort studies | Cohort study or control arm of randomized trial | Case-series or case-control studies, or poor-quality prognostic cohort study | NA |
| Does this intervention help? | What are the common harms? ## Skills and Simulation
- Surgical skill training used to be in the OR; now surgery is more complex with new technology such as MIS or robot — senior surgeons' science; staff and fellows do it all themselves, so residents operate less; residents' work is mostly "secretarial" (keeping cases, sending set lists, chasing labs, summarizing charts, finding beds) — when they finally operate, they lack confidence, never having been "first hand" (only holding the camera). This is where this topic starts.
- Early training practised on pigs, dogs or seniors (as printed); now skill training is in a skill lab.
- First-generation skill labs were basic: excising skin lesions, inserting T-tubes, practising abdominal wall closure — learning skills before the OR. Now skill labs must meet requirements: defined components, standards checks and accreditation from several bodies, so residents passing the lab are skilled and confident enough before operating on real patients.
- A good skill lab teaches, assesses and has a system to check whether those who pass are expert enough for real patients. The writers recommend the **Dreyfus model** — stepwise from beginner to expert (stepwise skill development):

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| Novice | Rigid adherence to taught rules or plans; no exercise of discretionary judgement |
| Advanced beginner | Limited situational perception; all aspects of work treated separately with equal importance |
| Competent | Coping with crowdedness (multiple activities, information); some perception of actions in relation to goals; deliberate planning; formulates routines |
| Proficient | Holistic view of situation; prioritizes importance of aspects; perceives deviations from the normal pattern; employs maxims for guidance, with meanings that adapt to the situation at hand |
| Expert | Transcends reliance on rules, guidelines and maxims; intuitive grasp of situations based on deep understanding; has a vision of what is possible; uses an analytical approach in new situations |

- **Training basic surgical skills**: simulation lab to train open skill, laparoscopic skill and flexible endoscopic skill — many exercises from simple (urinary catheter, knot tying, suturing) to skin graft, chest tube, central line, surgical biopsy, bowel anastomosis by hand or stapler, vessel ligation and anastomosis; for lap, basic and advanced laparoscopy skills.
- **Open surgical skills**: models from simple abdominal closure to vascular and bowel anastomosis.
- **Laparoscopic skills**: videoscopic skill boxes, two-handed — grasp, cut, clip, suture; when fluent, move to animals for tissue feel. RCTs comparing simulator-trained vs untrained: in LC, Nissen fundoplication, colectomy and TEP inguinal hernia repair, trained residents are faster with fewer errors (figure: laparoscopic virtual simulators).
- **Vascular intervention**: angioplasty models; simulators with fluoroscopy images for residents and fellows to practise catheter, balloon and stent placement (figure: EVAR simulator).
- **Flexible endoscopy**: models to practise colonoscopy (colonoscopic simulator).
- **Robotic simulator**: practise inserting instruments into the abdomen and controlling mechanical arms — tying, cutting, dissecting — on simulator models, then on a console like the real robot (figures: robotic simulator console; simulated abdominal robot; simulated vascular robot).
- **American Board of Surgery** added requirements from 2010: every resident taking the board must pass training and be certified in laparoscopic simulator training; latest in 2018, also certified in endoscopic simulator training — institutions providing simulator training must pass standards inspection by a committee appointed by ACS program directors.
- Overall, this chapter likely signals future Thai resident training: rotating to institutions with simulator labs, and board candidates having to pass simulator training.
