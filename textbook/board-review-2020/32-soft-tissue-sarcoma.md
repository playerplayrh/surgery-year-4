# Soft Tissue Sarcoma 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 696-718 (PDF pages 1793-1815)

## Introduction
- Soft tissue sarcoma is a rare cancer; apart from GIST, surgery residents tend to skip it and, when asked, mostly rely on luck. In the year-4 oral exam GIST and retroperitoneal tumor may come up; anyone with weak basics who meets a hardcore examiner risks failing.
- This chapter first covers principles of diagnosis and treatment, then the approach to soft tissue sarcoma at each site; GIST has much content residents should know deeply, so it is a separate chapter.
- As printed, encouragement: work is hard but we are tough; the exam is near, don't watch too many live clips. Remember: training in surgery and then failing the exam wastes the fees — score high or low doesn't matter, just pass first.

## Soft Tissue Sarcoma
- Rare tumors from mesenchymal tissue.
- < 1% of adult cancers, but 15% in children.
- 2 main groups:
  1. **Sarcoma of soft tissue** — e.g. from fat, blood vessel, muscle and nerve sheath
  2. **Sarcoma of bone** — not written here to save energy, since we sit the surgery board, not ortho
- > 50 subtypes; the common ones: MFH (malignant fibrous histiocytoma), liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma and malignant nerve sheath tumor.
- **WHO pathological classification** — by cell of origin:

| Cell of origin | Sarcoma |
|---|---|
| Adipocytic tumors | Liposarcoma |
| Fibroblasic/myofibroblastic tumor (as printed) | — |
| Fibrohystiocytic tr (as printed) | MFH |
| Smooth muscle | Leiomyosarcoma |
| Skeletal muscle | RMS |
| Vascular | AS |
| Periferal n (as printed) | Malignan peripheral nerve sheath tr (as printed) |

- **Location** — anywhere in the body:
  1. Extremity 50%, especially the leg
  2. Intraabdominal or GI sarcoma 25% — almost 90% of GI sarcoma is GIST
  3. Retroperitoneal sarcoma 15-20%
  4. Head & neck sarcoma — common in children; 9%
- **Risk factors**
  1. Radiation exposure — associated with osteosarcoma and MFH
  2. Occupational chemical products, e.g. vinyl chloride — associated with hepatic angiosarcoma
  3. Chronic lymphedema after ALND for breast cancer — associated with lymphangiosarcoma
  4. Genetic syndromes:
     - 4.1 Nerve sheath tumor — in neurofibromato(sis) (as printed); may occur with CNS tumor and pheochromocytoma
     - 4.2 Rhabdomyosarcoma — in Li-Fraumeni syndrome
     - 4.3 Angiomyolipoma — in tuberous sclerosis (has been examined)
     - 4.4 Desmoid tumor — in FAP and Gardner syndrome
  - Remember a little: a history of trauma is not related to soft tissue sarcoma.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): depend on where the mass is — arm, leg, intra-abdominal or retroperitoneal, and which organ it involves; most present as an asymptomatic mass. Retroperitoneal and proximal extremity masses are often huge at presentation. Less often, symptoms come from compression of bowel, vessels or nerves — pain, swelling — or an intra-abdominal mass big enough to cause bowel obstruction.
- A soft tissue tumor > 5 cm, fast-growing, or below the fascia → take history, examine, evaluate with imaging (usually US or CT first), and tissue diagnosis by CNB.
- **Imaging studies**: help diagnosis, show local invasion and metastasis, assist percutaneous biopsy, suggest benign vs malignant and, if malignant, low vs high grade; an expert reader can judge resectability.
- **CT scan**: suits intraperitoneal and retroperitoneal sarcoma; cheaper and quicker than MRI.
- **MRI**: suits extremity sarcoma and desmoid; good for assessing resectability.
  - T1: hypodense mass — may be hard to tell sarcoma from fibrosis
  - T2: tumor appears bright white

## Biopsy Technique
- Residents must pay attention, especially for extremity soft tissue sarcoma, as it can be examined: pathology gives the diagnosis, grading and staging. Before CNB or open biopsy, MRI first to rule out neurovascular or bony invasion — like triple assessment in breast.
- **Percutaneous technique**: FNA and CNB (core needle biopsy).
- **Open technique**: incisional and excisional biopsy.
- **FNA**: suits superficial lesions; for deep lesions such as mediastinum and retroperitoneum it does not work well for tissue diagnosis of soft tissue sarcoma. FNA is now rarely used because grading is hard and the cytologist must be truly expert; accuracy only 60%.
- **CNB (core needle biopsy)**: the preferred technique, strongly favoured; gives plenty of tissue — the pathologist reads the slide happily, especially for deep lesions.
- Figure: Principle of Trucut biopsy — needle with a slot for specimen inside a sheath; push needle into lesion → slide sheath over needle to cut specimen → withdraw needle, sheath and specimen.
- For deep masses, CNB should be US- or CT-guided — hitting a vessel and bleeding could be disastrous. CNB accuracy 93%.
- **Incisional biopsy**: when CNB is non-diagnostic or tissue is insufficient, especially deep lesions or large superficial lesions; accuracy > 95%.
  - **Technique** (pay some attention — it is examined)
    1. Local anesthesia
    2. **Incision**
       - 2.1 **Extremity** (arm or leg): incise along the long axis = longitudinal biopsy, in line with the future operative incision. Remember (จำไว้): a transverse incision may get you kicked (as printed).
       - 2.2 **Trunk** or chest: incise along the ribs, following Langer's line
    3. Incise over the center of the mass, aiming for the shortest route; avoid large vessels and nerves — the operator must know anatomy well.
    4. Do not raise flaps or dissect much — it breaks tissue planes; avoid bleeding or hematoma, which spread tumor cells and mean more tissue loss at later wide excision.
    5. Drains not routinely needed; if used, a closed suction drain such as redivac, with the drain hole as close to the incision as possible, so the drain tract is included at re-excision.
- **Excisional biopsy**: for superficial lesions of the arm, leg or chest wall < 3 cm (Greenfield uses 4 cm, most use 3 cm). Do not use excisional biopsy for deep lesions — dissecting through much tissue spreads tumor cells widely; if the result is sarcoma, plan re-excision.
  - Doing a wide en bloc excision just to obtain tissue for diagnosis should not be done.
- **Endoscopic biopsy or image-guided needle biopsy**: for deep, thoracic, abdominal or pelvic sarcomas.

## Staging & Prognostic Factors
- Still AJCC; the latest is edition 8 (2017); residents need only what is necessary.
- T1 size < 5 cm; T2 size 5-10 cm; T3 size 10-15 cm; T4 size > 15 cm.
- N stage: nodal spread is fairly uncommon, except angiosarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma; N+ means at least stage 3.
- **Histologic grade**: the most important prognostic factor — predicts recurrence after resection and distant metastasis. Pathologists look at cellularity, differentiation, pleomorphism, necrosis and number of mitoses; we only need necrosis and mitoses — if many, prognosis poor.
- 3 grades:
  1. **Low grade** or well diff. (G1): resectable 80%, metastasis ≤ 10%
  2. **Intermediate grade** or moderately diff. (G2): resectable about 30%, metastasis about 30%
  3. **High grade** or poorly diff. (G3): rarely resectable; metastasis 50-60%
- Texts may add G4 or undiff — that is fine.
- **Tumor type**
  1. Alveolar, embryonal rhabdo, Ewing: usually high grade
  2. Well diff. sarcomas and myxoid liposarcoma: low grade
- **Size**: > 5 cm is bad — higher metastasis risk.

| Size | 5-year survival |
|---|---|
| < 5 cm | 84% |
| 5-10 cm | 70% |
| 10-15 cm | 50% |
| > 15 cm | 33% |

- **Location**
  1. Tumor above the superficial fascia of the arm, leg or chest: usually good prognosis
  2. Tumor invading the superficial fascia, or deep lesions such as visceral organ, retroperitoneum, mediastinum: poor prognosis
- **Metastasis**: soft tissue sarcoma mostly invades locally; distant metastasis is less common than local recurrence.
  1. Size < 5 cm: little metastasis; > 5 cm: more metastasis
  2. Nodal spread very rare
  3. Distant metastasis:
     - 3.1 Extremity sarcoma — tends to go to lung
     - 3.2 Retroperitoneal sarcoma — tends to go to liver
- **Margin**: +ve micro or R1 → poor result; R2 (gross margin not free after resection) → recurrence is certain.

## Management
- For soft tissue sarcoma, surgery is the main treatment, but many helpers should be consulted — multidisciplinary treatment; if sued or jailed, you are not alone — there are friends sharing the bad luck (as printed).
- Surgery is the main treatment, margin 1-2 cm.
- RT: for T2, or when pathology reports intermediate or high grade tumor.
- **Chemotherapy**: for locally advanced high grade sarcoma:
  1. Rhabdomyosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma of any size
  2. Any soft tissue sarcoma > 10 cm, excluding dedifferentiate liposacoma (as printed)
  3. Pheomorphic (as printed) liposarcoma, round cell liposarcoma, synovial sarcoma of size 5-10 cm, or
  4. Distant metastasis
- **Principles of surgery** (หลักของการผ่าตัด)
  1. Soft tissue sarcoma has a pseudocapsule containing tumor cells → enucleation (as printed "eneucleation") gives frequent recurrence.
  2. Remove the mass with a cuff of surrounding tissue in all 3 dimensions, cutting in grossly normal tissue planes free of tumor contamination, margin > 1 cm. If the mass abuts a vessel or nerve without gross tumor involvement, remove only the adventitia or perineurium with the mass.
  3. Remove the mass in one piece.
  4. Extremity sarcoma: try to avoid amputation; the trend now favours limb sparing operation.
  5. Nodal spread is uncommon → LND not needed (จำ).
  6. After removal, clip the tumor bed as a guide for postoperative RT; if a close suction drain is used, the drain exit should be close to the wound edge.
  7. After en bloc resection, if pathology reports +ve margin or local recurrence occurs and nothing is done, the patient lives only about 1.5 years and dies quietly — consider re-resection.
  8. **Isolated metastasis** to lung or liver — remove if resectable.
     - **Indications for metastasectomy**
       - 8.1 The primary lesion can be completely removed
       - 8.2 No other metastasis apart from liver or lung alone; if the patient can tolerate, remove primary and lung lesions together
       - 8.3 If spread to > 1 organ, do not resect, except for palliation, e.g. a bleeding or foul infected mass

## Extremity Sarcoma
- Additional points: common types in the arm and leg are liposarcoma and MFH.
- Patients usually present with an asymptomatic mass; some have dull aching pain.
- Mass > 5 cm (Schwartz 9th ed. uses 3 cm), fast growing, painful, or present > 4 wk → biopsy.
- Biopsy: CT- or US-guided core biopsy gives enough tissue for diagnosis and grading. Remember: FNA does not work.
- If open biopsy, it should be incisional biopsy, with the incision along the long axis of the limb — not transverse, because re-excision must include the old incision (and the drain tract if a drain was placed), making closure difficult.
- Book example: transverse skin biopsy with a redivac drain far from the wound; at en bloc re-excision the wound was hard to close.
- **Imaging** — for correct staging:
  1. **MRI**: best imaging; CT sometimes added. Besides diagnosis, MRI shows fascial planes clearly and assesses resectability; masses ≥ 4 cm should have MRI before surgery. MRI shows whether the tumor has invaded the fascia and its relation to blood vessels, nerves, muscle, bone — so you can prepare to cut bone, resect and anastomose vessels, prepare prosthesis, and take care with nerves.
  2. **CT chest**: for lung metastasis, to rule out stage 4 disese (as printed); some, mostly with small masses, screen with CXR first — acceptable.
  3. If core biopsy shows myxoid liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, epitheloid sarcoma or angiosarcoma → add abdomen & pelvic CT, as these may spread to the liver.
  4. **PET scan**: not routine; use to assess response in patients given neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery.
- **Management**
  1. **Local treatment** — surgery is the main treatment:
     - 1.1 Limb salvage with adjuvant treatment, especially preop. or postoperative RT, benefits limb preserving surgery; even if bone must be removed, a prosthess (as printed) can replace it; survival and recurrence are no different from amputation; now used in > 90%.
     - 1.2 Limb amputation
  2. **Systemic treatment**: chemotherapy.

## Surgical Resection
1. Same principles as soft tissue sarcoma surgery; margin at least 1 cm all around is favoured; don't forget to include the old biopsy scar; re-resect any margin not free.
2. In the past muscle was removed from origin to insertion; Cameron's current view: removing the whole compartment or muscle group is not necessary — a wide margin is enough; with postop. RT, 5 year survival is as good as BK or AK amputation.
3. Place surgical clips at the margins of the resected bed as a guide for future RT.
4. **Indications for amputation**
   - 4.1 Attempted resection still R2 — much gross tumor left
   - 4.2 Tumor on bone, sciatic nerve or major blood vessel such that the preserved limb would be useless after resection
   - 4.3 High grade sarcoma previously operated and irradiated that still recurs at the same site
   - 4.4 Pathological fracture
   - 4.5 Bleeding or foul infected mass
   - 4.6 Patient preference
- Note (หมายเหตุ): indications for amputation are now fewer because:
  1. RT and chemo. shrink tumors so more can be resected
  2. Major vessel involvement can be resected with graft replacement
  3. Tumor on a nerve, R1 after resection — now RT helps, so a major nerve need not be sacrificed
  4. Bone: MRI first; if needed, resect bone and use bone graft or prosthesis, then RT
  5. Close margins may be necessary to preserve uninvolved critical neurovascular structures, bone and joint (NCCN)

## Radiation Therapy
- **Indications** (ควรจำ)
  1. Mass > 5 cm or T2 and above — remember: mass < 5 cm, no RT
  2. High grade tumor, regardless of size
  3. Post-resection pathology with close margin or margin 1 cm or less
  4. Preoperative RT, especially for tumors near the neurovascular bundle or bone, to shrink the tumor until an adequate margin is possible

## Chemotherapy
- For soft tissue sarcoma, results are not as good as RT. Some give adjuvant doxorubicin with ifosfamide, or step up to a taxane with gemcitabines — it just prolongs the patient's suffering (as printed); exceptions are Ewing, rhabdo and osteosarcoma, which show some response to chemo.
- Europe recommends **isolated regional limb perfusion**: doxorubicin at 40°C through a perfusion circuit — results better, amputation rate reduced by up to 80%.
- **Anti-tumor agents** or targeted therapy: very expensive; reported as the future drug group for tumors resistant to all chemo. Now there is ET-743 or trabect (trade name yondelis), with several more still in phase 1 study; paid from our taxes it would be very costly (as printed).
- **Ablative treatment** such as RFA or cryotherapy: of little use, since tumors are usually large.

## Summary of Treatment for Soft Tissue Sarcoma (ข้อสรุปของการรักษา)
- The author is a fan of John L. Cameron, so quotes Uncle's summary from Current Surgical Therapy, close to NCCN:
  1. Mass < 5 cm, low grade → complete resection with free margin > 1 cm only
  2. Mass > 5 cm, low grade sarcoma → complete resection + PO. RT
  3. Mass > 5 cm, high grade sarcoma → complete resection + IORT + PO. RT
  4. Mass > 10 cm, high grade sarcoma → consider preop CMT or RT, then resection + IORT + PORT
- IORT = intraoperative RT; more detail under retroperitoneal sarcoma.

## Follow Up for Soft Tissue Sarcoma
1. Mass < 5 cm (T1), low grade → visit and CT lung every 6-12 months for the first 2-3 years, then yearly until doctor or patient dies first (as printed).
2. Mass > 5 cm, high grade → visit and CT chest every 3-6 months for the first 2-3 years, then every 6 months until 5 years; if still well, yearly.

## Extremity Sarcoma & Lung Metastasis
- Lung is the most common metastatic site of soft tissue sarcoma; resection gives a survival advantage. Candidates for lung resection: primary completely removed, fit for surgery, and adequate pulmonary reserve after lung resection. Even bilateral lung spread can be operated; anatomical resection is not needed — just staple out the nodules.
- If lung lesions recur after resection and look resectable, operate again.
- Surgery is increasingly done by VATS.

## Retroperitoneal Sarcoma
- **Anatomy**: the retroperitoneal space is very large; by the time a mass causes symptoms it is very big.
  1. Top to bottom: from the diaphragm (T12) down to the levator pelvic diaphragm at the coccyx
  2. Laterally: from the ascending colon on the right and descending colon on the left to the midline with aorta and IVC
- Tumors here: 83% malignant, 17% benign.
- Malignant: 1/3 soft tissue sarcoma, 1/3 lymphoma, 1/3 germ cell tumor.
- Sarcomas: number 1 liposarcoma, then leiomyosarcoma and malignant fibrous histiocytoma.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): usually a large abdominal mass, often asymptomatic, except dull pain or symptoms from compressing nearby organs — IVC (leg swelling), ureter (hydronephrosis), or nerve roots (pain radiating down the leg).
- 70% of masses are > 10 cm and 50% > 20 cm at diagnosis; adherence to vital organs makes a free margin difficult; the most commonly invaded organ is the kidney.
- **Local recurrence** within 5 years after resection about 75%, and is the cause of death.
- 5 year survival about 35-58%; prognosis worse than extremity sarcoma because it is a deep lesion.
- **Evaluation & workup**
  1. History and examination; general status — fit for surgery?
  2. Rule out hormone-secreting adrenal gland tumors, or lymphoma — often fever, night sweat, generalized nodes, high LDH; CT showing a chain of paraaortic or paracaval masses suggests lymphoma
  3. Don't forget to palpate the testes in men to rule out germ cell tumor; if the testis is enlarged, draw tumor markers such as AFP, HCG
  4. Remember: sarcoma — resect without removing nodes; lymphoma — chemo.; germ cell tumor — radical retroperitoneal lymphadenectomy ± chemo.
- **Imaging studies**
  1. **CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast**: mass size, relation to vessels, kidney, colon, local invasion; also look at the liver, since retroperitoneal sarcoma tends to spread to the liver, unlike extremity sarcoma which spreads to lung. CT also helps diagnose liposarcoma, the most common retroperitoneal tumor: fat attenuation -10 to -100. Benign lipoma: well defined, uniform encapsulated mass; malignant: very large, ill-defined, with internal soft tissue component.
  2. **CT chest or chest x-ray**: either one, since retroperitoneal sarcoma rarely goes to lung, so no need to spend much — except if chemotherapy is planned before or after surgery; experts prefer CT over plain chest film on this point.
  3. **MRI**: only when invasion of psoas or quadratus muscle, or neurovascular invasion, is suspected; otherwise not needed.
- **Biopsy**: generally not needed — for a resectable case, whatever the tissue diagnosis (if not lymphoma), en bloc resection is done anyway; exception: when neoadjuvant treatment is planned, the tissue result is needed first.
  - **Indications for preoperative biopsy**
    1. Suspected lymphoma or germ cell tumor
    2. Preop. RT or chemo. planned
    3. Unresectable disease, for RT or chemo.
    4. Suspected metastasis from a primary elsewhere
    5. Mass found incidentally during surgery for another disease
  - **Biopsy method** (วิธีการทำ Biopsy): try to avoid percutaneous biopsy — fear of needle-tract seeding, or tumor rupture if it is GIST; if needed, choose among:
    1. CT-guided core needle biopsy, at the site closest to the primary tumor, avoiding passing through the peritoneal cavity, and with the needle site removable en bloc
    2. Open or laparoscopic biopsy — rarely used; risk of tumor rupture or contamination
    3. EUS biopsy — NCCN strongly favours it, especially for GIST

## Surgical Management for Retroperitoneal Sarcoma
- En bloc resection is the most effective treatment for primary and recurrent tumors; may need removal of kidney, colon, pancreas or spleen too — organs resected together in 50-80%. IVC occlusion of the infrarenal vena cava without metastasis is still resectable. When sarcoma causes gradual IVC occlusion, good collaterals form — the legs are not swollen and CT shows dilated veins in the paravertebral area and pelvis; resect the tumor en bloc with the involved vena cava without graft replacement.
- Unresectable cases live about 1.5 years; RT or chemo. hardly works. Because masses are huge, R0 is possible in only 40-60%.
- Prepare bowel prep and check both kidney functions, since colon and kidney may need removal.
- Retroperitoneal sarcoma surgery is a difficult case — whoever operates should have good backbone (as printed, a joke about residents who only play on line or facebook not getting these cases).
- If CT shows the whole mass cannot be removed, do not open — it is just an expensive biopsy and makes the next surgeon's job harder. Often a newcomer opens, closes saying it cannot be removed, then refers, and an expert reopens and gets R0.
- If uncertain whether R0 is achievable because the mass is very large, consider preop. RT, which works better than preop. chemo.; preop. RT has been reported to shrink the mass enough for R0.

## Role of Palliative Resection
- May be used in:
  1. Intestinal obstruction
  2. Intractable pain
  3. Bleeding
  4. Well diff liposarcoma, which may benefit from repeated tumor debulking

## Surgical Technique
1. Supine; prep skin from neck to knees.
2. The author favours midline incision; if extension is needed, add a lateral incision as a T; some extend into the chest, or use bilateral subcostal incision — wide exposure.
3. Assess the abdomen: liver, peritoneum for tumor seeding; if none, check whether tumor invades great vessels, especially aorta, IVC, SMA, SMV and renal vein; if not, proceed — even IVC invasion has been reported resectable in some cases.
4. If kidney, pancreas or colon is invaded and resection would give R0 → go ahead.
5. Place silver clips around the tumor bed to mark the field for postoperative radiation.
6. If known preoperatively to be liposarcoma → clear the fat from diaphragm to pelvis completely.

## Recurrence
- Up to 75%, at the tumor bed and liver; if resectable and the patient is fit, operate again.

## Adjuvant Treatment for Retroperitoneal Sarcoma
- **Radiation** — 3 ways:
  1. **Preop. RT** then surgical resection — suits:
     - 1.1 Large size
     - 1.2 High grade tumor
     - 1.3 Recurrent low grade tumor
     - 1.4 High risk case
  2. **Postop. RT**: often problematic — radiosensitive bowel lies in the way, so high doses cannot be given.
  3. **Intraoperative RT (IORT)**: irradiate the tumor bed directly after removing the mass, shielding bowel, so high doses can be given; then postop. RT at a dose low enough to avoid radiation enterocolitis — IORT reduces bowel injury. Few institutions can do IORT, so most reports come from single centers; long-term outcome and local disease control are better than without it.
  - Figure: Example regimen — preoperative IMRT (GTV 50-56 Gy (SIB), PTV 45-50 Gy (25 Fx)) → surgery + IOERT (10-12 Gy).
  4. **Newer RT techniques**, e.g. IMRT or 3D-RT using proton after surgery — look very good; awaiting RCT results.
- **Chemotherapy**: some benefit in liposarcoma, MFH, rhabdo, or neuroectodermal in organ (as printed).

## Intraabdominal Soft Tissue Sarcoma
- 2 main groups:
  1. **GIST** — long topic, increasingly seen, likely examined; written in a separate chapter
  2. **Non-GIST** — leiomyoma, leiomyosarcoma, desmoid tumor
- **Non-GIST sarcoma**: symptoms, diagnosis and treatment similar to retroperitoneal sarcoma — surgery is main; RT is a supplement; chemo is of little use.
- **Principles of treatment of intraabdominal soft tissue sarcoma**
  1. **Resectable mass** without evidence of metastasis → en bloc resection + IORT
  2. **Primary resectable mass with limited & isolated liver metastasis** → resect both primary and the liver lesion
  3. **Unresectable primary or disseminated metastasis** → very poor prognosis
     - 3.1 Asymptomatic → just observe; NCCN says it's fine (as printed, "คุณแม่ไม่ว่า")
     - 3.2 If the doctor feels itchy to act → try chemo., RT, embolization; without complications may live 2-3 months
  4. **Recurrence PO**: if assessed R0-resectable, and the surgeon is capable and the patient fit → re-resection

## Leiomyoma
- Benign tumor of the bowel wall; upper > lower GI tract.
- Most leiomyomas are asymptomatic, found incidentally at endoscopy.
- Problem: hard to distinguish from leiomyosarcoma; large masses bleed or obstruct.
- Treatment: small → limited resection only; large, especially > 5 cm, high malignancy risk → radical resection.

## Leiomyosarcoma
- In the lower GI, most common in the rectum; early symptoms similar to leiomyoma.
- Treatment: radical resection.

## Desmoid Tumor
- Aggressive fibromatosis from fascia or musculo-aponeurotic structures (not bone or peritoneum); sporadic, plus a group associated with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP).
- Slow-growing with local invasion but no metastasis (จำ); frequent recurrence after resection (จำ); often classified as low grade fibrosarcoma although histology resembles ordinary benign fibroblastic cells. The Schwartz 11e author concludes desmoid is not fibrosarcoma.
- **Cause of death** (สาเหตุการเสียชีวิต): usually locally progressive disease, not metastasis.
- **Natural history**: hard to predict — moody: sometimes slow, sometimes fast, sometimes stops growing or regresses.
- Usually a painless mass in the abdominal wall; if painful, think of other diseases first.
- Common in women; often grows fast during pregnancy, after delivery, or at an old surgical scar; some believe high estrogen in pregnancy stimulates growth.
- Mostly sporadic; a minority of desmoids are FAP-associated.
- **Location**
  1. **Abdominal wall**: young pregnant women, or FAP after total proctocolectomy + IPAA
  2. **Intraabdominal mesenteric desmoid**: after FAP surgery in 7-16%, especially in the mesentery, where behaviour is very nasty and aggressive
  3. **Extremity desmoid**: men or women, slightly older; fairly large masses
  4. **Breast desmoid**: hard to distinguish from cancer — clinical and mammogram similar — so biopsy is needed
- **Evaluation & workup**
  1. History and examination — don't forget family history of FAP
  2. Chest x-ray (lung)
  3. CT for intra-abdominal desmoid; MRI for desmoid of abdominal wall, chest, arms, legs (remember: one best — don't choose US)
  4. Biopsy: if resectable with free margin > 2 cm, wide local resection straight away; large masses should be confirmed first by core or incisional biopsy
- **Management**
  1. **Surgical resection**: free margin about 2-4 cm, confirmed by intraoperative frozen section. Most surgeons believe local recurrence comes from non-free margins, but oddly, even with pathology-confirmed free margins, up to 1/3 recur after surgery. So surgeons are still debating whether microscopic control is needed; those who say frozen is unnecessary cite NCCN: if recurrence occurs, re-excise — only in symptomatic or life-threatening tumors, or rapidly growing masses with local invasion where surgery has very high morbidity; incompletely resected cases get adjuvant treatment, especially RT.
  2. **RT**: for unresectable desmoid, or cases where surgery would have high morbidity.
  3. **Cytostatic agents**: tamoxifen, interferon alpha, NSAIDs such as sulindac and celecoxib — found to cause tumor regression; don't worry about the mechanism, just remember. Celecoxib: beware in heart disease patients. Estrogen or the pill is forbidden.
  4. **Chemotherapy**: when 1, 2 and 3 fail; drugs: metrotrexate (as printed) and vinblastin.
  5. **Targeted therapy**: a 2002-2006 trial tried imatinib (used for GIST) in desmoid — found reasonably useful.
- **Recurrence after surgery** (หลังผ่าตัด): 5-50%, depending on the surgeon's skill and looks (as printed).
  1. If operable → re-resection
  2. Not suitable for surgery, e.g. large recurrence or involving major neurovascular structures, especially nerves → consider RT with cytostatic agents
- **Chest wall desmoid** (จำ)
  - Surgery (การผ่าตัด): wide local resection with margin 2-4 cm + intraoperative F/S; in addition, remove the ribs above and below the mass; rib margin 4-5 cm.
- **FAP & mesenteric desmoid**
  - A desmoid in a young person → beware FAP. Desmoid is linked to mortality in FAP and Gardner syndrome: after total proctocolectomy, FAP patients rarely die of CRC but more often die of desmoid. Usually an asymptomatic mass; later pain, bowel obstruction, ureter obstruction, GI bleeding, DVT.
  - **CT scan**: ill-defined heterogeneous soft tissue mass with infiltration and encasement of adjacent bowel or SMA.
  - **Treatment** (การรักษา): desmoid after FAP surgery, especially mesenteric, is hard to treat and aggressive; going in may need so much bowel resection that short bowel results, since desmoid often recurs around the SMA — the more you cut, the more it recurs. So unless there is complete small bowel obstruction or uncontrolled GI bleeding — advice given with good wishes: don't mess with mesenteric desmoid in FAP; observe or give sulindac ± tamoxifen.
- **FAP & abdominal wall desmoid**
  - Usually at the midline surgical scar of the abdominal wall, not inside the abdomen.
  - **Treatment**: here it can be touched — wide local resection with free margin under F/S control.
- **Head & neck desmoid**
  - Especially around the eye socket. Treatment: en bloc resection ± orbital exenteration — rather brutal (as printed).
- **Dermatofibrosarcoma protubarans (DFSP) or Andrew disease** (as printed)
  - Rare low grade fibrosarcoma of the dermis; nodular, slow-growing; on trunk, extremity, head and neck; some have satellite lesions with finger-like extensions around.
  - Behaviour like desmoid — also no metastasis; some say it looks like an infected keloid (as printed: as you like).
  - **Treatment** (การรักษา): like desmoid, but margin 2-3 cm controlled by frozen section; the deep margin should be deeper than for ordinary desmoid, usually including the deep fascia. If completely removed, RT is not needed.

## Head & Neck Sarcoma
- 1% of head & neck malignant tumors; common in children; number 1 is rhabdomyosarcoma, usually at the tongue and soft palate.
- **Rhabdomyosarcoma** of the neck — 2 subtypes:
  1. Embryonal rhabdo. — more common
  2. Alveolar rhabdo. — less common, worse prognosis
- Fast-growing; usually presents as a mass with pressure effect — pain, numbness.
- **Diff. diag.**
  1. Benign: infection such as TB, or congenital such as branchial cleft cyst
  2. Malignant: primary such as lymphoma, or metastasis from face, neck or thyroid cancer
- **Management**
  1. Surgery remains main, but free margins are very hard because of many vital structures; full-dose RT is often not possible either.
  2. Luckily rhabdo. responds well to chemo. (doxorubicin group), so treatment is usually surgery combined with chemo.

## Bone Sarcoma
- Most common: osteosarcoma, originating from metaphyseal cells.
- **Location**: usually around the knee joint.
- **Plain film**: starburst appearance.
- Often already metastatic at diagnosis.
- **Treatment** (การรักษา): amputation is now less common — bone is resected and a prosthesis inserted; leave it to ortho — that's their path.

## Radiation Induced Sarcoma (heading as printed: "Radiation Indued Sarcoma")
- < 1%, but increasingly discussed, so included.
- Dose that raises sarcoma risk: > 55 Gy carries risk; < 30 Gy does not increase risk.
- Mean latency about 13 years 10 months.
- Common types: malignant fibrous histiocytoma, osteosarcoma, chondosarcoma (as printed), angiosarcoma.
- **Diagnosis — Cohan's criteria** (as printed)
  1. Microscopic evidence of non-malignant nature of the initial condition
  2. Sarcoma subsequently developed within the RT beam
  3. Asymptomatic period > 5 years
  4. All sarcomas proved by histopathology to be genuine
- Fairly aggressive: local invasion of neurovascular structures and distant metastasis to lung and bone.
- 5 year survival about 10-30% — worse than non-RT sarcoma of the same stage.
- Treatment: resect if possible; for large masses that look unresectable, consider neoadjuvant therapy according to sarcoma type.
- Repeat RT is limited, but may be given as intraoperative RT boost to the tumor bed or brachytherapy.

## Summary: Management Guideline of Soft Tissue Sarcoma (สรุป)
- **Sabiston 20e 2017**
  Figure: Management algorithm, as steps —
  1. History and physical exam; CT/MRI of primary site; biopsy — core vs incisional; determine prognosis by histologic type, size, grade, patient age.
  2. Low grade → function sparing complete excision:
     - Negative margin → no adjuvant therapy.
     - Microscopic positive margin → consider postoperative IMRT if recurrence would result in significant functional loss.
  3. High grade (Ewing's/Rhabdo → preop chemo):
     - ≤ 5 cm → function sparing complete excision.
     - > 5 to < 10 cm → consider preoperative chemotherapy if SS or RC/Pleo LS → function sparing complete excision.
     - ≥ 10 cm → consider preoperative chemotherapy → function sparing complete excision.
  4. After excision of high grade tumor:
     - ≥ 1 cm margin: ≤ 5 cm → no adjuvant therapy; > 5 to < 10 cm → consider perioperative BRT or postoperative IMRT; > 10 cm → postoperative IMRT.
     - < 1 cm margin or microscopic positive margin: ≤ 5 cm → consider perioperative BRT or postoperative IMRT; > 5 to < 10 cm → perioperative BRT or postoperative IMRT; > 10 cm → postoperative IMRT.
- **Schwartz 9e 2009**
  Figure: Management algorithm, as steps —
  1. Establish diagnosis: CT/US guided core biopsy, expert pathology review.
  2. Establish disease stage: MRI extremity, CXR/CT chest.
  3. Treatment by stage:

| Stage | Definition | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| I | Low-grade tumors | Surgical resection |
| II | ≤ 5 cm high-grade tumors | Surgical resection ± radiation |
| III | > 5 cm high-grade, or nodal disease | Surgical resection, radiation ± chemotherapy |
| IV | Distant metastatic disease | Chemotherapy ± surgical resection |
