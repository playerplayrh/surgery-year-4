# Spleen 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 646-672 (PDF pages 1743-1769)

## Introduction
- For the spleen, surgeons must know trauma well; for splenic diseases, knowing this much is enough — unless you have a friend in Med or Hemato, then you will need to know more (as printed, a joke about a girlfriend/boyfriend).
- Before the Buddhist era, the spleen was believed to balance the body: since the liver is on the right, the body built the spleen on the left so it would not tilt (as printed).
- Before the Christian era, Galen believed the spleen was an "organ of mystery" that purified substances from blood coming from the liver, then released them back into the stomach via the gastrosplenic A.

## Anatomy
- Largest RE organ; weight about 150 gm; in the LUQ under ribs 9-11; length 7-11 cm.
- **Splenomegaly**: spleen weight > 500 gm (ครึ่งกิโล) or length > 15 cm.
- **Massive splenomegaly**: weight > 1 kg or length > 22 cm.
- A palpable spleen means it is at least 2 × normal size, or weighs not less than 750 gm.
- **Suspensory ligaments**
  1. Splenocolic
  2. Splenorenal
  3. Phrenosplenic
  4. Gastrosplenic — the only ligament carrying blood vessels (short gastric vessels); the other 3 are avascular ligaments, except in portal hypertension.
- **Blood supply**: splenic A., a branch of the celiac axis; venous return by the splenic V., which joins the IMV and drains into the PV.
- **Accessory spleen**: most common at the splenic hilum (54%); can even occur at the ovary, but not found in Gerota fascia.
- Figure: Sites of accessory spleen — hilar region 54%, pedicle 25%, greater omentum 12%, tail of pancreas 6%, splenocolic ligament 2%, mesentery 0.5%, left ovary 0.5%.
- **Red pulp** (85%): continuation of the capillaries just before they drain into veins; contains sinusoids, macrophages and histiocytes; acts as a filter removing deformed or aged RBCs, and does pitting of material on the RBC membrane.
- **White pulp** (15%): lymphocytes around capillaries; immune function and site of bacterial clearance.

## Function
- Main functions of the spleen:
  1. **Hematologic function**
     - 1.1 Blood filter: removes aged or misshapen RBCs, bacteria and particulated antigen
     - 1.2 Makes RBC and WBC only in fetal life (จำ); afterwards bone marrow takes over
     - 1.3 Destroys aged Plt. and is also a reservoir storing Plt.
  2. **Immunologic function**: phagocytosis; the spleen makes IgM, tuftsin and properdin, used in opsonization to clear organisms.
- **Opsonization**
  - Clears bacteria, especially encapsulated bacteria: Strep. or Dipplococcus (as printed) pneumonia, Neisseria meningitis and H. influenza. Tuftsin stimulates phagocytosis; properdin helps activate the complement pathway. Without a spleen these infections occur easily.
  - Good bacterial clearance needs at least 1/3 of the spleen left, and it must be vascularized spleen. This is why autotransplantation after total splenectomy in trauma is opposed: small slices of spleen implanted in the greater omentum may raise IgM but not enough to clear bacteria.
- **Remember** (จำไว้)
  1. Spleen makes IgM — not IgG, IgA or T cells.
  2. Spleen destroys plt. — it does not make plt.

## Imaging of the Spleen
- **Indications**
  1. Before lap. splenectomy: CT to assess splenic size
  2. Trauma
  3. LUQ pain with suspected cyst or abscess
  4. Guide for percutaneous procedures
- **Selected points worth knowing**
  1. **CT**: for the spleen, 3D-CT is preferred over US or MRI.
  2. **Nuclear imaging**: Tc 99 m sulfur colloid scan shows location and size of the spleen, especially to locate an accessory spleen when a hematologic disease does not improve after splenectomy.

## Splenectomy
- **Absolute indications**
  1. Hereditary spherocytosis
  2. Trauma where the spleen cannot be saved, or spontaneous rupture
  3. Splenic abscess
  4. Echinococcal cyst of the spleen
  5. Primary tumor such as sarcoma
  6. Bleeding gastric varice from splenic vein thrombosis
- **Preoperative preparation** — besides the usual preparation, 5 extra points (numbering as printed):
  1. **Splenic artery embolization**: before removing a large spleen, to shrink it and make lap. splenectomy easier; beware catheter complications, pancreatitis, and pain from splenic infarction.
  2. **Bowel preparation**: not needed; the colon is rarely cut nowadays, and some no longer prep the bowel even for colon surgery (as printed).
  3. **DVT prophylaxis**: of interest because overall DVT is 5-10%; at least use a compressive device in every case. In myeloproliferative disorder, DVT risk is up to 40% → add LMWH.
  4. **Preoperative antibiotics**: 1 hour before skin incision.
  4. (as printed) **Vaccination**: especially in elective cases with hematologic disease; vaccinate every splenectomy patient.
  5. **Steroid**: hematologic patients such as AIHA or ITP often have long-term steroids → beware postoperative crisis (see the Adrenal gland chapter).
- **Partial splenectomy** — consider in:
  1. Children
  2. Splenic trauma
  3. Gaucher's disease
  4. Idiopathic splenic cyst that is not a hydatid cyst

## Laparoscopic Splenectomy (LS)
- Now, if lap. is possible it should be first choice; safety and efficacy suit hematologic diseases without splenomegaly, e.g. ITP. For patients: less blood loss, shorter hospital stay, less morbidity than open splenectomy.
- Contraindications to lap.: uncorrectable coagulopathy, acute and unstable trauma, portal hypertension, hematologic malignancy, and a very large spleen. Hand-assisted laparoscopic surgery (HALS) can remove a spleen of any size, even upper-to-lower pole > 22 cm, width > 19 cm.
- European Endoscopic Surgery recommends HALS or open splenectomy if the upper-to-lower pole length is > 20 cm.
- Problem of lap. splenectomy: removing the spleen from the abdomen — usually extend the incision at the anterior axillary line port and pull the spleen out in an endobag, or use a hand-assist device.
- **Open splenectomy** — choose in:
  1. Trauma
  2. Splenic abscess
  3. Portal hypertension
  4. Massive ascites
  5. Previous RT to the upper abdomen
  6. Massive splenomegaly
  7. Multiple previous operations — an expert laparoscopist may try lap. first and open if it cannot be done.
- Newer splenectomy techniques: hand-assisted splenectomy, single-incision laparoscopic surgery, robotic splenectomy, NOTES splenectomy (spleen removed via the vagina); none is better than standard lap.
- **Massive splenomegaly**: ligate the splenic A. lying along the upper border of the pancreas first → spleen shrinks, making surgery easier.
- **Routine drain**: not used, unless the pancreatic tail was injured — 75% of the tail lies within 1 cm of the splenic hilum.
- **Incidental splenectomy** — a "promotion package" (spleen removed as a bonus) in:
  1. Exocrine or endocrine pancreatic tumor at the tail with high likelihood of malignancy → remove the spleen as well
  2. Proximal gastric cancer
  3. Cancer at the LUQ, or retroperitoneal tumors such as sarcoma and adrenal tumors
  4. Large renal carcinoma, which often infiltrates the spleen

## Post Splenectomy
- After splenectomy, 3 problems arise:
  1. RBC morphology
  2. WBC & Plt.
  3. Immunization & OPSI
- **Post splenectomy RBC morphology**
  1. Howell Jolly bodies — nucleus remnant or nuclear fragment
  2. Heinz bodies — denatured Hb or hemoglobin deposition
  3. Pappenheimer bodies — iron granules in RBC; an RBC with iron deposit is called a siderocyte
  4. Target cells
  5. Spur cells or acanthocyte
  6. RBC count also rises (don't forget)
- **WBC & platelets**
  1. Leucocytosis — especially clear rise in lymphocytes and monocytes
  2. Thrombocytosis — in some cases over a million → needs ASA to prevent thrombosis
- **Immunization & OPSI**: remember IgM and properdin fall, not rise.
- **Summary — remember for exams**
  1. Splenectomy has no effect on RBC life span (still 120 days) and no effect on the RBC membrane.
  2. Schistocytes are pathologic cells of DIC and ITP (as printed), not a post-splenectomy feature.

## Overwhelming Postsplenectomy Infection (OPSI)
- Not common (remember: not common) — lifetime OPSI risk after splenectomy is only 1-5%; risk is highest in the first 2 years, then falls, but never to zero; OPSI has been reported 40 years after splenectomy.
- Mortality of OPSI is high: children 50%, adults 30% (children die more than adults).
- OPSI is now less frequent thanks to vaccination.
- **Risk of OPSI is higher in**
  1. Children < 5 years — the younger, the higher the risk
  2. Splenectomy for hematologic disease — first thalassemia, then sickle cell anemia; especially children < 3 years, who get OPSI more than item 3
  3. Compromised host or cancer patients
- Organisms: strep. pneumonia, H. flu, neisseria meningitides.
- Even with complete vaccination, not every strain is covered → OPSI can still occur.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ)
  - Early: non-specific insidious onset like a common cold, then rapid severe sepsis — pneumonia or meningitis — followed by DIC and shock, and death within 48 hours.
  - Remember: onset is slow, not sudden; but once sepsis starts, death is near (as printed: "book the temple").
- **Prevention**
  1. Avoid splenectomy in children < 4 years
  2. Try to salvage the spleen in splenic injury
  3. Vaccination
  4. Early detection — the most important method: advise splenectomy patients with a cold or diarrhea (possible OPSI) to see a doctor quickly
- **Antibiotic**
  - In the past, penicillin-group antibiotics were advised until age 18; now routine use is not widely agreed, except children < 5 years or splenectomy in a compromised host.
  - Schwartz 2019 p.1542 (spleen chapter) recommends:
    1. Antibiotics only for children after splenectomy
    2. Splenectomized children should carry a medical alert card
    3. Carry an antibiotic such as amoxyl for about 5 days; when the child has fever or sore throat, start it and see a doctor quickly
    4. If routine antibiotic is given, it should be for children ≤ 5 years, for no longer than 2 years; or take until age 5 and stop
- Splenic autotransplantation raises IgM but not enough to prevent OPSI. The topic came back because Moore EE ("godfather of Trauma") believes even small spleen pieces implanted in an omental pouch are better than no splenic tissue at all; it is still debated and not yet accepted.

## Vaccination
- **Pneumococcal vaccine (pneumovax)** covers strep. pneumonia up to 23 serotypes, or > 90% — so not every strain is covered (จำ). Booster every 5 years.
- **H. flu**: give, and booster once a year.
- **Meningococcus**: it was once debated whether routine use is worthwhile; now give it and repeat every 5 years.
- Current trend: give all 3, especially splenectomy in immunocompromised patients (e.g. HIV, chemo.) and children < 10 years.
- **Elective case**: vaccinate 2 wk before surgery — the most effective way to prevent OPSI.
- **Emergency case**: previously, vaccinate as soon as possible (within 1-2 days after splenectomy); current trend says wait 2 wk, because of concern about temporary immunosuppression associated with surgery; the author mostly follows the majority — 2 wk after emergency splenectomy.
- Figure: Vaccination timing after splenectomy, as steps —
  1. Elective → at least 2 weeks preoperatively: Pneumococcal PPV 23 vaccine; children < 2 years Pneumococcal 7 Valent vaccine; Haemophilus Influenza B; Meningococcal C vaccine.
  2. Emergency →
     - Immediate postoperative period: Pneumococcal PPV 23 vaccine; children < 2 years Pneumococcal 7 Valent vaccine.
     - At least 2 weeks postoperatively: Haemophilus Influenza B; Meningococcal C vaccine.
- **CDC 2016** (Shackelford 2019 p. 1596): same 3 vaccines after splenectomy; repeat pneumococcal and meningococcal every 5 years; Hib every year, unchanged.

## Splenectomy for Hematologic Disorders
- Surgery residents need not know as much as Med or Hemato doctors; this is written for memory and exams — only diseases likely to be examined — or at least so medical students cannot tease you, or if your partner is a Med doctor and you end up consulting each other (as printed).
- Disease groups where splenectomy has a role:
  1. RBC disorders
  2. WBC disorders
  3. Pletelet (as printed) disorders
  4. Bone marrow disorders
  5. Storage diseases

## RBC Disorders
- **Congenital** — 3 groups residents should know:
  1. RBC defect — usually named "Hereditary", e.g. hereditary spherocytosis, hereditary elliptocytosis
  2. Hb defect — Hb A deficit, e.g. thalassemia and sickle cell anemia; only thalassemia is covered here
  3. RBC enzyme deficiency — e.g. G6PD and PK deficiency; lacking the enzyme, RBC cannot utilize glucose
- **Acquired**: autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).
- **RBC defects where splenectomy has a role** — about 5 diseases; benefit differs by disease:
  1. Hereditary spherocytosis (HS) — must know well
  2. Hereditary elliptocytosis (HE)
  3. Hereditary pyropoikilocytosis (HP)
  4. Hereditary ovalocytosis (HO)
  5. Hereditary hydrocytosis (HH)
  - In HS splenectomy gives the best result (จำ); diseases 2-5 resemble HS but are milder, and splenectomy reduces hemolysis.
- **RBC defects where splenectomy gives no benefit** — 2 diseases:
  1. **Hereditary xerocytosis (HX)**: much potassium leaks out of RBC while little sodium enters → RBC shrinks and finally breaks. Splenectomy has no benefit in HX.
  2. **Hereditary high RBC phosphatidylcholine anemia (HPCHA)**: the author was too lazy to look it up (as printed); just remember splenectomy has no benefit and even worsens anemia (memorize at face value).

## Hereditary Spherocytosis (HS)
- Genetic disorder, AD inheritance; defect of the RBC membrane — lacks spectrin, ankyrin, ban 3 protein (as printed) → abnormal RBC shape: round instead of the normal biconcave disc.
- Round RBCs break easily; the spleen filters out and destroys these abnormal cells → short RBC life span.
- Found in young women, around northern Europe.
- **Clinical manifestation**
  1. Hemolytic anemia — pigmented gallstone up to 30-60%
  2. Mild jaundice
  3. Splenomegaly
  4. Leg ulcer
- Pigmented gallstones up to 41% → US of the gallbladder in every case.
- **Lab**: blood smear shows round RBCs, with reticulocytosis.
- **Treatment**: important — the hematologic disease where splenectomy gives the best result (จำ). Indications for splenectomy in HS:
  1. Moderate to severe hemolytic anemia
  2. Growth retardation
  3. Skeleton change and leg ulcer
- **Splenectomy**: curative; do it after age 4 to reduce OPSI risk, but if Hb < 6 it may need to be earlier; leg ulcers also heal faster after splenectomy. After splenectomy RBC are still round, but hemolysis and jaundice resolve as RBC life span returns to normal. In HS, if hemolysis persists after splenectomy → there must be an accessory spleen → nuclear scan, then remove the accessory spleen.
- **Cholecystectomy**: if preoperative US shows GS, do cholecystectomy as well; if no GS, prophylactic cholecystectomy is not needed.

## Thalassemia
- Hb defect, AD inheritance (as printed).
- Normal Hb has 3 chains — alpha, beta and gamma — which must be in balance; if any chain is reduced, Hb is unbalanced and RBC break easily.
- 2 forms:
  1. **Thalassemia major**: homozygous; RBC lack HbA but have excess HbF, so the RE system, especially the spleen, tries to clear these RBC → short RBC life; major affects both chains.
  2. **Thalassemia minor**: abnormality in one chain only; few or no symptoms.
- **Alpha thal**: incompatible with life — dies at birth before even being breast-fed, so there is no chance for splenectomy. Conclusion: splenectomy has no role in alpha thal. (จำ)
- **Beta thal**: the common form; homozygous, from lack of beta chain. Classic appearance: pale, short, big head, yellow, distended abdomen. Hb electrophoresis: little HbA but much HbF. Blood smear: hypochromic microcytic anemia.
- **Treatment**
  1. Transfusion with iron chelating agent because of iron overload
  2. Splenectomy — indications:
     - 2.1 Transfusion > 200 cc/kg/year
     - 2.2 Discomfort splenomegaly
     - 2.3 Painful splenic infarction
- Total splenectomy is usual; partial splenectomy has lower mortality but is still only a trial.
- Result of splenectomy: less RBC hemolysis and less transfusion need, but the natural disease does not change (จำ), and patients often die from hemosiderosis.

## G6PD Deficiency
- RBC lack G6PD enzyme → cannot use glucose.
- Predisposing factors such as infection, drugs, fava bean → RBC break easily.
- In G6PD the spleen is not the cause of RBC hemolysis; the spleen is normal and of normal size (ต้องจำ).
- **Diagnosis**: enzyme study. Again — if the spleen is large, do not think of this disease.
- **Treatment**: dietary counseling about foods or drugs that cause RBC hemolysis — avoid mothballs (as printed).
- Splenectomy gives no benefit (ต้องจำ).

## Pyruvate Kinase (PK) Deficiency
- AR inheritance (จำ) — unusual, since most surgical inherited diseases are AD.
- Rare disease, never seen in the author's career; know it for MCQ.
- Defect in the glycolytic pathway → RBC lack energy.
- Patients are pale with splenomegaly, whereas in G6PD the spleen is not enlarged.
- Splenomegaly causes pressure effect and hypersplenism, so splenectomy is beneficial in PK deficiency.

## Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)
- Autoantibodies destroy the patient's own RBC → short RBC life; the abnormal antibody is IgG made by the spleen; some cases remit spontaneously.
- Usually women, middle age (40-70 years); 50% idiopathic or primary.
- Secondary AIHA may come from infection, malignancy or collagen disease.
- **Clinical manifestation**
  1. Anemia — late, not early (จำ)
  2. Hemolytic jaundice — LFT shows high indirect bilirubin, not direct (จำ)
  3. Splenomegaly 30-50%
  4. Gallstone 25%
- **Abnormal autoantibody** — 2 types causing AIHA:
  1. **IgG**: normally the spleen should not make it; in AIHA the spleen makes much IgG that binds RBC at normal or warm temperature = **warm-reactive antibody** → RBC destroyed in the spleen (จำ).
  2. **IgM**: binds RBC at low temperature = **cold-reactive antibody** → RBC destroyed in the liver.
- **Lab**: anemia, reticulocytosis, Comb test +ve (as printed).
- **Treatment**
  1. **Steroid**: initial treatment, dose up to 2 mg/kg/day; hemolysis usually improves within 3 wk, but about 30% fail.
  2. Secondary AIHA: also treat the cause.
  3. **Splenectomy**: for steroid failure with no remission within 3 weeks, or when steroid cannot be continued, and only for warm antibody IgG; post-splenectomy success 80%.
- Summary: cold AHIA (as printed) — splenectomy does not work (จำ).

## WBC Disorders
- Residents need to know 2 big groups:
  1. **Leukemia** — directly from WBC, e.g. chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML): very large spleen → pressure effect, pain, early satiety and hypersplenism, so splenectomy in CLL surely helps. Hairy cell leukemia (HCL) used to be treated with splenectomy for massive splenomegaly; now purine analogs (pentostatin, cladribine) are the treatment of choice, with very high remission and spleen shrinkage 80-90% — better than splenectomy.
  2. **Lymphoma** — Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin.

## Hodgkin's Lymphoma
- Reed Sternberg cell — just remember the name.
- Most common type: nodular sclerosis.
- Best prognosis: lymphocytic predominant.
- Worst prognosis: lymphocytic depletion.
- **Staging** (know a little)
  - Stage 1: LN involvement in 1-2 areas close together, on the same side of the diaphragm
  - Stage 2: LN involvement in 2 areas apart, but on the same side of the diaphragm
  - Stage 3: LN involvement on both sides of the diaphragm; spleen involvement is stage 3
  - Stage 4: +ve in liver, lung, bone, or other non-lymphoid tissue except spleen
- Patients have enlarged nodes in neck, axilla or groin that are not very painful, like TB.
- Staging also has A and B: A = no symptoms; B = fever, night sweat, weight loss.
- **Staging methods** — 2:
  1. **Nonoperative staging**: CT, PET scan, lymphangiogram and BM biopsy.
  2. **Operative staging**: in the past every case had open exploration; now only when HL is suspected but nonoperative staging is inconclusive — lap. is recommended, for liver biopsy, splenectomy and LN biopsy at the retroperitoneum, mesentery and hepatoduodenal ligament. Either way it is a gamble — take risk.
- Figure: Staging laparotomy — liver needle and wedge biopsy, iliac crest bone biopsy, splenectomy, nodal sampling (portal, celiac, splenic, mesenteric, aortic, iliac) marked with silver clips, and midline oophoropexy.
- Why so much effort for accurate staging: in the past stage 1-2 got RT, stage 3-4 got chemo; doctors feared chemo toxicity and avoided chemo if not necessary.
- Now, with CT, PET and highly effective chemotherapy, chemo is given even in stage 1-2; since chemo is given at any stage, operative staging seems excessive.
- Conclusion: laparoscopic staging for HD is rarely used; with excellent imaging, operative staging remains only as pictures in textbooks.
- Summary: splenectomy for staging in HD no longer works (จำ).

## Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma (NHL)
- Prognosis worse than Hodgkin; when NHL is diagnosed it has usually spread, and most need chemo anyway — so staging splenectomy has no benefit in NHL (จำ).
- Indication for splenectomy in NHL is treatment of splenomegaly and hypersplenism, not staging (จำอีกครั้ง).

## Platelet Disorders
- 2 main diseases:
  1. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) — rarely seen; not knowing it is fine
  2. Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) — often examined; you must know ITP
- Review: basic hemostasis by Plt. level
  1. > 50,000: rarely bleeds
  2. 30,000-50,000: early bruising
  3. 10,000-30,000: ecchymosis or spontaneous petechiae
  4. < 10,000: risk of internal bleeding, and 1% get intracerebral hemorrhage

## Idiopathic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)
- Autoimmune disorder: low Plt. because autoantibodies made by the spleen destroy Plt.; Plt. production is still normal. Remember: the spleen is not enlarged in ITP.
- Types:
  1. **Primary** — no known cause
  2. **Secondary** — from SLE, HIV, lymphoproliferative disorder, hepatitis C, antiphospholipid syndrome, and drugs such as heroin and quinidine
- **Lab**: low blood plt, but BM shows increased megakaryocytes.
- **Childhood ITP (primary ITP, acute ITP)**
  - Girls about equal to boys (about 50%), whereas adult ITP is mostly women; peak at age 2-5 years; fairly acute petechiae or purpura after an infectious process, especially viral infection.
  - 85% of childhood ITP resolves within 2-6 months. If Plt > 30,000, advise avoiding anti-platelet drugs and accidents only — no admission or drugs needed; if Plt < 30,000 persistently, give steroid.
  - **Treatment** is controversial because primary ITP usually resolves by itself; special treatment is needed in few cases, ≤ 2%, with very low Plt. (< 5,000) at risk of intracranial bleeding, or children whose activity must be restricted so much it affects quality of life → steroid with IV. immunoglobulin (IVIG) for 2-3 days; few cases still need splenectomy.
  - Summary: in childhood ITP, the chance of splenectomy is very low.
- **Adult ITP (secondary ITP)** (as printed)
  - Mostly women, age 18-40 years; fairly chronic, insidious onset, unlike childhood ITP; adult ITP usually needs steroid if Plt. persistently < 30,000; if Plt. > 30,000, observe (repeated again).
  - Spontaneous remission in adult ITP only 2%, and 50% of adult ITP have Plt. < 10,000 — high risk.
  - **Treatment**
    1. **First line**: oral prednisolone — fairly good; Plt. usually > 100,000 (เกินแสน) within 3 wk. If Plt. does not rise on steroid, or there is internal bleeding, or Plt. < 5,000 → add IVIG or IV. immune globulin G 1 gm/kg/day for 2-3 days; response rate up to 80%. Newer drugs: thrombopoietin receptor agonist with retuximab (as printed), response rate 63-80%; plasmaphreresis (as printed) and metrotrexate (as printed), once used, are now out of trend.
    2. **Splenectomy**: long-term remission rate 66-85%. Indications:
       - 2.1 Prednisolone > 10-20 mg/day for > 3-6 months and still cannot stop — Plt. falls < 30,000 (accepted level for ITP) every time the drug is stopped
       - 2.2 Cannot take steroid, e.g. drug complications
       - 2.3 Patient wants surgery (patient preference)
       - 2.4 Neurological symptoms, especially intracerebral hemorrhage → emergency splenectomy: give IV. methylprednisolone, IVIG and Plt. conc, then straight to OR for splenectomy
- **Elective splenectomy for adult ITP** — good results expected with:
  1. Short interval between diagnosis and operation
  2. Initial response to steroid therapy
  3. +ve HIV status
  4. High preoperative Plt. count — before surgery Plt. > 10,000
  5. Young age (< 40 years) — believed to be the best preop. indicator (จำ)
- **Tips & technique for splenectomy in ITP**
  1. Preoperative Plt. not needed — the spleen destroys it all
  2. Give Plt. after the splenic artery is ligated
  3. Look for accessory spleen; remove all found
  4. Plt. rises within 1-3 days after surgery
  5. Taper steroid gradually; off steroid in about 4-6 wk
- If Plt. still does not rise after splenectomy: try prednisolone and IVIG first, especially in the elderly who often respond poorly; then localize with Tc scan or CT for an assessory (as printed) spleen; if present, remove it. Going straight to surgery without investigation is wrong.

## Bone Marrow Disorders
- Problem in this group: splenomegaly causing infarction and pressure effect, so splenectomy has a role in all of them. Just remember the names; knowing more may get you confused, or if you are too clever, hematologists may be annoyed (as printed).
- **Bone marrow disorders where splenectomy helps**
  1. CML
  2. AML
  3. CMML
  4. ET (Essential Thrombocytopenia) (as printed)
  5. PV (Polycythemia Vera)
  6. MM (Myeloid Metaplasia)
- **CLL** (chronic lymphocytic leukemia): cancer of lymphocytes.
- **CML** (chronic myeloid leukemia): cancer of myeloid cells and granulocytes in the bone marrow.

## Storage Diseases
- Surgical textbooks list about 5; all have splenomegaly, so splenectomy helps through pressure effect and hypersplenism.
- **Storage diseases where splenectomy helps** (remember the names):
  1. Felty's syndrome — written in more detail, likely examined
  2. Gaucher's disease — Schwartz recommends only partial splenectomy if splenectomy is done; knowing this is enough
  3. Neimann Pick (as printed)
  4. Amyloidosis
  5. Sarcoidosis

## Felty's Syndrome
- **Triad**
  1. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)
  2. Splenomegaly
  3. Autoimmune neutropenia
- Plt. is normal; what is low is neutrophil — thought to be abnormal IgG antibody against neutrophils → the spleen destroys neutrophils rapidly; low neutrophils → infections easily.
- Common in women; some have leg ulcers; 1% of RA patients develop Felty.
- **Treatment**
  1. Hematopoietic growth factors — initial treatment, usually quick response
  2. Indications for splenectomy:
     - 2.1 Failure of medical treatment with recurrent infection
     - 2.2 Splenomegaly
     - 2.3 Severe neutropenia — neutrophil < 2,000/mm³ (as printed)
- Very good response after splenectomy: neutrophils rise within 2-3 days and leg ulcers heal.

## Splenic Cysts
- Very rare; 2 groups: parasitic cyst from echinococcal cyst (the most common cause) and nonparasitic cyst.
- **Nonparasitic cyst**
  1. **Primary or congenital cyst (20%)**: most are simple cyst or epidermal cyst
  2. **Secondary cyst (80%)**: usually post-trauma, though about 30% may have no trauma history; these are pseudocysts
- **Treatment**: based on cyst type and symptoms; if surgery is needed, lap. is now preferred over open.
  1. **Parasitic cyst**: medical treatment (albendazole) with splenectomy (best), or PAIR as in the liver
  2. **Neoplastic or dermoid cyst**: total splenectomy
  3. **Simple cyst**: asymptomatic and < 5 cm → conservative with periodic follow-up; resolution has been reported (จำ). Symptomatic or > 5 cm → treat:
     - 3.1 Superficial cyst → unroof, but may recur
     - 3.2 Most favour laparoscopic partial splenectomy including the cyst
     - Note (หมายเหตุ): do not do percutaneous drainage — frequent recurrence even with sclerosing agent injected into the cyst; it also causes subsequent inflammatory reaction in the LUQ, making later splenectomy difficult.
     - 3.3 Avoid total splenectomy, except for multiple cysts or a cyst so large the spleen cannot be preserved.
- Summary: asymptomatic simple splenic cyst < 5 cm → no treatment (จำ). Symptomatic or > 5 cm → partial splenectomy first, by lap before open.
- Greenfield gives the operative indication as size > 10 cm, but most authorities believe 5 cm should be operated; the author agrees with the majority.

## Splenic Tumors
- Very rare tumors.
- **Primary**
  1. **Benign**: most common is hemangioma, with typical MRI features as in the liver; no treatment if asymptomatic.
  2. **Malignant**: usually sarcoma or NHL; resect if resectable; for NHL see WBC disorders above.
- **Secondary**: uncommon; metastatic cancer to the spleen — number 1 is from lung. Treatment: palliative care — pray, or prepare to go and be with God (as printed) (จำ).

## Splenic Artery Aneurysm (SAA)
- **Incidence**: next after AAA and iliac A. aneurysm.
- Among visceral aneurysms, SAA is number 1 (60%); usual site: mid to distal third.
- F : M = 4 : 1.
- **Causes** (สาเหตุ): in women, medial dysplasia; in men, atherosclerosis; now SAA is seen more in splenic trauma patients treated by nonoperative Rx.
- **Diagnosis**: mostly incidental on imaging.
- **Risk of rupture**: 3-9%; likes to rupture during pregnancy, as with hepatic adenoma.
- SAA rupture in pregnancy: maternal death 70%, fetal death 90%; whoever is on duty is likely to be sued — damages of 20 million (as printed).
- SAA rupture when not pregnant: chance of death about 30-50%.
- **Indications for surgery**
  1. Presence of symptoms
  2. Pregnancy or child bearing potential
  3. Pseudoaneurysm post pancreatitis — thin wall, ruptures easily
  4. Size > 2 cm (Schwartz); Shackelford uses > 2.5 cm. Memorize the size because < 2 cm without symptoms needs no surgery.
- **Treatment** (following Schwartz first)
  1. **Proximal aneurysm**: excision and ligation of the distal splenic A.; if the aneurysm is large and hard to mobilize or ligate, open the aneurysm and suture the ostia from inside.
  2. **Mid splenic aneurysm**: proximal and distal ligation of the splenic A. and all collateral vessels. In 1 and 2 the spleen still gets blood supply via the short gastric A.
  3. **Distal or hilar aneurysm**: aneurysmectomy with splenectomy.
  4. **Embolization**: for high-risk patients who cannot tolerate surgery; drawback is splenic infarction.
  5. **Ruptured SAA**: resuscitate quickly, then emergency splenectomy; do not send for embolization — the patient may die on the intervention table.
- Summary: proximal and mid SAA do not need splenectomy — aneurysmectomy with distal ligation is enough (จำ).
- Now endovascular Rx. with coil embolization or covered stent across the aneurysm gives very good results; Shackelford 2013 recommends trying endovascular Rx. first, then surgery if it fails (in theory the author agrees with this approach).

## Splenic Vein Thrombosis
- Secondary to acute pancreatitis, especially GS pancreatitis, or pancreatic neoplasm.
- Can come from hematologic disease, but less often than from AP.
- Suspect in pancreatitis patients presenting with UGIH and splenomegaly not due to liver or hematologic disease.
- 50% have gastric varices > esophageal varices, but chance of bleeding is no more than 10%.
- **CT**: thrombus in the splenic vein.
- **Upper endoscope**: shows gastric varices; whether every splenic vein thrombosis patient needs endoscopy is still debated, since non-bleeding varices need no treatment anyway. The author's personal view: scope, because varices at high bleeding risk, e.g. with red wale marking, warrant caution or favour splenectomy.
- **Asymptomatic case** — whether to do prophylactic splenectomy: Greenfield favours splenectomy, as followed-up case series showed bleeding in up to 50%.
- Recent study: bleeding from gastric varices only 4-18%. Splenectomy recommended when:
  1. Bleeding from gastric varice
  2. Laparotomy for another cause anyway → remove the spleen as well
  3. Endoscopic feature of red wale marking
- No indication for splenectomy → Rx. observation is enough (จำ).
- High-risk patients who cannot tolerate surgery: splenic artery embolization is another option, but splenic infarction or abscess may occur in up to 25%.
- Anticoagulation does not reduce risk of variceal bleeding and may promote bleeding — more harm than good.

## Hypersplenism
- The spleen destroys or sequestrates circulating RBC, WBC and platelets → anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly.
- **Primary hypersplenism**: very rare; unrelated to hemolytic process — the spleen becomes overactive for no reason and destroys its own blood cells. Diagnose only after ruling out secondary causes. For primary hypersplenism, treatment is splenectomy (จำ).
- **Secondary hypersplenism**: many causes; most common are portal hypertension and CHF with high venous pressure; also malignancy such as leukemia, infections such as AIDS, and storage disease. Principle of treatment: treat the cause first; splenectomy may be needed for symptomatic hypersplenism.
- **Portal hypertension with secondary hypersplenism**
  1. If caused by splenic vein thrombosis — repeated again and again — splenectomy cures, because it fixes both hypersplenism and gastric varices.
  2. Portal hypertension from other causes: splenectomy alone does not work (จำ) — esophageal varices do not collapse and portal pressure stays high.

## Splenic Abscess
- Uncommon; incidence 0.14-0.7%.
- **Pathogenesis**
  1. **Hematogenous spreading**: the most common route; most common cause is bacterial endocarditis (usually strep and staph); next diverticulitis, IV. drug abuse, typhoid, UTI, malaria, osteomyelitis
  2. **Direct spreading**: from an adjacent intraabdominal source such as colon or kidney infection. Acute pancreatitis, especially edematous type, has no organisms, so it is unlikely to cause splenic abscess, except infectious pancreatic necrosis
  3. **Postsplenic trauma** treated non-operatively → infected hematoma ends up as abscess
  4. Hematologic disease: polycythemia vera, sickle cell anemia
  5. **Malignancy**
  6. **AIDS**
- **Suggestive features** (อาการชวนสงสัย): fever or signs of sepsis in a drug addict, LUQ pain, splenomegaly in 1 of 3 cases, raised WBC.
- Rarely thought of → diagnosis often delayed, average 18-22 days.
- Pathogens mostly aerobes: E.coli and Strep.
- Prognosis of multilocular abscess is worse than unilocular.
- **Imaging studies**
  1. Plain film: with luck, gas in the spleen
  2. US or CT: best is CT — fluid-filled cavity, gas in the cavity, wall enhancement; sensitivity and specificity up to 95%
- **Treatment**
  1. Start IV antibiotics, then open total splenectomy + LUQ drainage — splenectomy is the best treatment; then PO. antibiotic about 14 days.
  2. Very ill patient unfit for surgery with unilocular abscess → may try needle aspiration with cath. drain; success no more than 50%.
  3. Endocarditis with splenic abscess → splenectomy first; if the patient survives, treat the heart later.

## Spontaneous Rupture
- Usually in patients with splenomegaly from infection or hematologic disease; no trauma history; sudden shock with LUQ pain radiating to the left shoulder, or +ve Kehr's sign.
- **CT**: features of splenic rupture.
- **Causes**
  1. Most common in USA: infectious mononucleosis
  2. Most common in Thai: malaria — in exams, answer malaria
  3. Others: sarcoidosis, leukemia, polycythemia vera
- **Treatment**: splenectomy.

## Wandering Spleen
- Lax suspensory ligaments → the spleen swings and lies in an abnormal position; usually women aged 20-40 years; mostly asymptomatic.
- **Diagnosis**: CT abdomen.
- Asymptomatic → observe. With pain → consider repairing the suspensory ligaments = splenopexy, fixing the spleen to the diaphragm, abdominal wall or omentum in its normal position; popular in children; in adults splenectomy is recommended.
- Torsion at the pedicle with infarction → usually emergency pain; then the spleen must be removed.

## Summary Before the End (สรุป ก่อนจะจบ)
- **"Most common" questions**
  1. Intraabdominal organ injury in blunt abdominal trauma: spleen
  2. Anomaly: accessory spleen
  3. Location of accessory spleen: hilum
  4. Indication for emergency splenectomy: trauma; for elective cases: splenectomy in ITP
  5. Best result after splenectomy: hereditary spherocytosis
  6. Complication of splenectomy: atelectasis
- **Always need for splenectomy**
  1. Hereditary spherocytosis
  2. Echinococcal cyst
  3. Splenic vein thrombosis
- More than 100 diseases benefit from splenectomy — too many to remember. For the Board exam, residents should remember only the diseases where splenectomy has no role — about 14:
  1. Hereditary xerocytosis (HX)
  2. Hereditary high RBC phosphatidyl choline hemolytic anemia (HPCHA)
  3. Alpha thalassemia
  4. G6PD deficiency
  5. Cold AIHA
  6. Hodgkin and Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma — for staging only
  7. Primary ITP or Childhood ITP
  8. Asymptomatic simple cyst < 5 cm
  9. Metastatic cancer to the spleen
  10. Proximal and mid splenic artery aneurysm
  11. Splenic vein thrombosis with gastric varices that do not bleed
  12. Portal hypertension causing secondary hypersplenism
  13. Asymptomatic wandering spleen
  14. Sickle cell disease — autosplenectomy is common, so before booking splenectomy check whether there is still a spleen to remove
- Hematologic diseases without splenomegaly: ITP and G6PD deficiency — if the spleen is large, it is not these diseases.
- Inherited diseases of the spleen are AD 99.99% — you can guess this.
