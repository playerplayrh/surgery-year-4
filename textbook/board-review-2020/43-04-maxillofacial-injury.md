# Maxillofacial Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 155-202 (PDF pages 2289-2336) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated ๒๙ พฤศจิกายน ๒๕๕๙)

## Introduction
- Injury in this region covers the head, neck, oral cavity and nasal cavity; this chapter covers only soft tissue and bone injury — enough for an idea and, the author expects, for exams.
- For more depth, consider a fellowship with อาจารย์ปรีชา ศิริทองถาวร at Siriraj, the real expert on ATLS and maxillofacial injury.

## Basic Anatomy for General Surgical Residents
- Just what a surgery resident should know, so the injury sections make sense and can be pictured.

## Facial Bone
- 5 main bones: frontal, nasal, zygoma, maxilla and mandible.
- Facial bones join at suture lines bound by fibrous tissue and do not move = **fibrous joint**.
- Exception: the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) of the mandible is a **synovial joint**, so it moves.
- Face is divided into 3 parts:
  1. Upper 1/3 — frontal bone
  2. Middle 1/3 — midface (trace a finger over your own face to feel it)
  3. Lower 1/3 — mandible
- Facial bones often contain air sinuses, making them light, and foramina through which artery, vein and nerve exit.

## Nasal Cartilage
- The nasal cavity is divided by the nasal septum into left and right nostrils.
- The nose has 2 cartilages:
  1. **Lateral cartilage** — continuous with the septal cartilage, extending from the nasal bone
  2. **Alar cartilage** — below the lateral cartilage; forms the ala (nasal wing)

## Cranial Nerve
- **CN 1** — smell; total loss of smell suggests fracture base of skull at the anterior fossa.
- **CN 2** — vision; must be assessed before every operation (if the patient saw well before surgery and is blind afterwards, expect trouble).
- **CN 3, 4, 6** — control extraocular muscle movement.
- **CN 5** — both motor and sensory:
  1. Motor branch — muscles of mastication
  2. Sensory branch — worth attention (exam topic); reviewed briefly for anatomy. Sensation of the face, in 3 divisions:
     - 2.1 **V1 (ophthalmic division)** — forehead
     - 2.2 **V2 (maxillary division)** — cheek, nose, upper lip, upper teeth and gum. Key branch: **infraorbital nerve**; compression from fracture zygoma → numbness in this area.
     - 2.3 **V3 (mandibular division)** — lower face: lower lip, lower teeth and lower gum. Key branch: inferior alveolar nerve (inferior dental nerve) / **mental nerve**, exiting the mental foramen; may be injured by fracture mandible → numbness of the lower lip and lower face (จำ).
  - Note (หมายเหตุ): as printed, a joke that a resident with V1-V3 neurapraxia would feel nothing when scolded at mortality conference.
- **CN 7** — nerve of facial expression; exits the stylomastoid foramen, then divides into 5 branches:
  1. Frontal
  2. Zygomatic
  3. Buccal — runs parallel with Stensen duct
  4. Mandibular — runs along the lower border of the mandible, crosses the facial vein, supplies the angle of the mouth
  5. Cervical
- **CN 12** — supplies the tongue muscles: movement for chewing, swallowing, spitting, speech (as printed, plus a joke about flattering the boss; with V1-V3 palsy as well, the face becomes shameless — "fit for politics").
- **Taste**
  1. Chorda tympani of CN7 — anterior 2/3
  2. Glossopharyngeal nerve — posterior 1/3

## Imaging for Maxillofacial Injury
- **Film skull** — full set = 4 views:
  1. **AP** — midface; also body and ramus of mandible
  2. **Lateral** — nasal bone
  3. **Towne** — occipital skull, foramen magnum and condyle of mandible
  4. **Waters** — shows almost every facial bone, especially orbit and zygoma, which other views show poorly; if only one best view, choose **Waters view**
- **Film panoramic view** (printed "paronamic") — best for the mandible: rotating x-ray tube shows both sides of the mandible and all teeth. The patient must be able to stand, so it is unsuitable in the early phase (จำ).
- **Film C-spine** — severe maxillofacial injury or a drowsy patient may have fracture C-spine (~1.3%), so send C-spine film as well.

## CT Scan for Maxillofacial Injury
- 3D-CT is now available and gives far more information than plain film.
- But: high cost, limited availability, contrast risk, and ordinary facial fractures can be diagnosed from plain film with enough information for treatment → CT should be selective.
- Use CT for complex, difficult injuries, e.g. midface fracture, orbital fracture, or patients with brain injury (the brain gets imaged at the same time).
- Request both axial and coronal views.

## Management of Maxillofacial Injury
- Focus on indications for definitive airway and on good bleeding control.
- Almost everywhere in Thailand, on weekend nights after midnight, teenage gangs turn highways into racetracks, so on-call surgeons see many more head and maxillofacial injuries.
- Residents trained in Bangkok may see fewer of these cases than those in Chonburi, Korat or Ubon.
- This chapter aims to give enough basics for initial management and for handling soft tissue and simple facial fractures (e.g. nose or mandible) yourself, since there may be no plastic surgeon nearby.

## Initial Assessment and Resuscitation
- Emergencies needing urgent treatment: upper airway obstruction and bleeding. Principle: follow ATLS.
- **Position**
  1. Conscious patient: sit leaning forward, head bowed, or lie on the side, so blood does not run down the throat.
  2. Drowsy patient: maintain and clear the airway first, before strapping or restraining the patient.
- Segmental or parasymphysis fracture of the mandible: beware the tongue falling back and blocking the upper airway; often a towel clip is needed to pull the tongue out of the mouth.
- Endotracheal intubation is somewhat harder but feasible. Beware blind nasotracheal intubation — needs a skilled operator.
- **Do not use blind nasotracheal tube in:**
  1. Apnea, unconscious
  2. Fracture base of skull
  3. Fracture maxilla — the tube pushes the fracture apart and down → profuse bleeding
- If oral intubation fails, do not persist — do cricothyroidotomy.
- During definitive airway, beware C-spine injury:
  1. Maxillofacial injury has associated C-spine injury in 1.3%
  2. Fracture mandible — associated C-spine injury at C1-C4
  3. Fracture maxilla — associated C-spine injury at C5-C7
- So do not look only at the face; protect the C-spine as well.

## Indication for Definitive Airway
1. As for other injuries, e.g. GCS ≤ 8, agitation, cyanosis → intubate.
2. Laryngeal or tracheal injury: noisy breathing like stridor, palpable crepitus at the larynx.
3. Site of facial fracture:
   - 3.1 Parasymphysis fracture of the mandible — tongue falls back and blocks the upper airway
   - 3.2 Midface fracture, e.g. fracture maxilla — beware severe bleeding; often needs a tube plus nasal packing
4. Long-distance referral → intubate before transfer.

## Indication for Tracheostomy
1. Severe head injury needing IMF (intermaxillary fixation) or wiring of the teeth
2. Massive neck swelling where a tube cannot be passed
3. Nasopharynx, larynx or tracheal injury

## Bleeding Maxillofacial Injury
- **Bleeding from soft tissue**
  1. Scalp — simple suturing usually stops it
  2. Face — if bleeding is heavy and there are more serious problems, wrap the face with a bandage first; **no blind clamp**
- **Bleeding from facial bone fracture** can be stopped by:
  1. Compressing the bony fragments together
  2. Fixation with plate or IMF
  3. Angiographic embolization
- The real challenge (needs a senior-level hand) is bleeding from fracture maxilla — midface fracture bleeding pours from both mouth and nose.
- **Stopping severe bleeding from fracture maxilla:**
  1. Anterior nasal packing stops profuse epistaxis (printed "epitasis")
  2. If anterior packing fails, the bleeding is from behind → Foley into both nostrils, inflate the balloon and pull back; bleeding should lessen or stop
  3. Gauze over the face, then elastic bandage wrapped Barton-style, pulling the jaw up toward the head, to compress the fracture temporarily
  4. Still uncontrolled → wake the radiologist for embolization
- Ligating the external carotid artery to stop bleeding **does not work** — many anastomoses with branches of the internal carotid artery.
- Anterior and posterior nasal packing should not stay > 24 hours — raises infection risk.
- **Secondary survey** — once the crisis has passed:
  1. Examine skin and soft tissue of face, scalp and neck
  2. Examine facial bones, nerves, eyes and vision
  3. Check ears and nose thoroughly; clear fluid from the nose may be CSF → **no NG tube or nasotracheal tube**
- Watch vision closely: if vision was good initially and is lost later, think retrobulbar hemorrhage or optic nerve compression → treat urgently or the patient goes blind; get help quickly (ophthalmologist, neurosurgeon); if none available, referral to a center is safer.
- Principle: serious associated injuries (e.g. epidural hematoma, hemothorax, intraabdominal bleeding) are more urgent than the maxillofacial injury — treat them first.

## Management of Skin and Soft Tissue Injury
- Test facial muscles/nerves before local anesthetic or sedation — afterwards nothing can be examined.
- **Abrasion**
  - Treat like a burn: clean with NSS, remove all foreign bodies to avoid traumatic tattoo; wait for epithelial cells from skin appendages to cover the wound. Most abrasions heal within 5-7 days.
- **Laceration**
  1. Irrigate with plenty of NSS, remove as much foreign material as possible
  2. Debride only as needed; keep anything doubtful or bruised, because blood supply here is quite good
  3. Approximation: find landmarks first (eyebrow margin, hair line, vermillion border) and place key stitches
  4. Deep wounds: close the subcutaneous layer — on the face fine plain gut is acceptable, good for fast-healing areas or children's faces where you do not want to remove stitches (as printed, children cry just at the sight of you). Plain catgut loses strength by 7 days. Chromic suits wounds in the mouth or nose, as does vicryl/polyglactin, which absorbs in 3 weeks.
  5. Outer skin: nylon 5-0 or 6-0, continuous or interrupted; remove stitches by day 5 to reduce scarring.
- **Avulsion with skin loss**
  - After good debridement and wound care, skin graft or flap later:
  1. Face: donor skin from the postauricular area first; if not enough, supraclavicular area. Graft should be full thickness — takes less easily but looks better than partial thickness, which contracts up to 40%.
  2. Hand or arm: donor skin usually from the hip flexion crease at the groin; alternatively antecubital crease or inner arm — disadvantage: more scarring, ugly.
  3. **Hematoma**
     - Early: cold compression usually resolves it — except where there is cartilage (ear, nose):
     1. Hematoma of the ear — do not leave it; the ear becomes deformed like a cauliflower (cauliflower ear deformity)
     2. Hematoma of the nasal septum (จำ) — also needs I&D, otherwise the cartilage resorbs and collapses (saddle nose deformity, "like a pug")
     - Treatment: I&D through the mucosa of the nasal septum, express all hematoma, then anterior nasal packing as a splint.

## Injury in Special Area
- **Scalp wound**
  - No need to shave: an RCT showed infection rates are no different whether hair is shaved or not; suture directly.
  - Before suturing, palpate for skull fracture (depressed vs linear). If there is a lot of foreign material, suture in the OR rather than struggle in the ER.
  - Close galea with subcutaneous tissue using vicryl 3-0; outer skin with nylon 3-0 or staples.
- **Brow (คิ้ว) wound**
  - Never shave the eyebrow — its line is the landmark for suturing. Inner layer vicryl 4-0 or 5-0; skin nylon 5-0 or 6-0.
- **Eyelid wound**
  - Torn muscle or tarsal plate → repair with absorbable vicryl or chromic 4-0; skin nylon 6-0, with edges level — overlapping edges cause a notched, distorted eyelid.
  - Place the knots on the outside, not the inside, as they irritate the conjunctiva.
- **Medial canthus wound**
  - Do not forget the lacrimal duct, especially the lower canaliculi, which open at the lower punctum.
  - Tears drain via the nasolacrimal duct into the inferior meatus of the nose; a cut duct left unrepaired → constant tearing = **epiphora**.
  - Test for duct transection: pass nylon 4-0 into the lower punctum; if its tip appears in the wound, the duct is cut.
  - Repair: chromic 5-0 or 6-0 over a silicone tube left as a stent for 1 month.
  - Upper canaliculi: if cut, no repair needed.
- **Ear wound**
  - Through-and-through cartilage defect: suture cartilage with vicryl 5-0, skin with nylon 5-0 or 6-0. Cartilage heals by fibrosis; edges aligned without overlap give a neat, non-lumpy scar.
- **Nose wound**
  - Torn mucosa or cartilage: repair as for the ear; remove stitches within 5 days.
- **Parotid duct**
  - Gland wound: suture and drain. The problem is a cheek wound leaking clear fluid — need to know whether Stensen duct is cut.
  - Figure: Structures in the cheek — the parotid gland lies under the middle third of a line between the tragus of the ear and the commissure of the lips; division of facial nerve branches anterior to a vertical line (drawn down from the lateral canthus) causes minimal deformity.
  - **Finding the parotid (Stensen) duct**
    1. Externally the duct lies 1.5 cm below the zygomatic arch, or along an imaginary line from the midpoint of the upper lip to the tragus.
    2. Internally it runs backward, pierces the buccinator muscle and opens into the mouth opposite the upper second molar teeth.
    3. Probe from the oral opening with nylon 3-0 or a small silicone tube; the opening is a raised papilla, more prominent than the surrounding mucosa.
  - **Repair of parotid duct**
    1. If repairable: chromic 6-0 over a stent left 14 days.
    2. If not repairable:
       - 2.1 Create a new opening into the mouth, or
       - 2.2 Ligate the proximal end (gland side): saliva stops, the gland atrophies, and the resulting scar helps seal the leak.
- **Lip wound**
  - Suture the vermillion border first — the junction of the dark-red lip (vermillion) with the paler inner mucosa. Inner oral mucosa chromic 5-0, muscle vicryl 5-0, skin nylon 5-0.
- **Facial nerve injury**
  - Causes: temporal bone fracture, penetrating injury, iatrogenic injury.
  - **Principles of treatment**
    1. Use the midpupil line (vertical line down from the centre of the pupil or the lateral canthus):
       - 1.1 Facial nerve cut medial/anterior to the midpupil line (toward the nose) = distal small branches → no repair needed; other small branches compensate.
       - 1.2 Cut proximal/posterior to the midpupil line → must repair.
    2. Temporal branch injury gives a major functional defect (patient cannot close the eye) → of the 5 branches of CN 7, the temporal branch most needs repair.
    3. Nerve repair: nylon 6-0 to 8-0, epineurial suture end to end; if the gap is large, use the great auricular nerve as an interposition graft.
- **Tongue wound**
  - Repair the muscle well and stop bleeding — a swollen tongue can cause airway obstruction.

## Maxillofacial Fracture
- Suspect facial fracture with:
  1. Pain over facial bones, cannot open the mouth, or teeth do not meet properly
  2. Deformity, e.g. depressed nasal bridge
  3. Palpable crepitus or step deformity (like a stair step)
- **Diagnosis** — mainly by physical examination:
  1. Eyes — rough vision check, diplopia, eye movement
  2. Globe position — enophthalmos is found in orbital floor fracture; exophthalmos after trauma → think of retro-orbital bleeding or carotid cavernous fistula
  3. Ask the patient to open and close the mouth; check dental occlusion
  4. Others — see each facial fracture type later in this chapter
- **Principles of management**
  1. Treat life-threatening problems first per ATLS. Early on the face is swollen and hard to examine; if unsure, wait for swelling to settle and re-examine later.
  2. Once out of danger, no need to wait for swelling to resolve completely — if treatment is feasible and the condition is not too bad, treat promptly to avoid delayed treatment, especially open fractures (waiting makes the wound rot).
  3. Exception: nasal fracture can wait, but not more than 3 weeks — once the bone has united, reduction is difficult.

## Nasal Fracture
- **Most common facial fracture.**
- **Diagnosis** — clinical, by physical examination (best); film helps confirm.
- **Symptoms** — nasal obstruction, epistaxis.
- **Examination** — the upper nose is bone, the lower part cartilage; with fracture the nasal bridge is depressed or deviated; crepitus on palpation.
- Film nasal bone — lateral view.
- Do not forget associated injuries, e.g. fracture base of skull, torn lacrimal duct.
- **Treatment**
  1. Control epistaxis first — usually easy: pinch, press, pack with a little adrenaline.
  2. Undisplaced fracture — leave it alone; an external splint may be placed just for show (to keep the family happy).
  3. Displaced fracture with little swelling — lift the depressed bone with Asch forceps, straighten the nasal septum, then nasal packing to support the framework for 2-3 days.
  4. Displaced fracture with marked swelling — wait about 7 days for swelling to settle (not more than 3 weeks), then reduce.

## Fracture Zygoma
- Zygoma = cheekbone; 3 legs attaching to 3 neighbouring bones: frontal, temporal and maxilla.
- Second most common facial fracture after nasal fracture; usually with associated maxillary fracture.
- **Fracture patterns**
  1. **Tripod fracture** — broken at all 3 sutures; usually an unstable fracture
  2. **Arch fracture** — cheekbone depressed
- **Symptoms**
  1. Swelling, bruising over the cheekbone or orbit; epistaxis
  2. Loss of facial prominence
  3. Subconjunctival hemorrhage
  4. Infraorbital nerve paresthesia — numbness beside the nose, cheek and upper lip
  5. Trismus — if the fracture presses on the "coracoid process" (as printed)
- Film for zygoma: Waters and submentovertex view.
- **Treatment**
  1. A few need no surgery (no displacement at all) — follow up.
  2. **Indications for surgery**
     - 2.1 Facial asymmetry, marked depression
     - 2.2 Tripod fracture
     - 2.3 Enophthalmos
     - 2.4 Diplopia
     - 2.5 Infraorbital nerve compression
     - 2.6 Associated facial fracture
  3. Surgery: usually ORIF with miniplate fixed with screws.
  4. Except the arch: open reduction alone without fixation is enough — incision at the temple, insert an elevator and lift the arch = **Gillies temporal approach**.

## Fracture Mandible
- Third most common.
- **Fracture sites**
  1. Condylar 36% (usually a fall striking the chin on the floor)
  2. Body 21%
  3. Angle 20%
  4. Symphysis 14%
- **Cautions** (ข้อควรระวัง)
  1. Many muscles attach to the mandible → fragments pull apart widely, and multiple fracture sites are common.
  2. Parasymphysis fracture or bilateral midline fracture → beware the tongue falling back (glossoptosis) and blocking the upper airway; often with associated angle or condyle fracture.
  3. Do not forget the C-spine.
  4. Poorly treated → most common long-term complication is malocclusion.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Jaw pain and swelling
  2. Trismus, especially with condyle fracture
  3. Malocclusion; crepitus may be palpable
  4. Numb lower lip from compression of the inferior dental or mental nerve
  5. Foul breath, because the patient cannot spit out saliva well
  6. Fracture near the "coracoid process" (as printed) may pierce the external ear canal
  7. Fracture near the angle may cause facial palsy from CN 7 injury
- Clearest film: panoramic view.
- **Treatment** — aim for normal occlusion; most need surgery, except condyle fractures with little displacement. Summary:
  1. **Isolated condylar fracture**
     - 1.1 Minimal displacement with normal occlusion (can open the mouth and swallow) → analgesics, liquid and soft diet about 3 weeks
     - 1.2 Minimal displacement with malocclusion → IMF (intermaxillary fixation) with arch bar
     - 1.3 Severe displacement → ORIF with miniplate
  2. Fracture at angle, body, parasymphysis → ORIF is now preferred.

## Fracture Maxilla
- The maxilla lies in the centre of the face, joined laterally by many other bones forming the orbit, nasal bridge, oral cavity and facial frame — so neighbouring bones usually break with it.
- Usually from a direct frontal impact.
- René Le Fort classified maxillary fractures into 3 types:
  1. **Le Fort 1 (low transverse fracture)** — horizontal fracture above the upper teeth, separating the maxillary process from the midface; holding the front teeth, the upper teeth rock back and forth.
  2. **Le Fort 2 (pyramidal fracture)** — along the zygomaticomaxillary suture; a pyramid/triangle-shaped segment separates the midface (including upper facial bones and orbit). Rocking the front teeth moves the lower orbit too; the distance between the medial canthi widens = **telecanthus**, and the face looks flat = **disc face deformity**.
     - Figure: normal interpupillary distance 60 mm; normal intercanthal distance 30 mm; telecanthus shown as 45 mm.
  3. **Le Fort 3 (craniofacial separation)** — fracture through zygoma, orbital floor and nasoethmoid; the whole midface separates from the upper facial bones. The face looks elongated with a depressed centre — **donkey like appearance**; malocclusion; the globe sinks inward (enophthalmos). If the cribriform plate breaks → CSF rhinorrhea.
- Film: Waters view shows it clearly.
- **Treatment**
  1. Watch bleeding closely. If a tube is needed, intubate orally and then do tracheostomy — a nasal tube presses the maxilla down, separates the fracture further, and bleeding becomes uncontrollable.
  2. Fracture maxilla is now treated by ORIF (miniplate and screw); if the fragments are fixed in place with good occlusion, IMF (wiring the teeth) is not needed.
  3. CSF rhinorrhea: most (95%) closes by itself within 2-3 weeks; persistent leak → neurosurgeons repair the dural defect — do not attempt it yourself.

## Orbital Fracture
- Emphasis on **blow out fracture**: blunt trauma squarely on the orbit → sudden rise in orbital pressure → the bones forming the orbital floor break, especially the medial wall (medial to the infraorbital nerve).
- The orbit is made of 7 bones: frontal, ethmoid, lacrimal, maxillary, zygomatic, and greater and lesser wing of sphenoid bone.
- A wide floor fracture lets orbital contents (fat, nerve, extraocular muscles — especially inferior rectus and inferior oblique) herniate down into the maxillary sinus.
- Palpating the fracture line helps diagnosis, but often it cannot be felt.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Periorbital ecchymosis and swelling
  2. Enophthalmos (not exophthalmos — จำ), from posterior displacement of the globe
  3. Palpebral and subconjunctival hemorrhage
  4. Limited ocular motion → diplopia, the most common finding, especially upward gaze diplopia
  5. Cheek numbness from infraorbital nerve injury
  6. Occasionally a very strong blow transmits to the optic nerve → blindness
- Beware (ระวัง): pain with mandibular excursion is found in zygomatic arch fracture with the arch displaced onto the temporalis ("temporaris" as printed) muscle — not in orbital fracture.
- **Forced duction test** — confirms muscle entrapment: instil anesthetic drops, grasp the inferior globe with forceps and pull upward; if it will not move up, entrapment is real.
- **Imaging study**
  1. Film Waters view — haziness of the maxillary sinus; floor fracture line may be seen.
  2. CT with thin cuts, both axial and coronal — shows the fracture much more clearly. Send CT when plain film shows or suggests orbital floor fracture (not suspected → no need).
- **Indications for surgery**
  1. Diplopia — some consider it a weak indication (bruised globe or hematoma can also cause double vision), but for surgery residents, treat it as an indication.
  2. Muscle entrapment
  3. Enophthalmos
  4. Film findings:
     - 4.1 Floor defect > 1 cm or > 50% of the floor
     - 4.2 Globe displaced backward > 3 mm
     - 4.3 Orbital content herniated into the maxilla or nasal cavity
- **Operative approach**
  1. Incision below the lower eyelid
  2. Push orbital contents back into place
  3. Cover the defect with a silastic or silicone sheet

## Frontal Sinus Fracture
- From a direct blow to the forehead: the frontal sinus breaks and is depressed.
- **Signs and symptoms**
  - Forehead laceration
  - CSF rhinorrhea
  - Supraorbital nerve anesthesia
  - Depressed frontal region
  - Subconjunctival ecchymosis
- Treatment: persistent CSF leakage or marked, disfiguring depression → open reduction and mini-plate fixation.
