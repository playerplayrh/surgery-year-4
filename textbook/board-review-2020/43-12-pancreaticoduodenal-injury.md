# Pancreaticoduodenal Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 383-425 (PDF pages 2517-2559) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 29 ธันวาคม 2558)

- When the author was a young staff member (about 20-odd years earlier), Prof. Donald D. Trunkey, then president of American Trauma, was a guest lecturer ("lecter" as printed) in trauma at Phramongkutklao. A saying of his that the author still remembers and that may help residents: "The knowledge of an excellent surgeon comes not only through reading but also through hand-on situation."

## Anatomy of the Pancreas
- Retroperitoneal organ lying on L1-L2.
- Weight 40-180 gram.
- Length x width x thickness = 15-20 x 3 x 1-1.5 cm.
- 4 parts:
  1. **Head**: the part to the right of the PV, encircled by the duodenal C-loop; the uncinate is the part of the pancreas behind the PV and SMV.
  2. **Neck**: lies in front of the PV; about 2 cm long.
  3-4. **Body and tail**: to the left of the PV and SMV, crossing the aorta at L2; the tail lies against the splenic hilum ("hilium" as printed).
- **Proximal pancreas**: the part to the right of the PV and SMV.
- **Distal pancreas**: the part to the left of the PV and SMV.
- **Volume of pancreas**
  1. Left of the PV and SMV: volume 56 and 68% respectively
  2. Left of the CBD: volume 89%
- Volume loss of 80% causes no exocrine or endocrine problem; losing > 90% of pancreatic tissue causes diabetes and exocrine insufficiency.
- **Pancreatic duct (PD)**
  - Starts at the tail, runs through the body along the length of the pancreas, midway between upper and lower borders; in depth the PD lies mostly toward the posterior rather than anterior.
  - Roughly: a tear > 50% of the thickness of the pancreatic tissue may involve the pancreatic duct; but in some the main PD is very superficial, so even a small tear may transect it.
  - The main PD has about 15-20 tributary (branch) ducts at right angles, draining directly into it.
  - **Major papilla**: opening of the joined main PD and distal CBD, on the posteromedial wall of the duodenum, 7-10 cm from the pylorus.
  - **Minor papilla**: 2-2.5 cm above the major papilla.
  - The PD is stiffer and more brittle than pancreatic tissue; a blow can sometimes transect the main PD while the capsule remains intact.

## Anatomy of the Duodenum
- Continues from the pylorus; about 28 cm long.
- 4 parts:
  1. **Superior (first) part**: cap or bulb, about 5 cm; from the pylorus at the pyloric vein of Mayo to the line of the GDA.
  2. **Descending (second) part**: about 7-10 cm, ending at the ampulla of Vater; the most frequently injured part.
  3. **Horizontal (third) part**: about 6-8 cm, ending at the SMV.
  4. **Ascending (fourth) part**: about 5 cm, ending at the ligament of Treitz.

## Pancreatic Injury
- Found in 4% of abdominal injury patients.
- 90% of pancreatic injuries have associated injury; 87% of duodenal injuries have associated injury.
- 20% of pancreatic injuries also injure the duodenum; 10% of duodenal injuries also have pancreatic injury.
- Penetrating : blunt injury = 70:30.
- Early death — from bleeding.
- Late death — from infection and MOF.
- Single most important predictor of outcome: presence of PD injury.

## Clinical Presentations
- Few symptoms early; needs a high index of suspicion in blunt trauma to the upper abdomen with pain out of proportion — except severe injury, which gives back pain, ecchymosis of the back or flank, then peritonitis with a board-like abdomen.
- On the abdomen there may be bruising or seat belt sign, and the patient keeps mentioning mild abdominal distension — keep pancreatic injury in mind.
- If a chest film is taken, glance at the lower thoracic spine — a fracture may be seen.

## Serum Amylase
- Unreliable marker — rises after drinking alcohol, and after being kicked on the shin (as printed).
- 40% of pancreatic injury patients have normal amylase.
- 35% of pancreatic injury patients have raised amylase.
- Whether normal or raised, repeat serum amylase 3 hours later; if normal → follow up clinically; if persistent hyperamylasemia plus history and exam suggest pancreatic injury → further investigation; the author favours CT.

## Double contrast CT Scan
- Gold standard; high accuracy.
- Sensitivity and specificity > 90%, but in the first 4 hours false positive may be 20%.
- If the first scan is normal but suspicion remains high (สงสัยหงุดหงิดมาก), repeat CT in the next 6 hours.
- **Positive signs**
  1. Thickening of anterior renal fascia — the most common sign
  2. Fluid collection around the pancreas
  3. Pancreatic parenchymal edema or disruption
  4. Active bleeding or extravasation

## Other Investigations of Pancreatic Injury
- **Plain abdomen**: no specific character; may show:
  1. Loss of psoas shadow
  2. Widening of gastrocolic distance from displacement of stomach and transverse colon
  3. Sentinel loop
  4. Fracture thoracolumbar spine or scoliosis
- **US**: distended abdomen and bowel gas obscure the view; the author thinks it does not work for the pancreas.
- **MRCP or ERCP**: for persistent hyperamylasemia with suspected ductal injury and unclear CT; also helps treat pancreatic fistula.
- **DPL**: unreliable — raised fluid amylase is not specific to the pancreas; does not work, like US; if it appears among exam choices, mark it wrong.

## AAST Pancreatic Injury Scaling System

| Grade | Description (AAST Pancreatic Organ Injury Scale) |
|---|---|
| I | Minor contusion / superficial laceration; no duct injury |
| II | Major contusion or laceration; no duct injury |
| III | Distal transection or parenchymal laceration with duct injury |
| IV | Proximal transection or parenchymal laceration with ampulla injury |
| V | Massive disruption of pancreatic head |

- 5 grades.
- Grade 1, 2 = simple injury.
- Grade 3, 4, 5 = complex or severe injury.
- Severe injury at multiple sites → up 1 grade.

## Management of Pancreatic Injury
- Treatment now leans conservative — avoid complex operations or fancy procedures with many anastomoses that take a long time; they look impressive to the girls and make residents excited, but everyone leaks and dies (as printed).
- Nonoperative management has a role in isolated grade 1-2 injury from blunt trauma with stable hemodynamics; reports show success without surgery, but select cases carefully: NPO, serial physical examination with lab and imaging at intervals.
- **Principles of operative treatment** — 3:
  1. Stop bleeding
  2. Debridement — preserve as much good tissue as possible
  3. Control pancreatic secretion and adequate drainage
- External drainage: soft closed suction drain such as Jackson Pratt no. 10 or Redivac — better than sump or Penrose; leave the drain fairly long, about 7-14 days.
- Complex injury grade 4, 5 → consider placing a feeding jejunostomy at the same time.
- **Intraoperative suspicious of main PD injury**
  1. Complete transection or more than 50% of parenchymal thickness
  2. Severe maceration & saponification
  3. Penetrating with central perforation
  4. Direct visualization of ductal disruption — pancreatic juice seen leaking; the most certain sign
- **Intraoperative main PD evaluation**
  - Intraoperative assessment of main PD injury; usually invasive, now rarely recommended — takes longer, more tissue trauma, little information. Given here only as an idea, enough to keep examiners happy; in the author's experience residents usually mix them up and fail.
  1. **Needle cholecystocholangiopancreaticography** (very long name): inject contrast into the gallbladder and have the anaesthetist give IV morphine to cause spasm of the sphincter of Oddi ("sphincted" as printed), forcing contrast into the main PD.
  2. **Transduodenal pancreatography**: may cause a duodenal leak.
  3. **Distal pancreatic resection & duct canulation**: invites fistula.
  4. **Intraoperative ERCP**: with the patient supine — extremely hard.
  5. **Direct canulation at the site of PD injury**: silly (ประสาท).
  6. **IOUS**: one successful report from Japan diagnosing PD injury in trauma — let the experts do it (as printed).

## Operative Management
- **Intraoperative suspicious of pancreatic injury**
  1. Proximity of injury to pancreas
  2. Central or paraduodenal hematoma
  3. Bile stain
  4. Saponification of retroperitoneal fat
  5. Bruise or dent on the pancreas where it lies on L2
- **Exposure of the pancreas**: residents must explore everything; a miss guarantees trouble.
  1. **Head and uncinate process**
     - 1.1 Kocherization up to the SMV, lifting the duodenum and head of pancreas.
     - 1.2 Mobilize the hepatic flexure ("flexor" as printed) downward for a clearer view.
  2. **Body and tail**
     - 2.1 Lesser sac exploration: divide the gastrocolic omentum and pull the transverse colon down — almost the whole anterior pancreas is seen.
     - 2.2 To see the posterior surface: lift the pancreas at its lower border together with mobilizing the spleen up, then lift the tail; mobilizing the splenic flexure ("flexor" as printed) down a little gives a clearer view.

## Operative Management of Pancreatic Injury
- Treatment depends on injury grade and the patient's condition; associated injury in almost 90% here, especially major vessels.
- On opening, if the pancreatic tissue is oozing, stay calm; do not grasp pancreatic tissue with a clamp — it tears more. Cauterize or place shallow sutures to control it. If bleeding is profuse and you are stuck and getting stressed, push a swab down on it or do Kocherization first, then compress the pancreas with your hand for a while — gently and tenderly.

## Management Grade 1,2
- Grade 1, 2 — no main PD tear; principles: stop bleeding, debridement and adequate drainage.
- Subcapsular hematoma with pancreatic contusion → explore the hematoma to grade the injury correctly; do not leave the hematoma alone — unlike a liver or spleen hematoma that has stopped bleeding, which is best left untouched.
- Torn capsule or shallow parenchymal tear → do not suture the capsule closed (pseudocyst). The author also leaves the capsule unsutured, except for a parenchymal tear, which he sutures down to the wound base with prolene 4-0. Basics-focused examiners may object, fearing pseudocyst and further injury from suturing (in theory do not suture; in practice the author sutures — "2 standards").
- The author places 2 closed suction drains for at least 7 days; when dry, removes them.

## Management Grade 3
- The type most often examined: pancreas transection right at the neck or to the left of the SMV.
- Treatment: distal pancreatectomy. Before dividing, place sutures at the upper and lower borders; the author likes cutting the end into a fish-mouth shape. If the PD opening is found → close it with nylon 3-0; usually it cannot be found → transfix suture; a stitch through the middle is sure to catch the duct.
- **Splenic preservation**: in theory, a child with isolated pancreatic injury who stays hemodynamically stable should have the spleen preserved, but it takes at least 45 more minutes.
- In the author's real life the spleen is always removed; if asked, he says BP dropped at that time (as printed).
- Place 2 drains at the tail.
- Unstable, needs damage control → suture to stop bleeding, place drains and get out quickly; the author is old and wants to sleep, so the juniors keep watch (as printed).

## Management Grade 4
- Proximal injury: main PD transected; only the CBD remains intact.
- Grade 4 is rather controversial; several options:
  1. **Proximal end closure with Roux-en-Y pancreaticojejunostomy**: for cases where removing the distal part would leave < 20% of the gland. In theory best for grade 4 with severe injury of the duodenum or ampulla, but it takes time, is hard, bleeds a lot and may leak. Do not tell anyone (as printed): the author does not like it in practice, but in year-3 exams, for a stable grade 4 patient, mark this option.
  2. **Extended distal pancreatectomy or subtotal pancreatectomy**: choose when the ampulla is intact and > 20% of pancreas remains — roughly, after resection about 2 cm of pancreas remains beyond the C-loop. If resectable, the author prefers this over option 1 — no anastomosis, and exocrine/endocrine insufficiency can be helped with drugs; but on review this method is not very suitable for exams (Trauma 7th ed. no longer mentions it).
  3. **Stop bleeding with wide external drainage** plus one feeding jejunostomy — even if a pancreatic fistula develops, conservative treatment can still close it.
     Asked personally, the author prefers this over the other options (Sabiston 19th likes it a lot); in oral exams answer "drain" — doing little pleases most examiners.
  4. Heavy blood loss, cannot cope, needs damage control → suture to stop bleeding as far as possible, packing, many drains, then find a way to refer to someone we dislike to continue care (as printed).
- **Choosing between resection and Roux-en-Y** — the author's considerations:
  1. Heavy contamination or colon injury → resection better.
  2. Patient somewhat poor but not to the point of damage control → resect rather than many anastomoses.
  3. Surgeon not very experienced → extended distal pancreatectomy is easier.
- **Summary**: year-3 resident, stable patient with severe injury of the duodenum or ampulla → marking Roux-en-Y should be OK; without very severe injury, the author favours stop bleeding and drain. Year-4 with little experience → just stop bleeding, place many drains, then refer — safer.

## Management Grade 5
- Massive destruction of the pancreatic head — think of damage control in advance.
- Unstable → stop bleeding, place drains, and prepare for the mortality conference — you will certainly be grilled.
- A Whipple would only be for injury that has already nearly cut off the head and duodenum, mangled almost loose → resect it, place many drains, and once stable, go back to reconstruct.
- At the second look, place a feeding jejunostomy.
- **Indications for Whipple's operation**
  1. Laceration of intrapancreatic bile duct and proximal main PD
  2. Extensive demarcation of pancreas and duodenum
  3. Avulsion of ampulla with severe injury of second part of the duodenum and head of pancreas
  4. Retropancreatic portal vein disruption with uncontrol retropancreatic hemorrhage

## Duodenal Injury
- Uncommon; hard to diagnose.
- Penetrating injury 80%, blunt trauma 20%.
- Diagnosis needs a truly suspicious mind; delayed treatment has mortality almost 40%.
- **Location of injury**
  1. Second part — 35%
  2. Third part — 15%
  3. Fourth part — 15%
  4. First part — 10%

## Clinical Presentations
- History of a blow to the upper abdomen, e.g. bicycle handlebar or a kick to the abdomen.
- NG tube may return blood.
- Early only mild vague pain; after 24-48 hours the abdomen becomes rigid, then shock.

## Investigations of Duodenal Injury
- **Plain abdomen**: positive in about 1 in 3 cases.
  1. Retroperitoneal air around the right kidney; free air is uncommon
  2. Obliteration of right psoas shadow
  3. Scoliosis of lumbar spine
- **US**: does not work well.
- **UGI study** with water-soluble contrast: film with the patient in right-side-down decubitus; positive in 50%. Contrast leakage → perforation; coiled spring or stacked coin sign → intramural hematoma.
- **CT scan**: gold standard; suits hemodynamically stable cases; feed water-soluble contrast; sensitivity 80%.
- **Positive signs from CT**
  1. Extraluminal gas or extraluminal contrast
  2. Fat stranding with loss of sharp tissue plane
  3. Intraluminal hematoma or focal wall thickening > 4 mm

## AAST Duodenal Injury Scaling System

| Grade | Type | Injury description |
|---|---|---|
| I | Hematoma | Involving simple (as printed) portion of duodenum |
| I | Laceration | Partial thickness, no perforation |
| II | Hematoma | Involving more than one portion |
| II | Laceration | Disruption < 50% of circumference |
| III | Laceration | Disruption 50-75% of circumference of D2 |
| III | Laceration | Disruption 50-100% of circumference of D1, D2, D4 (as printed) |
| IV | Laceration | Disruption > 75% of circumference of D2 |
| IV | Laceration | Involving ampulla or distal common bile duct |
| V | Laceration | Massive disruption of duodenopancreatic complex |
| V | Vascular | Devascularization |

- Grade 1, 2 = simple.
- Grade 3, 4, 5 = severe or complex injury.
- Duodenal injury is classified by site as D1, D2, D3, D4.
- Injury at multiple sites → up 1 grade.

## Management of Duodenal Injury
- 75-85% can be treated by debridement and closure of the perforation.
- **Principles of treatment** — 3:
  1. Repair or anastomosis without tension
  2. Good pancreaticobiliary drainage
  3. Protective procedure in severe injury
- **Intraoperative suspicious of duodenal injury**
  1. Proximity of injury to duodenum
  2. Paraduodenal hematoma
  3. Bile strain (as printed)
  4. Crepitus
- **Exposure of the duodenum**
  1. Kocher maneuver
  2. Lesser sac exploration
  3. Transection of the ligament of Treitz to see the fourth part ("fouth" as printed) and DJ junction
  4. Cattell-Braasch maneuver ("Cattel-Braash" as printed)
- On finding a duodenal injury, decide:
  1. Can it be primarily repaired? — 80% can
  2. Is pancreaticobiliary drainage needed?
  3. Is a protective procedure needed?
  4. Is damage control needed?
- **Synder Duodenal Severity Scale** (as printed)

| | Mild | Severe |
|---|---|---|
| Agent | Stab | Blunt or missile |
| Size | < 75% wall | ≤ 75% wall (as printed) |
| Duodenal site | 3, 4 | 1, 2 |
| Injury repair interval (hr) | < 24 | ≥ 24 |
| Adjacent injury | No CBD; no pancreatic injury | CBD; pancreatic injury |

- Severe group → protective procedure.
- Grade 1, 2 with significant vascular injury → protective procedure as well.
- **Protective procedure**
  1. Repair & reinforcement or buttressing with omentum — patching, as in esophageal repair
  2. Duodenal decompression & diversion of gastric content
     - 2.1 Tube duodenostomy
     - 2.2 Triple ostomy
  3. Pyloric exclusion
  4. Berne diverticulization
- When the author was a resident, his teacher at the Police General Hospital (ครูที่โรงพยาบาลตำรวจ) taught:
  1. Repair done but you cannot sleep → do duodenal decompression.
  2. Did 1 and still cannot sleep → do diversion.
  3. Did 3 (as printed) and still cannot sleep → you are close to going mad (as printed).

## Tube Duodenostomy / Triple Ostomy
- **Technique of triple ostomy** (วิธีทำ)
  1. Gastrostomy for gastric decompression
  2. Retrograde tube duodenostomy or retrograde jejunostomy for duodenal decompression
  3. Prograde feeding jejunostomy
- In triple ostomy some skip the gastrostomy: pass the NG tube with its tip in the duodenum, or use a Foley as a lateral tube duodenostomy instead.
- The author personally dislikes triple ostomy: tubes everywhere on the abdomen including the Jackson Pratt — plus IV, NG, Foley and monitor lines; the nurses recording output are exhausted; and once the bowel shrinks, the tubes fall out — real trouble (กรูซวย).

## Pyloric Exclusion
- **Technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. Gastrotomy: open the stomach on the anterior greater curve at its most dependent part.
  2. Closure of pylorus: grasp the pylorus with a Babcock, pull it up and close with silk or prolene 3-0, running or interrupted; after about 3 weeks the pylorus reopens by itself (sutures come loose). Stapler also possible. Do not use chromic or vicryl — the pylorus reopens within 2 weeks (too early).
  3. Side-to-side antecolic gastrojejunostomy; vagotomy not needed ("tmarginal ulcer" as printed — [unclear]).
  4. Feeding jejunostomy — decide case by case.
- The author uses this every time a protective procedure is needed; takes ≤ 20 min; diverts gastric content away from the duodenal wound and also lowers duodenal pressure.
- Post-op: fewer tubes, easier care.

## Berne Diverticulization Procedure
- A protective procedure now used less.
- Still used for grade 3 injury with major tissue loss above the ampulla (first and second parts) that cannot be closed and cannot take a Roux-en-Y jejunal anastomosis — just resect.
- **Technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. TV + antrectomy, B2 reconstruction
  2. T-tube in the CBD to drain bile
  3. Tube duodenostomy

## Management Grade 1,2
- Wall contusion only → nothing needed; usually resolves within 72 hours.
- Serosal tear → close with silk 3-0.
- Perforation: after debridement, hole ≤ 50% of circumference → close transversely; the author closes in 2 layers.
- For grade 2 some patch with omentum — if it makes you happier, do it.
- Intramural hematoma → the author opens the serosa, scoops out the clot, then closes the seromuscular layer with silk 3-0.
- Grade 1, 2 patients rarely need a protective procedure.

## Management Grade 3
- Tear > 50% of circumference or heavily bruised tissue, with no CBD or ampulla injury → resect and anastomose.
- **Operative approach**
  1. If after assessment there is no tension — usually third with fourth part ("fouth" as printed) → end-to-end duodenoduodenostomy with a protective procedure.
  2. Joining the duodenum would be under a lot of tension — especially second part below the ampulla with third part → close the distal end and do Roux-en-Y duodenojejunostomy to the proximal end.
  3. Same condition as 2 (too much tension) but the lesion is proximal to the ampulla → Berne diverticulization.
  4. Jejunal serosal patch over the hole, recommended by Thal — no longer used; Thal said it was good, but the study was in dogs; in humans the patch does not hold well, may come loose or leak; the author thinks it should not be used.

## Management Grade 4
- Besides the duodenal tear, there is injury to the CBD or ampulla.
- The duodenal wound is treated as described above.
- The problem is the injured distal CBD and ampulla:
  1. CBD repairable: tear < 50% → repair and leave a T tube as a stent; tear > 50% of circumference → repair with a Roux-en-Y jejunal limb.
  2. Not repairable, or little experience (spent training on admin and chasing girls, as printed) → put a tube drain into the CBD and refer to someone else we are fed up with to continue (as printed).
  3. Avulsion or disruption of the ampulla but pancreatic head spared → recommended ampulla replantation through the posterior duodenum; the author has never done it — he would do a staged Whipple.

## Management Grade 5
- Extremely severe — combined injury of pancreas and duodenum.
- 2 options:
  1. Damage control: stop bleeding, many drains, send to ICU, definitive treatment later
  2. Whipple

## Intramural Hematoma of the Duodenum
- Classic: a child on a bicycle hit in the abdomen by the handlebars, then upper gut obstruction.
- Symptoms usually about 48 hours after injury: distension from gastric dilatation, then bilious vomiting, because the hematoma usually lies below the ampulla.
- Some have jaundice if the clot is near the ampulla.
- **Imaging**
  1. Plain abdomen — gastric dilatation or double bubble
  2. Upper GI — coiled spring or stacked coin sign (caption: UGI study shows complete obstruction due to the hematoma)
  3. Now CT with oral contrast — same findings as UGI (caption: barium study shows luminal stenosis at the horizontal duodenum causing partial obstruction; contrast CT shows prominent mural thickening of the horizontal duodenum)
- **Treatment**
  1. **Diagnosed before surgery** → NPO + IV + NG + TPN for about 2 weeks; usually improves — abdomen flattens, vomiting stops, NG output falls.
     - Indication for surgery:
       - 1.1 Not better after > 3 weeks
       - 1.2 Worsens during conservative treatment — rigid abdomen from leakage
     - Hematoma size or degree of initial obstruction is not a criterion for surgery.
  2. **Intraoperative diagnosis** → Kocherization, open the serosa, scoop out clot without perforating the mucosa, stop bleeding completely, then close the seromuscular layer with silk. In some cases (uncommon), the serosa is so bruised you would rather not open there, or clot evacuation fails → gastrojejunostomy as alternative treatment.

## Procedure Rarely Used Today
1. Serosal patch ("esrosal" as printed)
2. Berne diverticulization
3. Extended distal pancreatectomy for grade 4 — Trauma 7th ed. dropped it
4. Suturing the capsule or parenchyma in grade 1, 2 — for fear of pseudocyst
5. Splenic preservation in grade 3
6. Direct repair of the main PD, or Roux-en-Y jejunum over the main PD
7. Whipple

## Complications of Pancreaticoduodenal Injury
- **Bleeding**
  - When the author was a resident there were few aids and no interventions; his boss taught: "Bleeding pancreas not primarily controlled is never controlled." If not dry, he does not leave the OR.
  - Do it well the first time: going back for rebleeding is truly hellish — as hard as necrosectomy for infected pancreatic necrosis.
  - In such a case, consider consulting for embolization before rushing back in.
- **Pancreatic fistula**
  - Most common complication; definition: output > 100 cc per day for > 10 consecutive days.
  - (Caption: ERCP showing significant contrast leak from the tail of the pancreas.)
  - Treatment:
    1. Conservative — usually responds very well; 90% close spontaneously.
       - 1.1 TPN early, then feeding jejunostomy
       - 1.2 Correct fluid and electrolytes, IV antibiotic, skin care
       - 1.3 Octreotide — not yet proven in trials to speed fistula closure or lower mortality (the author does not give it)
    2. Still draining after > 2 weeks → the author requests MRCP to see where it leaks, how big, and whether the main PD is obstructed; try a stent first; if the stent fails, then operate.
- **Duodenal fistula**: green bile out of the drain — guaranteed nerve-wracking; see the small bowel chapter on enterocutaneous fistula (the author is too lazy to repeat it).
- **Pancreatitis**: about 10-15%; abdominal pain and raised amylase; conservative treatment — most improve on their own.
- **Pancreatic pseudocyst**: diagnosed by US or CT; treated as pseudocyst, as covered elsewhere by the author.
