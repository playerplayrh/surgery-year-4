# ADRENAL GLAND 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 828-851 (PDF pages 1925-1948)

## Introduction
- The adrenal is a frightening organ: reading textbooks, the basic physiology of adrenal hormones is so dense that most readers get confused, faint, short of breath (as printed). Students should try to understand the adrenal thoroughly — it is hard; sometimes the author asks them just to memorize, not to be shy, no need to open more textbooks — it will not make life better. When the author was an oral examiner, Cushing cases were deadly; sorry, Phramongkutklao people (as printed: 55555).
- Thai name: ต่อมหมวกไต ("kidney-hat gland"); lies on the upper pole of each kidney, 2 glands. To treat, we must know whether the adrenal disease is on the left or right.
- Some diseases, e.g. pheochromocytoma, may lie outside the normal position = extra adrenal gland.
- **Emphasis on the rules for approaching endocrine patients**
  1. From history, examination and signs, suspect endocrine disease.
  2. Confirm diagnosis with hormone studies — is a hormone being secreted above normal?
  3. **Determine source** — which endocrine gland is the excess hormone coming from?
  4. **Localization** — right gland, left gland, or both?
  5. Then **proper treatment** — drugs or surgery.

## Anatomy
- Weight 4-6 gm.
- Size (width × length × thickness) = 4-5 × 2-3 × 1 cm.
- Shape: right pyramidal, left crescent.
- Colour: golden orange; a surgeon who is not colour blind will see it differs from perinephric fat.
- **Arterial supply**: left and right alike — numerous vessels (pedicles = adrenal A).
- Figure: anatomy of adrenal glands — right adrenal vein short into the IVC; left adrenal vein into the left renal vein, joined by the left inferior phrenic vein; right and left inferior phrenic arteries; left inferior adrenal artery.
  1. Superior pedicle (printed "pedica") or superior adrenal A — on top, from the inferior phrenic A.
  2. Middle pedicle or middle adrenal A — in the middle, from the aorta.
  3. Inferior pedicle or inferior adrenal A — below, from the renal A.
- **Venous drainage** differs between sides:
  1. Left drains into the left renal vein.
  2. Right is only 0.5-1 cm long (shorter than the left vein) and drains directly into the posterolateral aspect of the IVC (remember — น่าจำ). Beware in right adrenalectomy: bleeding here is hard to control.
- 2 main layers:
  1. Adrenal cortex — outer
  2. Adrenal medulla — inner

## Adrenal cortex
- Derived from mesoderm.
- Figure: transverse and microscopic section — capsule, zona glomerulosa, zona fasciculata, zona reticularis, medulla.
- 3 zones:
  1. Zona glomerulosa — outermost; secretes aldosterone
  2. Zona fasciculata — middle; secretes cortisol
  3. Zona reticularis — innermost; secretes sex hormone
- Remember which zone secretes which hormone: **GFR = ACS**.
- If examined, note that aldosterone comes only from the glomerulosa.
- Sex hormone from the adrenal = androgen.

## Adrenal medulla
- Derived from ectoderm and neural crest, which migrate to the medulla and to extra-adrenal sites.
- Most common extra-adrenal site: around the IMA on the left = organ of Zuckerkandl.
- **Catecholamines** (epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine) are made in the CNS, sympathetic nervous system and adrenal medulla, with tyrosine as substrate.
- **Note** — remember and understand the abbreviations:
  1. Right vein drains directly into the IVC; left drains into the left renal vein
  2. GFR & ACS
  3. Aldosterone from G
  4. Cortex from mesoderm, medulla from ectoderm

## Adrenal Imaging
- Usual CT protocol: noncontrast and IV contrast of the lower chest and upper abdomen, cuts about 3 mm; start noncontrast, then IV contrast enhanced, ending with delayed contrast washout.
- MRI is better than CT for lipid content and local invasion of nearby organs; MRI suits pregnant patients or contrast allergy.
- On CT look at size, imaging phenotype, HU, and contrast washout.
- Size > 6 cm, irregular outline, heterogenicity or invasion → points to malignancy.
- **Hounsfield unit (HU) for tissue densities** (named for Sir Godfrey Newbold Hounsfield, 1919-2004; CT for brain 1972-73, whole body 1975; Nobel prize 1979; knighted 1981):

| Tissue | CT number (HU) |
|---|---|
| Bone | +1000 |
| Liver | 40-60 |
| White matter | -20 to -30 |
| Grey matter | -37 to -45 |
| Blood | 40 |
| Muscle | 10-40 |
| Kidney | 30 |
| CSF | 15 |
| Water | 0 |
| Fat | -50 to -100 |
| Air | -1000 |

(White/grey matter values as printed.)
- Look at noncontrast HU, which depends on cell lipid concentration:
  - HD < 18 (as printed, = HU): lipid-rich → usually benign adenoma
  - HD < 10: very fatty → definitely benign; the one found is myelolipoma
  - HD > 18: little fat → think of pheochromocytoma and malignancy
- **Washout contrast**: compare CT without contrast, during contrast, and delayed film 15 minutes after contrast; contrast washout < 60% → suspect malignancy.

## Preoperative Management
- **Pheochromocytoma**: phenoxybenzamine 10 mg/day or selective alpha-1 blockade × 3 wk with adequate hydration.
- **Cushing**: preop — nothing to fear as much as PO; may have adrenal insufficiency for years afterwards.
- **Aldosteronoma**: few problems; correct hypokalemia before surgery.

## Cushing's Syndrome
- Clinical hypercortisolism with loss of diurnal variation of cortisol level.
- Overall, exogenous Cushing is more common than endogenous (printed "endogeneous"); exogenous is iatrogenic — doctors prescribing steroids, e.g. for SLE.
- **Most common causes of endogenous Cushing**
  1. **Pituitary cause (Cushing disease)** — ACTH-producing pituitary adenoma; most common, 70%
  2. **Adrenal cause** — second, 20%
     - 2.1 Adenoma — most common
     - 2.2 Carcinoma — second in the adrenal group
  3. **Ectopic ACTH** — various tumors make ACTH, which drives the adrenal to secrete cortisol, e.g.:
     - 3.1 Oat cell or small cell lung cancer — a classic question: Cushing features with hemoptysis
     - 3.2 Malignant thymic tumor
     - 3.3 PETs
     - 3.4 Carcinoid
     - 3.4 MTC (medullary thyroid cancer) (numbering as printed)
     - 3.5 Pheochromocytoma
- Suspicious → confirm → find source → localization → treatment.

## Suspicious
- Figure: features of Cushing's syndrome — emotional disturbance, enlarged sella turcica, moon facies, osteoporosis, cardiac hypertrophy (hypertension), buffalo hump, obesity, adrenal tumor or hyperplasia, thin wrinkled skin, abdominal striae, amenorrhea, muscle weakness, purpura, skin ulcers (poor wound healing).
- Think of Cushing with:
  1. Trunk obesity — most common, 95%
  2. Next, round full face = moon face
  3. Hump on the back like a buffalo (forbidden misprint — as printed joke) = buffalo hump
  4. HT, DM, muscle wasting & weakness, hirsutism, striae (lift the shirt — the abdomen shows tiger-stripe striae), weight gain

## Confirmed Diagnosis
- Next: screen and diagnose true Cushing — need 2 +ve tests; Sabiston 2017 uses items 1 and 2.
  1. **24 hours urine free cortisol** — normally free cortisol should not appear in urine; finding it implies (อนุมาน — written fancily to show good Thai, as printed) very high blood cortisol. Sensitivity > 95%, specificity 98%.
  2. **Late-night salivary cortisol** — cortisol can now be measured in saliva; > 550 ng/dL (as printed) fits this disease.
  3. **Low dose dexamethasone suppression test (DST)** — overnight 1 mg dexamethasone at 1100 PM, then serum cortisol at 8 am; normal people suppress plasma cortisol, Cushing patients do not.
  4. **48-hour 2 mg DST suppression test**

## Determine Source
- Figure: HPA axis — hypothalamus CRH (+) → pituitary (adenoma) ACTH (+) → adrenal cortex → cortisol, which feeds back negatively (−) on hypothalamus and pituitary.
- Residents must understand cortisol control well; then determining source and localization feel easy.
- Once Cushing is diagnosed, find the misbehaving organ releasing too much cortisol: adrenal, pituitary, or ectopic ACTH.
- 2 ways to find it:
  1. Plasma ACTH — adrenal cause or not
  2. High dose DST — separates pituitary from ectopic ACTH
- **First step**
  1. **ACTH low** → the adrenal itself is the cause, releasing so much cortisol that the pituitary is suppressed (dwarfed) → low ACTH.
  2. **ACTH high** → pituitary or ectopic:
     - 2.1 The pea-sized pituitary releases ACTH; use the "big one stomps the small one" method — high dose DST: if the pituitary is the cause, cortisol is suppressed.
     - 2.2 High dose DST fails to suppress → ectopic ACTH is likely. Observation: ectopic cases have very, very high ACTH.
     - 2.3 Still unsure after 2.1 and 2.2 → last resort: bilateral petrosal venous sampling.

## Petrosal Venous Sampling
- Figure: venous anatomy — cavernous sinus, inferior petrosal sinus, confluent pituitary veins, jugular vein.
1. Catheter into the internal jugular vein and up to the branch called the petrosal vein.
2. Draw blood from the petrosal vein and measure ACTH versus peripheral ACTH = gradient.
3. Petrosal ACTH > 2 times peripheral at basal stage, or > 3 times after CRH stimulation → the cause is the pituitary.

## Localization
- Adrenal cause — where are you? Left or right? CT and MRI work very well now; fat content also helps separate benign from malignant (not written here — too much; for more, go to the "Line" of the radiology tent: consult them sweetly for a happy life — as printed).
1. CT scan with IV contrast of lower chest and upper abdomen — suits adrenal lesions; thin cuts ≤ 3 mm.
2. MRI — suits pituitary lesions and local invasion of adrenal lesions.
3. Iodocholesterol scan (NP 59) — cortisol is made from cholesterol; radio-tagged cholesterol accumulates in the part of the adrenal cortex making lots of cortisol, likely the tumor.
4. Ectopic → CT chest, abdomen.

## Proper Treatment
- **Surgical treatment**
  1. **Pituitary**
     - 1.1 Trans-sphenoidal resection + PO RT if the tumor is not fully removed
     - Figure: trans-sphenoidal resection of the pituitary gland.
     - 1.2 Stereotactic radiosurgery with gamma knife guided by CT — the author knows little; reportedly for persistent or recurrent disease after surgery.
  2. **Adrenal** — lap. adrenalectomy preferred; size > 6 cm or suspected malignancy → open. After surgery give stress-dose steroid, then taper slowly, because the other adrenal has been suppressed for a long time; generally for 6-18 months.
  3. **Ectopic ACTH** — first is small cell lung cancer; treat by cause.
- **Medical treatment** — for those who should not be operated on: disease spread too far, patient near death (as printed), or primary not found.
  1. Steroid inhibitors, e.g. mytyrapone (as printed), ketoconazole, aminoglutethimide
  2. Chemotherapeutic agent, e.g. mitotane
- **Bilateral lap. adrenalectomy** — when everything else has failed (drugs or surgery) and severe Cushing problems persist, consider removing the problem adrenal glands (bilateral adrenalectomy); afterwards the patient takes prednisolone for life.
  - **Indications for bilateral lap. adrenalectomy**
    1. Micronodular bilateral hyperplasia
    2. Failed surgical or adjuvant therapy in pituitary Cushing
    3. Occult ectopic source
- **Guideline diagnosis of Cushing's syndrome (Schwartz edition 11 2019 p.1690)**
- Figure: Cushing diagnosis, as steps —
  1. Confirm the diagnosis: overnight DST; 24-hour urinary free cortisol; 11:00 pm salivary cortisol.
  2. Determine source of hypercortisolism: plasma ACTH; high-dose DST and urinary cortisol.
  3. Decreased ACTH, lack of suppression → adrenal source → CT scan adrenals.
  4. Increased ACTH, positive (suppression) → pituitary source.
  5. Increased ACTH, lack of suppression → ectopic ACTH source.
  6. Equivocal results → further testing: bilateral petrosal vein sampling → ACTH gradient? Yes → pituitary source; No → ectopic ACTH source.
- **Guideline management from Cameron Current Surgical Therapy**
- Figure: Cushing management, as steps —
  1. Patient with Cushing's syndrome (confirmed with 24-hour urinary free cortisol +/- low-dose dexamethasone suppression test) → check ACTH level.
  2. ACTH < 5 pg/mL → adrenal Cushing's syndrome (ACTH-independent) → adrenal CT scan (adrenal mass protocol):
     - Unilateral tumor → laparoscopic adrenalectomy.
     - Micronodular bilateral hyperplasia (rare) → laparoscopic bilateral adrenalectomy.
  3. ACTH > 10-15 pg/mL (ACTH > 500 suggests ectopic disease) → high-dose dexamethasone suppression test:
     - Suppressed → pituitary source → head MRI → transsphenoidal resection vs. radiation → bilateral adrenalectomy for failed surgical or adjuvant therapy.
     - Not suppressed → ectopic source → chest/abd CT → treat based on location.
- That is enough summary on Cushing. Before hyperaldosteronism, remember the mechanism of aldosterone secretion — it helps in understanding adrenal and vascular disease such as renal artery stenosis:
  1. Fall in renal artery pressure or serum sodium → stimulates JGA cells to secrete renin.
  2. Renin converts angiotensinogen from the liver into the odd substance angiotensin 1, which converting enzyme in the lung turns into angiotensin 2, a potent vasoconstrictor; besides raising BP, angiotensin 2 stimulates the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone.
  3. Aldosterone retains sodium and excretes potassium and hydrogen.
  4. When aldosterone is no longer needed → negative feedback blocks the JGA from secreting renin.
- Figure: renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system — kidney (JGA) renin → liver angiotensinogen → angiotensin 1 → lungs (ACE) → angiotensin 2 → adrenal gland → aldosterone → acts on distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct.

## Hyperaldosteronism
- **Overproduction of aldosterone** → salt retention → hypertension with loss of potassium.
- **Causes**
  1. **Primary** — the adrenal itself secretes excess aldosterone; mostly adenoma (remember), not hyperplasia, which is less common. In 1ry: aldosterone high & renin low.
  2. **Secondary** — reduced renal blood flow, e.g. renal A. stenosis or cirrhosis → renin secreted all the time. In 2ry: aldosterone high & renin high too.
- Separate primary from secondary mainly by plasma renin.

## Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome)
- **Suspicious**
  1. Diastolic HT — systolic not very high but diastolic very high, and refractory to medical treatment
  2. Muscle weakness or cramps from hypo K+
  3. Serum K+ < 3.4 mEq/L
  4. Alkalosis from H+ excretion

## Screening
- **Plasma aldosterone concentration & plasma renin activity ratio (PAC : PRA ratio)** — now the screening test. PAC : PRA > 30, or PAC alone > 20 ng/dL → likely Conn. PAC : PRA is used instead of K+ because some patients have normal K+. If still unsure, 24 hr urine free aldosterone > 14 gm/day (as printed) → likely this disease.

## Confirmed Diagnosis
- Conn patients have inappropriate aldosterone secretion despite salt loading; this principle is used for investigation.
1. **Saline suppression test** — oral salt or IV sodium loading, then PAC; PAC > 10 ng/dL confirms the disease.
2. **Captopril test or ACE 1 test** — not popular, but worth knowing: normally captopril lowers plasma aldosterone; in Conn, plasma aldosterone does not change.

## Determine Source
- The cause of hyperaldosteronism must be in the adrenal — hah (แหง ๆ, read "แจ๋ๆ"); the question is only whether it is the left or right adrenal.

## Localization
1. **Thin cut adrenal CT (3 mm cuts)** — nonenhanced (printed "nonenchanced") CT 10-15 HU and delayed contrast washout at 15 minutes > 60% → benign; sensitivity 90%.
2. **NP 59** — sensitive 88%.
3. CT unclear → invasive test: adrenal venous sampling — compare aldosterone : cortisol ratio between the 2 sides; the side with ratio > 5 times higher is the diseased side. Some centers recommend it in every case.
- Figure: adrenal venous sampling — catheters inserted in the femoral veins, passed via IVC into the right adrenal vein (directly off IVC) and the left adrenal vein (via the left renal vein).

## Proper Treatment
1. **Adenoma** — best is lap. adrenalectomy; after surgery aldactone can be stopped; continue potassium until serum potassium is normal, then stop.
2. **Hyperplasia** — control BP with K+ sparing diuretic (aldactone or amloride, as printed), possibly plus calcium channel blocker (nifedipine); BP takes about 2-3 weeks to normalize. Beware — lasix or thiazide are used as decoys.
3. **Suspected malignant** (printed "suspectious") — imaging looks malignant → open adrenalectomy.
- Figure: management of hyperaldosteronism, as steps —
  1. Patient with HTN and hypokalemia (K < 3.4), or persistent HTN (refractory to medical tx), or HTN and adrenal mass → check plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) & plasma renin activity (PRA); calculate PAC : PRA.
  2. PAC > 15 ng/dL, PRA < 0.5 ng/ml/h, PAC : PRA ≥ 30 (urinary aldosterone > 14 µg/day during saline infusion to confirm dx if necessary) → primary hyperaldosteronism → adrenal CT scan with adrenal mass protocol.
  3. PAC > 15 ng/dL, PRA > 0.5 ng/ml/h, PAC : PRA < 30 → secondary hyperaldosteronism → medical treatment: spironolactone (printed "sprinolactone")/eplerenone & K+.
  4. CT unilateral adenoma → adrenal vein sampling (highly desirable) → aldosterone : cortisol > 5 unilaterally → laparoscopic adrenalectomy.
  5. CT no lesion → adrenal vein sampling → aldosterone : cortisol > 5 unilaterally → laparoscopic adrenalectomy.
  6. CT bilateral adrenal hyperplasia → medical treatment: spironolactone/eplerenone & K+.

## Pheochromocytoma
- Tumor arising from chromaffin cells and sustentacular cells of the adrenal medulla and extra-adrenal paraganglia; right > left; only adrenal pheochromocytoma can make epinephrine.
- The 10% tumor, like Whartin (as printed): malignant, multiple, bilateral, extra adrenal, familial, children. 10% of adrenal pheo are malignant, but extra-adrenal pheo has 30-40% chance of malignancy.
- **Extra-adrenal**: arises from paraganglionic tissue in sympathetic ganglia from brainstem, neck, mediastinum, abdomen, pelvis, urinary bladder; most common in the retroperitoneum, first being the organ of Zuckerkandl at the IMA origin. Extra-adrenal tumors secrete only norepinephrine.
- **Suspect malignancy** when:
  1. Invasion and metastasis (most important)
  2. Weight > 500 gm — suspect malignancy first
  3. Location at the organ of Zuckerkandl — more often malignant than benign
  4. Female sex — suspicious of malignancy
  - Note: histology is a poor guide to malignant vs not — often wrong.
- **Triad of symptoms** suggesting the disease: HT with headache, palpitation, sweating = hypertensive spell.
- **Indications for investigation**
  1. Paroxysmal or severe sudden-onset HT, hard to control
  2. HT in pregnancy that is not toxemia — toxemia has edema, pheo does not
  3. Incidentaloma — may be subclinical pheo, 10%
  4. Odd diseases — after passing the board, feel free to forget them:
     - 4.1 Family history of pheo
     - 4.2 MEN 2
     - 4.3 Von Reckling Hausen or neurofibromatosis
     - 4.4 Von Hipple Lindau syndrome (as printed): pheo with possible retinal angioma, renal cyst or cancer, and pancreatic cystic tumor
     - 4.5 Sturge Weber syndrome
     - 4.6 Tuberous sclerosis
     - 4.7 Carney's syndrome: gastric leiosarcoma (as printed), pulmonary chondroma and extraparaganglioma
- **Caution** — in exam questions with these symptoms, beware pheo:
  1. Standing or sitting to urinate → faint, palpitation, sweating
  2. Headache after urinating
  3. In the OR, during intubation, abdominal prep, sterile draping or palpation around the kidney, BP shoots up — the local soothsayer would say today is unlucky, a doomsday, not a victory day: abort quickly and close the abdomen fast (as printed).

## Confirmed Diagnosis
1. **Plasma free metanephrine & normetanephrine** — breakdown products of circulating catecholamine; the most reliable test, best screening test and most sensitive test — sensitivity and specificity > 95%; then confirm with 24 hr urine metanephrines and catecholamines; if high again → pheo for sure.
2. Urine VMA — the old diagnostic test, little used now because of low specificity.
3. Clonidine suppression test — for patients with normal or slightly raised metanephrines; generally not needed.
4. Remember: invasive studies such as venous sampling are absolutely forbidden — they can induce crisis.
5. Serum catecholamine does not work well — fluctuates a lot.

## Localization
1. **CT or MRI** — pick one (MRI suits children, pregnant or breastfeeding women). For pheochromocytoma, scan from the neck down to the aortic bifurcation.
2. **MIBG scan** — now a second choice: cumbersome, time-consuming, adds little. If used, for patients < 50 years with suspected multifocality.
- **Note**: remember — venography is absolutely forbidden; it causes hypertensive crisis.

## Surgical Treatment
- Beware hypertensive crisis.
- **Preoperative preparation**: pheo makes both alpha and beta agonists, but alpha dominates → constant vasoconstriction → high BP, fast HR, low intravascular volume. So preparation = BP control plus volume expansion.
  1. **Alpha adrenergic blocker** — the first drug to reach for, to prevent hypertensive crisis during surgery. Formerly phenoxybenzamine 10 mg oral morning-evening for 1-4 wk before surgery, increasing up to 300-400 mg/day (as printed). Now selective alpha 1 blockers such as prazosin, doxazosin or terazosin with hydration, until BP about 160/90 or lower; HR target 60-80/min; or titrate until orthostatic hypotension appears, showing the dose controls BP well.
  2. BP controlled but HR still high or persistent arrhythmia → **betablocker** 10-40 mg every 6 hours.
  3. Pheochromocytoma surgery removes one adrenal only, so preoperative steroid is not needed.
- **Prohibitions** (ข้อห้าม)
  1. Betablocker may be given, but never before adequate alpha block
  2. Diuretic — worsens volume contraction (printed "protract volume")
- **Operative approach**
  1. **Benign pheo** — lap. adrenalectomy is now first choice; on entering, the first thing is to ligate the adrenal vein so catecholamine does not enter the circulation when the tumor is manipulated.
  2. **Malignant pheo**
     - 2.1 Resectable → open adrenalectomy
     - 2.2 Locally advanced → open debulking ± RT + PO prazosin
     - 2.3 Not worth operating → I131 MIBG ablation and chemo
     - 2.4 RFA, arterial embolization help somewhat
     - 2.5 Targeted therapy — sunitinib reportedly usable, better than nothing
  3. Intraoperative hypertensive crisis → vasodilator promptly, e.g. nitroprusside. When handling things in the abdomen, be gentle; at the adrenal, ligate the vein first.
  4. Intraoperative hypotension when the tumor comes out → vasodilatation; treat with rapid IV fluid, possibly add IV adrenalin.

## Adrenal Incidentaloma
- An adrenal mass found by chance on imaging, size > 1 cm, without any indication of adrenal disease, in a patient without history of cancer. Most common cause: nonfunctional cortical adenoma.
- **Incidence**
  1. 1-4% of CT abdomen
  2. Age < 30: 1%; age > 70: 7%
- **Etiology**
  1. Non-function 80%: nonfunctioning cortical adenoma, myelolipoma, adrenal cyst, adrenal hemorrhage, ganglioneuroma
  2. Cushing 5.3%
  3. Pheochromocytoma 5.1%
  4. Carcinoma 4.7%
  5. Metastatic carcinoma 2.5%
  6. Conn 1%
- Bilateral adrenal incidentaloma is uncommon; if found, may be:
  1. Cortical adenoma or hyperplasia
  2. Bilateral pheochromocytoma
  3. Myelolipomas
  4. Infection, e.g. TB, giving masses in both adrenals

## Diagnostic and Management
- **First step**: decide whether it is a functioning tumor — screen and confirm pheochromocytoma, Conn syndrome and Cushing syndrome first, using plasma fractionated metanephrine or 24 hour urine metanephrine, overnight dexamethasone suppression test, PAC : PRA.
- No biopsy — even FNA is unsafe: pheo → trouble; malignancy → seeding. Exception: suspected metastasis from a previous cancer of lung, breast, colon, kidney, melanoma, lymphoma.
- Figure caption (CT): CT with IV contrast showing bilateral enhancing adrenal incidentalomas (left 4.5 cm; right 1.5 cm).
- **Treatment**
  1. **All functioning tumors**, or symptoms from mass pressure → whatever the size, go to the OR for lap. adrenalectomy.
  2. **Non-functioning** → consider size and malignant-looking features (phenotype) on CT or MRI; sizes < 4 cm, 4-6 cm and > 6 cm:
     - 2.1 **< 4 cm and benign-looking on CT** → CT cuts 3-5 mm at 3 months and 12 months, then yearly CT for 2 years, with functional assessment every 12 months.
       - 2.1.1 Unchanged over 2 years → no further follow-up needed.
       - 2.1.2 Grows > 1 cm or becomes functioning → lap. adrenalectomy.
     - 2.2 **4-6 cm, or CT looks bad** (malignant-looking) — patient young and fit → now lap. adrenalectomy recommended.
     - 2.3 **> 6 cm** → adrenalectomy, except 2 cases needing nothing: HU density < 10 suggesting myelolipoma (as on the CT shown), or a completely cystic lesion without solid component.
  3. **History of cancer** (lung, breast, colon, kidney, melanoma or lymphoma) → suspect the adrenal incidentaloma is metastasis from that cancer:
     - 3.1 Surgical candidate → adrenalectomy; formerly open only; now if the capsule is intact, lap. is not wrong.
     - 3.2 Not a surgical candidate → FNA-B; if malignant, chemo before death (as printed). This is the only indication allowing FNA-B; anyone else doing it — off with their head (as printed).

## Summary Indications for Adrenalectomy for Incidentaloma
1. All functioning tumors
2. Non-functioning > 4 cm
3. During follow-up, grows or becomes functioning
4. Symptomatic tumor
5. CT or MRI features suspicious for malignancy:
   - 5.1 Irregular unclear margin
   - 5.2 Inhomogeneous texture (heterogeneity)
   - 5.3 Hemorrhage, necrosis
   - 5.4 Calcification (very suspicious)
   - 5.5 Evidence of invasion — lymph node enlargement, liver metastasis
   - 5.6 Size > 6 cm: cancer risk up to 35% (size > 6 cm is the most suggestive sign); 4-6 cm: 10%; < 4 cm: < 2%
   - 5.7 MRI bright signal on T2 Wt. (adrenal mass to liver ratio = 1.2 : 2.8) with enhancement and slow washout after gadolinium contrast
   - 5.8 CT non-contrast HU density > 18, and contrast washout ratio < 40% at 15 minutes
- **Notes** (หมายเหตุ)
  1. HU < 18: usually benign — plenty of fat
  2. HU < 10: certainly benign
  3. HU > 18: little intracellular fat — found in cancer and pheochromocytoma

(PAC/PRA; plasma-free metanephrines and normetanephrines; overnight 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test) → Yes: adrenalectomy; No: follow patient with repeat CT scan and biochemical evaluation.

- Figure: Schwartz incidentaloma questions, as steps —

  2. Is the tumor metastatic? Past history of cancer? — Yes → solitary metastasis? yes → consider adrenalectomy; no → systemic therapy. No → question 3.
  3. Is it at high risk of being malignant? — < 4 cm, benign imaging features → repeat imaging in 3-6 months, biochemical evaluation annually. ≥ 4 cm, indeterminate or suspicious imaging features → adrenalectomy.

## Adrenocortical Carcinoma
- Women > men; mostly unilateral; bilateral very rare.
- Bimodal distribution — peaks at age 5 and 50.
- 2/3 secrete biologically active hormone: first cortisol 30%, then aldosterone; androgen and estrogen < 10%.
- 50% of patients have a functioning tumor; the other 50% are non-functioning, presenting with mass effect.
- Children: 90% virilization — boys get precocious puberty, gynecomastia, gonadal atrophy, loss of libido; girls get virilization, hirsutism with hair loss on the head, irregular menses.
- Adult women: usually functioning tumor with rapid onset of Cushing's syndrome & virilizing features; adult men: more often nonfunctioning than functioning.
- 80% are already advanced stage at diagnosis.
- **Features suspicious of malignancy**
  1. Abdominal pain, weight loss, weakness
  2. Mass > 6 cm or > 100 gm
  3. Local invasion, enlarged lymph nodes
  4. Evidence of metastasis
  5. Feminization (printed "ferminization") and virilization
- **Diagnosis**
  1. R/O pheo, Conn, Cushing
  2. CT with IV contrast — suspect malignancy if size > 6 cm (92%) or malignant-looking features
  3. Biopsy of an adrenal lesion is not needed — too little tissue to separate adenoma from cancer, plus seeding risk
- **Prognosis** — 5-year survival:
  1. Localized disease: 40-60%
  2. Metastasis +ve: no 5-yr survival; 1-year survival only 10%
- **Treatment**
  1. Open en bloc resection — lap. only if the capsule is intact; so answer "open" first — mom won't scold (as printed). Include contiguous lymph nodes and organs stuck to the mass, e.g. diaphragm, kidney, pancreas, liver, IVC.
  2. Locally advanced → debulking + RT + chemo (mititane, as printed)

## Adrenal Metastases
- In a patient with underlying malignancy — especially small cell lung, renal cell, melanoma, breast, stomach, HCC, esophagus — an adrenal mass on imaging is adrenal metastasis first.
- **CT**: irregular border, hemorrhage, necrosis; non-enhanced CT density > 20 HU.
- **PET**: very useful — should be done.
- Figure caption: lung cancer metastatic to the left adrenal gland on CT and PET.
- Rule out pheochromocytoma first; if not pheo, needle it and send for examination.
- **Treatment**
  1. Isolated adrenal metastasis with the 1ry cancer controlled → adrenalectomy, open or lap.; can prolong survival 2-3 years.
  2. Palliative care if not operable.

## Adrenal Insufficiency
- Most common cause: long-term exogenous steroid administration = 2ry adrenal insufficiency.
- 1ry adrenal insufficiency is most often autoimmune.
- Figure: Addison's disease — bronze pigmentation of skin, changes in distribution of body hair, GI disturbances, weakness, hypoglycemia, postural hypotension, weight loss. Adrenal crisis: profound fatigue, dehydration, vascular collapse (↓BP), renal shut down, ↓serum Na, ↑serum K.
- **Symptoms**: tired, weak, weight loss, listless, drowsy.
- **Lab**: cortisol low, ACTH high, hypoglycemia, aldosterone low.
- **Treatment**: corticosteroid.

## Adrenal Crisis
- Acute adrenal insufficiency, usually on top of existing chronic adrenal insufficiency — a patient on long-term steroids who was not given steroid preop. During or after surgery: **unexplained shock** not responding to IV resuscitation, with fever, tachycardia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, dehydration, weight loss, drowsiness.
- **Lab**: hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia.
- Beware trick questions on 2 points: "hypertension" — correct is BP drop; "hypernatremia" — correct is hyponatremia.

### Clinical and laboratory findings suggesting adrenal crisis
- Dehydration, hypotension
- Nausea and vomiting with a history of weight loss and anorexia
- Abdominal pain, so-called "acute abdomen"
- Unexplained hypoglycemia
- Unexplained fever
- Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypercalcemia
- Hyperpigmentation or vitiligo

- **Treatment**
  1. Hydrocortisone 100 mg + 5% D/NSS 1,000 cc IV at 10 mg/hr over 10 hours — better than IV push. The patient improves within 1-2 hours; continue 10 mg/kg/hr (as printed) + 5% D/NSS IV drip for at least 2-3 days; start tapering on day 2, down to 100 mg/day.
  2. Day 3: maintenance with prednisolone (5 mg) at the usual previous dose, morning-evening after meals, continuing.
  3. A patient on long-term steroid needing surgery → continue preoperative steroid dosing; during surgery give 50-100 mg hydrocortisone IV.
  4. Knowing the idea is enough — once in practice, let the medicine and anesthesia doctors look after it, better.
- Issue the surgical "tent" wants you to know: preoperative preparation for patients on long-term steroid, e.g. ITP, SLE:
  1. **Minor surgery** (e.g. hernia) → IV hydrocortisone 25 mg, a single dose before surgery is enough; then continue oral prednisolone.
  2. **Major surgery** (e.g. Whipple) → IV hydrocortisone 100-150 mg/day for about 2-3 days; once eating, give oral.

## Adrenalectomy
- Open and lap.
- Open: ipsilateral or bilateral subcostal incision (anterior approach), and thoracoabdominal approach for very large masses.
- Figure: open right adrenalectomy exposure — liver retracted, gallbladder, right adrenal vein entering the inferior vena cava, duodenum and pancreas swept medially, transverse colon below.

## Laparoscopic Adrenalectomy
- **Indications**
  1. Aldosteronoma
  2. Cushing's syndrome
  3. Pheochromocytoma
  4. Incidentaloma size 4-6 cm
  5. Small virilizing adenoma
  6. Single site of adrenal metastasis
  7. Small things such as myelolipoma, cyst, ganglioneuroma
- **Absolute contraindications**
  1. Size > 10 cm (the decoy is > 6 cm)
  2. Feminizing tumor (printed "ferminizing") — high malignancy risk
  3. Malignancy or suspected malignancy — the author thinks this is more relative: an experienced surgeon can use lap. for adrenocortical carcinoma without obvious invasion.
- **Relative contraindications**: usually technical difficulty for lap, e.g. previous surgery in the area or heavy adhesions.
- **Advantages of lap. over open adrenalectomy**: shorter hospital stay, fewer wound infections, less analgesic; disadvantages: longer operative time and higher cost.
- **Operative approach** — 3, summarized for the exam:
  1. **Transperitoneal lateral** — via the flank
  2. **Transperitoneal anterior** — via the front of the abdomen
     - The tent wants you to know pros and cons of both: pro — wide working space; con — exposure of the adrenal, especially the right with its short vein, is difficult.
     - Remember: both are unsuitable for right adrenalectomy.
  3. **Posterior retroperitoneal approach** — patient prone, trocars in the back. Pro: direct access to the gland; well suited to the right adrenal (remember). Con: narrow working space and less familiar anatomy.
- Figure: lateral transperitoneal port positions (10 mm and 5 mm cannulas, optional 5 mm); prone jack-knife positioning for the posterior retroperitoneal approach.

## Robotic Adrenalectomy
- Reports of nearly 200 robotic cases show intraoperative and postoperative complications no different from lap.; comparative studies with lap. on operative time, blood loss (printed "lossm") and hospital stay are awaited.
