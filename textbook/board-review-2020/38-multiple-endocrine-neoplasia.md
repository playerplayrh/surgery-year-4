# Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 861-865 (PDF pages 1958-1962)

## Introduction
- A single endocrine disease is already enough to wear a resident out; MEN, which bundles several endocrine diseases into one, leaves residents feeling flattened (as printed, a joke).
- Most teachers have never seen a MEN patient themselves and rely on textbooks and articles; past exams show MEN is a syndrome to memorize by rote (ท่องจำ). With a good basic grasp of adrenal, thyroid and parathyroid disease, the questions are manageable.

## Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia (MEN)
- A syndrome of abnormalities in several endocrine glands at once.
- Inheritance: autosomal dominant (AD). As repeatedly said, genetic diseases in the surgery exam are AD 99.99% of the time (as printed: "if you have to guess, guess AD").
- Large US centers now do genetic testing easily, so more MEN patients are being found.
- Types:
  1. **MEN 1** or Wermer's syndrome — defect in a tumor suppressor gene (จำ), the MENIN gene
  2. **MEN 2** — mutation of the RET protooncogene (จำ); subdivided into:
     - 2.1 MEN 2A or Sipple's syndrome
     - 2.2 MEN 2B

## MEN 1
- Made up of the "3P" group plus minor (small-print) diseases:
  1. **Parathyroid** — most common, up to 98%; mostly hyperplasia
  2. **Pancreas** — second, 50-75%. Before 2006 most were thought nonfunctioning; now pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PETs) in MEN 1 are more often functioning than nonfunctioning. PETs in MEN 1:
     - No. 1 = gastrinoma 60-80% — unlike sporadic PETs, where No. 1 is insulinoma (do not mix them up)
     - No. 2 = insulinoma 20-30%; the rest are minor tumors, e.g. glucagonoma, vipoma
  3. **Pituitary** — 35%, mostly adenoma
     - No. 1 = prolactinoma
     - Next: growth hormone, ACTH, or in some cases non-functioning tumor
  4. **Minor diseases** (กลุ่มโรคจิ๊บจ๊อย) that may appear as answer choices: lipoma, carcinoid, thyroid adenoma, ependymoma, facial angiofibroma, adrenal hyperplasia
- Beware: pheochromocytoma, carotid body tumor and MTC are standard distractors — they belong to MEN 2, not MEN 1.
- Remember: MEN with pancreas and pituitary involvement = MEN 1 only.
- Patients are usually aged 20-40 years; lab abnormalities appear about 10 years before symptoms.
- MEN 1 produces many hormones, but usually only one hormone dominates the clinical pattern.
- Genetic counselling for MEN 1 families. Carriers with +ve genetic testing need yearly calcium, PTH, gastrin and prolactin, starting per this guideline (Sabiston 2017 p. 999):
  1. Age 5 years: pituitary screening with prolactin level; if raised → MRI brain
  2. Age 8 years: parathyroid screening with serum calcium and PTH
  3. Age 20 years: pancreatic screening with serum gastrin, chromogranin A, proinsulin, glucagon
- Causes of death in MEN 1: PETs and thymic carcinoid.

## MEN1 & Parathyroid
- Suspect MEN 1 when HPT occurs in a patient:
  1. About 30 years old (sporadic HPT is usually > 50 years)
  2. With a family history of HPT, and the glands show hyperplasia
- If MEN is suspected, do genetic testing to settle it.
- Hypercalcemia worsens gastrinoma-related problems, e.g. peptic ulcer or pancreatitis. So the indication threshold for HPT surgery in MEN 1 is lowered (operate earlier); otherwise indications are similar to HPT in the parathyroid chapter.
- **Treatment** — treat as hyperplasia; parathyroid disease in MEN 1 is not cancer, so it is not very worrying. Two options:
  1. Total parathyroidectomy + autotransplantation
  2. Subtotal parathyroidectomy
  - Mainstream opinion leans toward total (author agrees), because recurrent hypercalcemia is then easy to handle; exception: Greenfield 2017 p. 1348-1349 favours subtotal.
- In MEN 1 needing treatment, the first thing to treat is the parathyroid.

## MEN1 & Pancreas
- Among PETs in MEN 1, No. 1 is gastrinoma; per NCCN it is usually multicentric with a high chance of malignancy, up to 90%.
- Gastrinoma in MEN 1 presents with peptic ulcer as in sporadic disease. Hormone levels usually rise about 10 years before symptoms, then it becomes malignant — so surgeons should treat before it turns malignant.
- Localization: besides MRI, CT, PET and IOUS, do not forget the duodenal wall — open the duodenum or use intraoperative endoscope.
- **Treatment**
  1. Resect as much as possible if resectable, even with liver met. (as printed: "cut what can be cut — die or live, sick or well")
  2. PPI can be used, but its effect is weaker than in sporadic disease.

## MEN 1 & Pituitary
- Good news: mostly benign adenoma; bad news: it sits in the skull near the senses — the author handed most of it back to the teachers already in medical school (as printed, a joke).
- **Prolactinoma**
  - Most common
  - Women: galactorrhea, infertility, amenorrhea
  - Men: impotence, small penis (as printed), mass effect (pressing around the sella)
- **Growth hormone, ACTH and non-functioning tumor** — less common.
- **Hormone study**: serum prolactin, growth hormone, ACTH.
- **Imaging of choice**: MRI.
- **Treatment**
  1. Prolactinoma is the one pituitary adenoma to treat with drugs: dopamine agonist, e.g. bromocriptine or cabergoline.
  2. Others: little to remember — if operable, transsphenoidal resection through the nose; if not completely removed, follow with RT.

## MEN 2
- Two main groups: MEN 2A and MEN 2B.

## MEN 2A
- Consists of:
  1. **Thyroid** — MTC 100% (1° cause of death)
  2. **Adrenal** — pheochromocytoma 50%
  3. **Parathyroid** — hyperplasia 20%
  4. Minor: lichen planus amyloidosis, Hirschsprung
- Initial symptom: thyroid mass.

## MEN 2B
- Picture President Lincoln's appearance — online rumour says he had MEN 2B and, had he not been shot, might have died of it (told only as a story).

| MEN2B feature | Abraham Lincoln |
|---|---|
| "Marfanoid" body shape | Tall; long limbs; small, thin head |
| Large, bumpy lips | Large lower lip; bumps on lower and upper lips |
| Intestinal symptoms | Constipated |
| Cancer | Emaciated by the end of his life |
| Dominant genetics | Mother with same body build; three sons with large, bumpy lips |
| Muscular involvement | Multiple signs of low muscle tone |

- Figure: MEN 2B features — mucosal neuromas (lips/tongue), Marfanoid habitus, medullated corneal nerve fibres.
- MEN 2B consists of:
  1. **Thyroid** — MTC 75-95%; slightly less often than in MEN 2A but far more vicious and aggressive than MTC in MEN 2A
  2. **Adrenal** — pheochromocytoma
  3. Minor: Marfan's habitus and mucosal neuroma
- MTC is still the 1° cause of death in 2B, as in 2A.

## MEN 2 & Pheochromocytoma
- Must be investigated urgently and treated first.
- For the approach, see the adrenal gland chapter.
- Often multifocal and bilateral, but luckily mostly benign.
- **Treatment**: bilateral adrenalectomy followed by steroid replacement.

## MEN 2 & MTC
- Sporadic MTC usually occurs at age 40-50; MTC in MEN 2 occurs at age ≤ 30.
- Suspect MEN when MTC is in a young patient, multifocal, and bilateral.
- If MEN 2 is suspected, check serum calcitonin and RET gene mutation.
- **Treatment**
  - After the pheochromocytoma is dealt with, treat MTC: total thyroidectomy + bilat. MRND.
  - Debated point: especially in MEN 2A with a 20% chance of HPT — since total thyroidectomy is being done anyway, some add total parathyroidectomy with parathyroid implanted in the forearm at the same time, because operating later is very hard.
  - NCCN 2006 gave a neat answer: remove only the largest of the 4 glands first and clip-mark the remaining glands for later treatment if needed.
- **Prophylactic total thyroidectomy + bilateral CND** — for patients with RET mutation, because MTC is certain to come:
  1. MEN 2A: operate when the child is about 5 years old
  2. MEN 2B: operate as soon as RET +ve is diagnosed, or at age 6 months, because MTC in 2B is very aggressive
- **Summary for 2A and 2B**
  1. MTC comes first: young age and aggressive
  2. Pheochromocytoma: investigate first and treat first
  3. Screening test: genetic testing for RET mutation
  4. High calcium → likely 2A; Marfan-like face with a bumpy tongue → think MEN 2B
