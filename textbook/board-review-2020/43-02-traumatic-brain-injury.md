# Traumatic Brain Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 59-110 (PDF pages 2193-2244) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated ๒๕ ธันวาคม ๒๕๕๘)

## Introduction
- Brain injury from road traffic accidents is now very common, and the general surgeon is usually the first one consulted. When referral is impossible we may have to do the craniotomy ourselves — so during the neuro rotation, scrub in on head operations often; once posted to a provincial hospital you will certainly be opening skulls (น้องได้เปิดกระโหลกแน่).
- ATLS 10th edition: if you are not in a center, early referral matters more than doing a CT.

## Anatomy
- Only the basics.
- **Scalp** — 5 layers: skin, connective tissue, aponeurosis (galea aponeurotica), loose areolar tissue, pericranium.
- Subgaleal hematoma (หัวโน) = blood collecting under the loose areolar tissue, above the pericranium.
- The scalp has a rich blood supply; beware children and the elderly — a scalp laceration can bleed until shock.
- **Brain**
  1. Cerebrum — split by the falx into left and right hemispheres
  2. Cerebellum — controls coordination and balance
- **Meninges** — 3 layers
  1. Dura mater — besides covering the brain, folds into sheets that divide the brain into compartments
     - 1.1 Falx cerebri — divides the brain vertically (sagittal) into left and right
     - 1.2 Tentorium cerebelli — divides the brain horizontally into 2 compartments: supratentorial and infratentorial
  2. Arachnoid — beneath it (subarachnoid space) lie CSF and small blood vessels
  3. Pia mater — directly on the brain tissue
- **Ventricular system** — choroid plexus makes about 20 cc of CSF per hour.
- **CSF circulation**: lateral ventricle → foramen of Monro → third ventricle → aqueduct of Sylvius → fourth ventricle → subarachnoid space over brain and cord → reabsorbed into the venous circulation. Bleeding can therefore disturb CSF absorption.

## Neurological Examination
- Written as the author sees fit — only what one should know.
- **Level of consciousness**
  1. Drowsy — sleepy; wakes when roused, then falls back asleep
  2. Confusion — confused, agitated, talks nonsense
  3. Stuporous — unresponsive lying still; with deep pain still shows some response, e.g. pushes the hand away
  4. Coma — no response to deep pain, or decerebrate posture; at this point the patient needs a tube
- **Eye examination** (emphasized)
  1. Look at the eyelids: bruising like a panda (raccoon eye) → suspect fracture base of skull at the anterior fossa
  2. Check pupils before sedating the patient, especially before endotracheal intubation
     - 2.1 Dilated pupil → think of uncal herniation compressing CN3
     - 2.2 Pinpoint pupil → think of a lesion around the pons
  3. Eye movement
     - 3.1 Eyes turned to one side, or conjugate deviation → the lesion is probably on that side
     - 3.2 Only one eye deviated → think of CN3, 4, 6 injury on that side
     - 3.3 Check doll's eye too; if found (as printed: get ready to ask for organ donation)
- If C-spine injury has not been excluded, do not test doll's eye or stiff neck.
- **Cushing reflex** — high ICP compressing the brainstem → high BP, slow pulse, and breathing that is irregular — sometimes fast, sometimes slow, deep.

## Signs and Symptom of Increased ICP
1. Worsening consciousness — drowsiness is the most common initial symptom
2. Progressive headache
3. Vomiting
4. Cushing response: high BP, slow HR, wide pulse pressure, slow but deep breathing
5. Pyramidal tract signs, e.g. hemiplegia, hyperreflexia

## Brain Herniation
- The brain is divided into smaller compartments by:
  1. Falx cerebri — in the midline, on the upper part of the cerebrum
  2. Tentorium cerebelli — above the cerebellum, posteriorly
- **Monro-Kellie Doctrine Hypothesis**: the brain is a noncompressible structure inside a nonexpandable skull; contents = brain tissue, blood volume and CSF. With an intracranial mass (blood, abscess or tumor) the brain compensates by reducing blood volume and CSF volume. (Figure shows normal → compensated with normal ICP → decompensated with raised ICP.)
- When ICP rises (e.g. bleeding or brain swelling) the brain can herniate in 3 directions:
1. **Transtentorial herniation**
   - 1.1 **Central herniation** — swelling or bleeding right in the middle of the brain pushes it straight down through the tentorial hiatus onto the brainstem. Features:
     1. Cheyne-Stokes breathing (printed "chyne stroke")
     2. Posture starts as decorticate (abnormal flexion), then decerebrate (abnormal extension) — limbs stiff, back arched
     3. Pinpoint pupil
     - With all 3 and still not relieved → compresses the medulla; death is certain.
   - 1.2 **Uncal herniation** — from bleeding at the side of the brain, usually temporal lobe; pushes the uncus (lower part of the temporal lobe) down onto CN3; untreated, it continues down onto the medulla and breathing stops. Features:
     1. Pupil dilated on the same side
     2. Hemiplegia on the opposite side
   - 1.3 **Kernohan herniation** — instead of moving downward, the brain shifts and presses against the skull on the opposite side → dilated pupil and weakness on the same side.
2. **Tonsillar herniation** (cerebellar herniation, foramen magnum herniation) — usually from bleeding in the posterior fossa, which has very little space; a small amount of blood pushes the cerebellar tonsil down onto the medulla → unconscious, apnea, rapid death. The worst form of brain herniation (จำ).
3. (Numbered "1" as printed) **Cirgular herniation** (as printed) or **falcine herniation** — swelling of one lobe (not the whole brain) shifts the brain sideways to compress the opposite side, without going downward as in transtentorial or tonsillar herniation. Only weakness of the opposite arm and leg.

## Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)
- Started at Glasgow University (Scotland), published in the Lancet to rate the severity of brain injury; easy to score, convenient and quick, so it is the most widely used scale today.
- Components:
  1. Eye opening (E) — simply, if the patient opens the eyes spontaneously and is fully awake, the brainstem is working well
  2. Verbal response (V)
  3. Motor response (M) — the M score matters most for predicting outcome, both survival and functional status; M is more reliable than E and V
- If motor response differs between the 2 sides, score the side with the best response.
- Decorticate: arms, wrists and fingers flexed in toward the body, legs extended with feet pointed down → lesion still above the midbrain or pons.
- Decerebrate: arms and legs extended, toes pointed, back arched (opisthotonus) → lesion already at midbrain level.
- Score range 3-15 (E4V5M6).
- Score ≤ 13 → should refer to a trauma center.
- Score ≤ 8 → endotracheal tube; below 5 → 50% chance of death.
- Score 3 → as printed, book the funeral hall or ask for organ donation (จองศาลา หรือขออวัยวะ).
- **Severity**
  1. Mild or minor brain injury — GCS 13-15
  2. Moderate brain injury — GCS 9-12
  3. Severe brain injury — GCS 3-8

## Classifications of Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)
1. **Primary TBI** — injury from the impact moment acting directly on the brain; brain damage occurs at once, from the start.
2. **Secondary TBI** — TBI that occurs afterwards, distinguished from primary TBI; may result from brain hypoxia following primary TBI, or from causes outside the skull that starve the brain of oxygen, e.g. shock, infection, hypoxia, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia.

## Primary TBI
- By biomechanism of injury to the brain — 2 types:
  1. **Focal brain damage** — localized pathology from a direct blow to the brain
     - 1.1 Vascular injury, e.g. epidural, subdural, intracerebral, subarachnoid hemorrhage
     - 1.2 Parenchymal injury, e.g. cerebral contusion, cerebral laceration
  2. **Diffuse brain injuries** — the brain is shaken by rotational force → stretching or tearing of axons → impaired axon transport. Mild force → only concussion, which recovers; very severe → diffuse axon injury (DAI), which can leave a persistent vegetative state (นอนเป็นผัก). DAI is a serious problem.
- By severity (GCS), primary TBI has 3 groups:
  1. Minor brain injury (GCS 13-15)
  2. Moderate brain injury (GCS 9-12)
  3. Severe brain injury (GCS 3-8)

## Skull Fracture
- **Closed skull fracture**: skull broken with scalp intact — usually needs no treatment, but beware a hematoma under the fracture line.
- In children, telling a diastatic fracture from a skull fracture: a diastatic fracture runs along a suture line, with suture width > 3 mm.
- **Open skull fracture**: the scalp is torn as well.
- **Indication for surgery**
  1. Gross wound contamination
  2. Depressed fracture 8-10 mm or more
  3. Depressed fracture with significant underlying intracranial hematoma, pneumocephalus, frontal sinus involvement, or focal neurological deficit from compression of brain parenchyma
  4. Persistent CSF leakage
  5. Gross cosmetic deformity

## Cerebral Concussion
- A temporary, reversible physiological change in brain function with no anatomical damage — the brain tissue still looks smooth and normal.
- Brief loss of consciousness, "not more than 10 วินาที, not more than 6 ชั่วโมง" (as printed); no memory of events during the blackout.
- CT normal.

## Cerebral Contusion
- Brain tissue bruised and swollen, with patchy bleeding spots in the white matter (printed "white mater").
- Like concussion but unconsciousness lasts longer (up to 24 h); some have focal loss of function of that part of the brain.
- CT: early on shows bruises as white spots, but on follow-up about 20% of presumed contusions turn into intracerebral hematoma, which is treated differently — bleeds may need surgery.
- So ATLS recommends repeat CT scan 12-24 h after the initial scan.

## Epidural Hematoma
- From tearing of the middle meningeal artery, between the outer layer of dura and the inner table of skull.
- Often with temporal bone fracture, where the hematoma is commonly found.
- Patient often has brief loss of consciousness or a lucid interval, then becomes agitated and restless, then drowsy, with an ipsilateral dilated pupil and contralateral hemiplegia, hyperreflexia and positive Babinski → uncal herniation has begun; little time left — act fast.
- Lucid interval: Western texts warn of "talk and die".
- Short retrograde amnesia → better prognosis than long amnesia.
- CT: white blood shadow shaped like a convex lens — biconvex or lenticular shape.
- **Treatment — operate if:**
  1. Clinical deterioration
  2. Significant mass effect
     - 2.1 Midline shift > 5 mm
     - 2.2 Thickness of hematoma > 15 mm
     - 2.3 Supratentorial hematoma > 30 cc
     - 2.4 Infratentorial hematoma > 10 cc

## Subdural Hematoma
- More common than epidural; from tearing of bridging veins between the inner layer of dura and the arachnoid mater. Blood spreads over the whole hemisphere, not localized like epidural.
- Prognosis much worse than epidural, as cerebral contusion usually coexists.
- **Presentation** — 3 phases:
  1. Acute — within 72 h of injury; usually severe with high mortality; CT shows blood as a hyperdense lesion (printed "hyperdencity")
  2. Subacute — 72 h to 3 weeks; suspicious feature: the patient keeps complaining the headache never goes away; CT isodense (printed "isodence")
  3. Chronic — after 3 weeks; classic in the elderly, alcoholics, or very young children who may have no trauma history. Clot liquefies → osmotic pressure draws fluid across a fibrinous membrane → the collection slowly enlarges until signs of raised ICP; CT density is hypodense (printed "hypodence").
- CT: blood seen as a white band shaped like a concave lens or a crescent moon (crescent shape), plus brain contusions as scattered bruise spots or small white patches, mostly at the lower frontal and temporal tips.
- **Treatment**: when there are signs of significant mass effect.

## Intracerebral Hematoma
- From tearing of an artery or vein within the brain tissue.
- Fairly severe symptoms depending on the amount and site of bleeding; often with cerebral contusion at the frontotemporal region or basal ganglia.
- Patient starts complaining of headache, with sudden loss of consciousness, signs of raised ICP and brain herniation.
- **Treatment**: operate if there is significant mass effect.

## Intraventricular Hemorrhage
- Bleeding directly into a ventricle, or subarachnoid bleeding that flows back into the ventricle.
- Symptoms from hydrocephalus: raised ICP, headache or double vision.
- **Treatment**: ventriculostomy.

## Subarachnoid Hemorrhage
- From tearing of veins in the subarachnoid space; blood mixed with CSF does not clot.
- **Symptoms** from meningeal irritation:
  1. Worst headache of one's life — RBCs block the small pores in the subarachnoid layer → poor CSF absorption, and RBCs irritate → arachnoiditis → raised ICP → extremely severe headache
  2. Neck pain, stiff neck (nuchal rigidity)
  3. Photophobia
- CT: hyperdense area of blood coating the brain's folds, running into the cerebral sulci.
- **Treatment**
  1. Small bleed, mild headache → symptomatic treatment; once RBCs are hydrolysed the headache resolves
  2. Severe headache from raised ICP → LP to lower pressure and help remove RBCs — but no LP if there is an expanding lesion: if herniation occurs, as printed, the one who did the tap will have bad luck all year (รับรองคนเจาะหลังซวยข้ามปี)
  3. If RBCs block flow and cause hydrocephalus → VP shunt

## Indication for Surgery in TBI
1. Raised ICP with rapid clinical deterioration
2. CT midline shift ≥ 5 mm
3. Intracerebral hematoma larger than 2 cm
4. Posterior fossa hematoma
5. Compound depressed skull fracture
6. Penetrating head injury

## Management of TBI
- Follow ATLS. If the patient talks sensibly, take the history of mechanism of injury: any blackout, how long, memory of the event, how bad the headache.
- Examine to exclude severe or serious injury first, then do the neuro exam.
- Exclude cervical spine injury.
- Grade by GCS into 3: mild, moderate and severe TBI.
- Check the criteria for admission and for CT brain.
- TBI with shock → find the cause of shock first: bleeding in chest, abdomen, pelvis, or from fractures.
- Non-responders to fluid who stay in shock with intra-abdominal bleeding → severe; open the abdomen first, CT brain later — CT brain can be sent after laparotomy.
- Responders or partial responders → CT brain together with CT of other body parts is acceptable.
- Fluid in TBI with shock: beware — overloading causes brain swelling.
- Remember: in TBI the best predictor of outcome is the presence of shock — the longer the shock, the greater the chance of a vegetative state (นอนเป็นผัก).

## Imaging Studies in TBI
- No routine skull film — rarely helps in choosing treatment.
- **Indications for CT scan**
  1. Abnormal symptoms, e.g. vomiting more than 2 times, hemiparesis, aphasia
  2. Unconscious > 5 min, retrograde amnesia > 30 min
  3. Severe or dangerous mechanism, e.g. hit by a car and thrown, fall down stairs > 5 steps, fall from > 3 feet, or penetrating head injury
  4. Age > 65 years or poor mental status
  5. GCS < 15 more than 2 h after injury
  6. Moderate or severe TBI
  7. Compound or depressed skull fracture
  8. Signs of fracture base of skull, e.g. raccoon eye with clear fluid leaking from the nose
- **Indication for follow-up CT scan** (within 12-24 hours)
  1. Abnormal initial CT
  2. Neurological status deterioration
  3. Preferably before discharge in moderate TBI

## Mild Traumatic Brain Injury (GCS 13-15)
- About 80% of patients; usually loss of consciousness < 20 min with post-traumatic amnesia, and no focal pathology such as contusion, EDH, SDH.
- Only 3% of mild TBI deteriorate.
- Mortality < 1%.
- Some can be discharged home directly if no admission criteria are met.
- **Indication for admission**
  1. Children, elderly, intoxicated, living far from hospital, or GCS < 15
  2. Risky medical condition, e.g. underlying coagulopathy
  3. Persisting neurological signs or symptoms, e.g. impaired consciousness
  4. CT scan not available → admit and observe first
  5. Indication for CT present
  6. Skull film (if done) shows skull fracture
- Before discharge: advise patient and relatives, give a warning sheet, and do not forget the follow-up appointment.
- Figure: Algorithm for management of minor brain injury (ATLS Figure 6-8), as steps —
  1. Definition: patient awake, may be oriented (GCS 13-15).
  2. History: name, age, sex, race, occupation; mechanism and time of injury; loss of consciousness immediately post-injury; subsequent level of alertness; amnesia (retrograde, antegrade); headache (mild, moderate, severe).
  3. General examination to exclude systemic injuries → limited neurologic examination → cervical spine and other imaging as indicated → blood-alcohol level and urine toxicology screen → CT head if criteria for high or moderate risk of neurosurgical intervention are present.
  4. Observe or admit if: no CT scanner, abnormal CT, all penetrating head injuries, history of prolonged loss of consciousness, deteriorating consciousness, moderate to severe headache, significant alcohol/drug intoxication, skull fracture, CSF leak (rhinorrhea or otorrhea), significant associated injuries, no reliable companion at home, abnormal GCS (< 15), focal neurologic defects.
  5. Otherwise discharge: meets none of the admission criteria; discuss need to return if problems develop and issue a "warning sheet"; schedule follow-up visit.

## Moderate Traumatic Brain Injury (GCS 9-12)
- Can still talk sensibly but is confused, maybe mild hemiparesis; about 10% deteriorate to severe TBI.
- Admit every case; after the primary survey call neuro to help — in provincial hospitals that is usually us (the general surgeon) acting as neuro.
- CT brain in every case, and possibly follow-up CT in 12-24 h if the patient worsens.
- Care as for severe TBI, but no ET tube needed yet.
- Figure: Algorithm for management of moderate brain injury (ATLS Figure 6-10), as steps —
  1. Definition: GCS 9-12.
  2. Initial examination: as for mild head injury plus baseline blood work; CT head in all cases; admit to a facility capable of definitive neurosurgical care.
  3. After admission: frequent neurologic checks; follow-up CT if condition deteriorates or preferably before discharge.
  4. If patient improves (90%) → discharge when appropriate, follow-up in clinic.
  5. If patient deteriorates (10%) → if the patient stops following simple commands, repeat CT and manage per severe brain injury protocol.

## Severe Traumatic Brain Injury (GCS 3-8)
- After the primary survey, intubate and ventilate; neuro wants CT brain fast — if there is a brain lesion needing surgery, straight to OR.
- Figure: Severe brain injury management (ATLS), as steps —
  1. Definition: unable to follow even simple commands because of impaired consciousness (GCS 3-8).
  2. Assessment and management: ABCDEs; primary survey and resuscitation; secondary survey and AMPLE history; admit to a facility capable of definitive neurosurgical care; therapeutic agents (usually after consulting a neurosurgeon) — mannitol, moderate hyperventilation (PCO2 32-35 mmHg), hypertonic saline; neurologic reevaluation — GCS (eye, motor, verbal), pupillary light response, focal neurologic exam.
  3. Then CT scan.
- Before sending to the CT scanner, the urgent next step is to hyperventilate together with mannitol 1 gm/kg to lower intracranial pressure quickly.
- If the brain lesion does not yet need surgery → ICP monitor to measure ICP and calculate CPP (printed "CCP"); always keep ICP < 20 mmHg.
- ICP measurement: ventricular catheter connected to a transducer; pressure > 20 → treat; CSF can also be drained through this catheter.
- ET intubation in severe TBI is fairly difficult, especially with seizure/spasm (เกร็ง); technique should be rapid sequence with cervical spine protection.
- Keep PaCO2 32-35 mmHg and PaO2 ≥ 100 mmHg (never below 60): CO2, like lactic acid (printed "lactic aid"), causes cerebral vasodilatation → more intracranial blood volume → more brain swelling.
- With high ICP, may hyperventilate to PaCO2 30-35 mmHg → vasoconstriction lowers intracranial pressure; but never below 30 — excessive cerebral vasoconstriction dangerously cuts cerebral blood flow.
- Head up about 20-30° lowers cerebral venous pressure (venous blood returns to the heart faster).
- **Analgesia** — reduces agitation and thus ICP: MO 4 mg continuous drip alternating with valium or midazolam; downside — harder to assess GCS. If sedation is needed, use propofol.
- **Antipyretic** — fever causes vasodilatation, raises cerebral blood flow and ICP → paracetamol or cooling blanket.
- **Fluid** in TBI: isotonic NSS; no hypotonic saline or glucose (worsens brain swelling). ATLS 9th ed recommends 3% hypertonic saline when BP drops with raised ICP.
- Keep SBP > 90, but not too high (brain swelling).
- Keep Hct > 30%.
- **Anticonvulsant**, e.g. phenytoin or dilantin, still given for the first 7 days to prevent early seizure; it cannot prevent late seizure. There are now warnings that early anticonvulsants should be given only when needed, as they may slow brain recovery.
- **Antibiotic** — reduces pneumonia but not overall mortality or length of stay → not recommended as routine.
- Foley catheter; NG tube for decompression early and for feeding later.
- **Nutrition** — start within 72 h of injury, full options within 7 days.
- **DVT prophylaxis** — wrap legs with elastic bandage or pneumatic compression device; no LMWH in the first 3 days.
- Useless and never to be given: **steroid** — does not lower ICP and has many complications; current view: no steroid in TBI (จำ).
- Figure: Severe injury with presumed TBI algorithm, as steps —
  1. Neurologic examination → urgent cranial CT.
  2. Operative lesion → craniotomy with hematoma drainage → (consider ICP monitor). Non-operative → consider placement of ICP monitor.
  3. Measure ICP and calculate CPP.
     - ICP/CPP adequate or not measured → continue ICU care: ongoing neurologic monitoring, continued organ system support, early nutrition, infection prevention, PUD and DVT prophylaxis as appropriate.
     - ICP elevated or CPP depressed → consider HOB elevation, sedation/pain control, drainage of CSF, mild hyperventilation, mannitol, hypertonic saline → refractory intracranial hypertension? No → back to measuring ICP/CPP; Yes → consider decompressive craniectomy.

## Intracranial Pressure (ICP)
- Normal ICP = 10-15 mmHg, or not more than 20 cm H2O.
- ICP > 10 to 19 mmHg → be careful and monitor closely.
- ICP ≥ 20 mmHg → treat promptly:
  1. Manitol 1-2 mg./kg. IV bolus (as printed)
  2. Isotonic or hypertonic saline
  3. Hyperventilate, keep PaCO2 30-35 mmHg
  4. Keep CPP (cerebral perfusion pressure = mean BP minus ICP) > 60 mmHg — helps improve neurological outcome
- If all treatments fail to bring ICP down → consider decompressive craniectomy.

## Summary: what not to do in TBI management (สรุปสิ่งที่ไม่ควรใช้ในการ management TBI)
1. Test stiff neck or intubate before cervical spine injury is ruled out
2. Routine skull film, routine CT brain
3. Hypotonic saline or glucose
4. IV steroid
5. LMWH in the first 72 h
6. Prophylactic hyperventilation
7. Prophylactic hypothermia
8. Prophylactic barbiturate
9. Muscle relaxant to treat seizures — it only masks the seizure while the brain problem stays untreated; correct approach: anticonvulsant first, then add muscle relaxant temporarily only if needed
10. Prophylactic antibiotic

## Fracture Base of Skull
- Fracture of the lower skull beneath the brain; the fracture line often continues from the upper cranial vault.
- If the dura is torn → CSF leak.
- **Anatomy** — the skull base has 3 parts:
  1. Anterior skull base (anterior cranial fossa) — cribriform plate and orbital floor; fracture can leak CSF from the nose or down the throat. CN 1 passes here.
  2. Middle skull base (middle cranial fossa) — sella turcica holding the pituitary gland, with connections to the middle ear and nasopharynx; if the eardrum is perforated → CSF otorrhea. CN 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 pass here.
  3. Posterior skull base (posterior cranial fossa) — petrous bone and occiput. CN 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 pass here.
- Skull base fracture distribution (figure caption): 70% anterior fossa, 20% middle central skull base, 5% middle and posterior fossa.

## Symptoms and Signs of Base Skull Fracture
- **Anterior fossa fracture**
  1. Raccoon eye — bruising around the eyes like a panda, without direct eye injury
  2. Anosmia — loss of smell from olfactory nerve (CN 1) injury
  3. Nasal tip anesthesia
  4. Subconjunctival hemorrhage
  5. CSF rhinorrhea
  6. Carotid cavernous fistula
- **Middle fossa fracture**
  1. Hearing loss (หูอื้อ)
  2. Tympanic membrane intact → hemotympanum
  3. Tympanic membrane perforated → CSF otorrhea
  4. Battle sign — bruising behind the ear without direct ear injury
  5. Beware epidural hematoma
  6. Hypopituitarism
- **Posterior fossa fracture** — usually continues from a middle fossa fracture; CSF has no exit here unlike anterior and middle fossa, so external signs look like middle fossa fracture. May have CN7 palsy or vertigo from CN8 injury. Bleeding here is very dangerous — the clot presses directly on the brainstem.

## Imaging for Base Skull Fracture
- **Plain film** — diagnostic in only 20%: fracture line through an air sinus, air-fluid level in the sphenoid sinus, or pneumocephalus.
- **CT brain** — shows fracture and pneumocephalus more clearly.
- (Figure caption) Skull base fracture involving the foramen magnum is concerning for: associated spinal fracture, cord injury, or blunt cerebrovascular injury.

## Management of Base Skull Fracture
- Warning again: no NG tube or nasotracheal tube in base skull fracture — oral route only.
- No CSF leak → just observe; the fracture heals by itself.
- Antibiotics do not reduce infection such as meningitis and raise the chance of drug resistance; so even with a CSF leak, in theory antibiotics are not needed (จำ).
- Residents must know the 2 complications examiners like to ask:
  1. CSF fistula
  2. Carotid cavernous fistula (CCF)

## CSF Rhinorrhea
- CSF has high glucose and transferrin.
- Fluid from the nose must be told apart: CSF, blood or nasal mucus.
- CSF is clear, not sticky or viscous like blood or mucus. When it runs down the throat the patient complains of a sweet-salty taste, together with anosmia.
- **Test for CSF**
  1. Drop the fluid on a tissue paper: the water part spreads out fast, leaving a central ring of blood surrounded by a ring of fluid = **target sign** or **halo sign**
  2. Glucose level: CSF glucose about 44-100 mg.%, or about 0.5 × blood sugar
  3. If results are unclear or uncertain, send the fluid for beta transferrin, which is found in CSF

## CSF Fistula
- Usually appears about 2-3 days after head injury with base skull fracture; luckily most leaks close by themselves.
- Infection risk is higher with CSF rhinorrhea than CSF otorrhea.
- (Re-read the CSF rhinorrhea section to appreciate it — as printed.)
- **Management**
  1. **Conservative** — heals in up to 85% within 1 week; as printed, whoever operates straight away deserves a slap (โดนตบชัก)
     - 1.1 Bed rest, head up 30° until the CSF stops
     - 1.2 Avoid activities that raise intracranial pressure, e.g. coughing, blowing the nose, picking the nose, straining, or forceful bowel movements
     - 1.3 Drugs that reduce CSF production, e.g. acetazolamide
     - 1.4 Antibiotic not needed (จำไว้)
     - 1.5 Still leaking after 96 h → CSF drainage; how to do it — ask neuro
  2. **Surgery** — needed in about 15%. Indication: still leaking after 10-14 days; left alone it causes ascending infection such as meningitis. The operation depends on where the leak is. Endoscopic approach is now used, especially when the fracture runs through a sinus.

## Carotid Cavernous Fistula (CCF)
- Usually from a middle fossa base skull fracture tearing a branch of the carotid artery, which then connects with the cavernous sinus around the sella turcica.
- High cavernous sinus pressure → venous drainage flows back to periorbital tissue.
- **Symptoms** — from high cavernous sinus pressure:
  1. Pulsatile proptosis (bulging, pulsating eye)
  2. Sudden exophthalmos with eye pain
  3. Ophthalmoplegia or limited eye movement
  4. Chemosis — red, swollen sclera
  5. Episcleral venous dilatation — engorged vessels on the white of the eye
  6. Diplopia, with gradually blurring vision
  7. Epistaxis (printed "epitaxis")
  8. Ocular bruit — whooshing sound at the temple on that side, in time with cardiac contraction
- **Diagnosis**: angiogram.
- **Treatment**
  1. Usually observe about 1-4 weeks — in some the fistula closes by itself
  2. If it does not close, or vision starts to blur during observation, do not wait — the patient is close to blindness; best treatment is endovascular treatment by embolization

## Brain Death Criteria
1. GCS = 3
2. Nonreactive pupils
3. Absent brainstem reflexes, e.g.
   - 3.1 Oculocephalic reflex
   - 3.2 Corneal reflex
   - 3.3 Doll's eyes
   - 3.4 Gag reflex
   - Figure: Oculocephalic (doll's eye) — normal response: head turned to the left → eyes move to the right; head turned to the right → eyes move to the left.
   - Vestibulocular reflex (figure text): no eye movements within 3 minutes after irrigating each tympanic membrane (if intact) in turn with 50 ml ice water for 30 to 45 seconds, with the supine patient's head elevated 30 degrees.
4. No spontaneous ventilatory effort (printed "ventilatomy") on formal apnea testing
5. Exclude reversible conditions, e.g.
   - 5.1 Hypothermia
   - 5.2 Barbiturate coma

## Brain Death (WHO criteria)
- Cessation of spontaneous respiration
- Irreversible coma
- Absence of cephalic reflexes and dilated pupils
- Absence of any electrical activity of the brain (electrocerebral silence)
- Absence of brain blood flow — blood flow absent in the cranial vault on cerebral scintigraphy or angiography
