## Wound Healing Process
- Three types of wound healing (figure: first, second and third intention):
  1. **1ry healing** (healing by 1st intention) — we help the wound heal by suturing it, or covering with graft or flap. After suturing, the epidermis starts to seal in about 24 h.
  2. **2ry healing** (healing by 2nd intention) — the wound is left to heal spontaneously by granulation tissue, relying on wound contraction by specialized myofibroblasts (containing actin myofilaments) and on epithelization from epithelial cells migrating from the wound edge; the wound narrows until closed. Wounds healed this way usually leave much scar.
  3. **3ry healing** (healing by 3rd intention) — the wound first heals by 2ry healing; once clean with no dead tissue, the surgeon sutures it or places a graft to close it.

## Wound Healing Phase
- Three phases:
  1. **Inflammatory phase** — first 4 days: capillaries dilate, swelling, redness, heat; then inflammatory cells — PMN first, then macrophages — clear foreign material and tissue debris.
  2. **Proliferative phase** (fibroblastic phase) — about D5-D20: fibroblasts make collagen, increasing tensile strength; the wound starts to contract and the edges come together.
  3. **Remodeling phase** — from day 20 to 9 months: collagen synthesis, deposition and degradation with collagen cross-linking; new collagen is organized into bundles, increasing tensile strength. Scar remodels from immature scar (well vascularized, red, raised, dark) to mature scar (less vascular), so the colour fades and the raised tissue flattens.
- Figure: Timeline of cells and roles —
  - Inflammation (minutes-days): hemostasis — platelets: fibrin clot formation, vasoactive mediator release, cytokine and growth factor release; inflammation — mast cells ("master cells" as printed): platelet-activating mediator release, vasoactive and chemotactic mediator release; neutrophils and monocytes: chemotaxis, inflammation, killing and phagocytosing, wound debridement; macrophages: chemotaxis, inflammation, killing and phagocytosing, wound debridement, cytokines and growth factor release.
  - Proliferation (days-weeks): skin resurfacing — keratinocytes: reepithelialization; dermal restoration — endothelial cells: angiogenesis; fibroblasts: fibroplasia.
  - Remodeling (weeks-months): keratinocytes: epidermis maturation; myofibroblasts: wound contraction, apoptosis and scar maturation; endothelial cells: apoptosis and scar maturation.

## Wound Tensile Strength
- Strength during the inflammatory phase, from wounding to the first 3 weeks: in the first 5 days there is no strength; the wound holds only by blood vessels and clotting protein plus the sutures. Once collagen forms, strength rises: day 10 ≈ 10% wound strength; 6 weeks ≈ 50%. Strength rises fastest in the first 30 days, then slows, and plateaus after about 2 months.
- With time the wound never reaches 100% strength — at most 80-90%.

## Factors Affecting Wound Healing
- Many factors affect healing (Schwartz 2015):

| Systemic | Local |
|---|---|
| Age | Mechanical injury |
| Nutrition | Infection |
| Trauma | Edema |
| Metabolic diseases | Ischemia/necrotic tissue |
| Immunosuppression | Topical agents |
| Connective tissue disorders | Ionizing radiation |
| Smoking | Low oxygen tension |
| | Foreign bodies |

- Trace elements that aid healing: zinc, copper, magnesium. Vitamins that aid healing: A and C.
- Steroid inhibits collagen and epithelium formation, lowers tensile strength and wound contraction → slow healing. Studies show vitamin A can counteract the steroid effect.
- **Selection of wound dressing**
  1. High exudate (very wet wound) → absorbent dressing (foam)
  2. Low exudate (slightly wet) → hydrocolloid to keep moisture
  3. No exudate (very dry) → hydrogel ("hydrojel" as printed) to add moisture
  4. Infected wound → silver dressing

## Suture Material
- **Absorbable suture**
  1. **Plain catgut** — dissolves fast, loses strength in about 7 days; suits fast-healing wounds and areas where stitches are hard to remove, e.g. periorbital area or wounds in children (removing stitches from a child is a struggle).
  2. **Chromic catgut** — dissolves more slowly than plain, loses strength in 2 weeks; suits wounds in the mouth and nostril. Remember: chromic is not used for facial skin wounds because it dissolves slowly and causes a lot of tissue reaction.
  3. **Polyglycolic and polyglactin (vicryl)** — lasts more than 2 months, loses 70% strength at 3 weeks; little tissue reaction. Monofilament forms exist, e.g. polydioxanone (PDS), which loses 50% strength at 4 weeks.
  4. **Non-absorbable suture**, e.g. nylon, prolene — for skin closure, tendon repair ("tenden" as printed), vessels.

## Skin Incision
- A good incision has low tension:
  1. Along skin lines or Langer lines
  2. Perpendicular to muscle contraction
- **Preventing dog-ear deformity** — when excising a skin nodule such as a mole, wound length (long axis) should be 4 times the width (short axis).

## Wound Closure
- **Basic wound closure**
  1. Avoid closing under much tension; for a tight wound, undermine the skin, or use a graft or flap.
  2. Debride devitalized tissue or heavily contaminated wounds before closing — cut until the edges bleed or the muscle contracts.
  3. When unsure whether skin should be debrided or kept: in theory, IV fluorescein helps assess skin viability accurately (as printed, a tip to tick in the exam).
  4. To avoid stitch marks, remove sutures before 5 days ("stich mark" as printed).
- **Z-plasty** — moves tissue from one place to another using a Z-shaped design: skin flaps of 2 triangles, made of a central member and two limbs; the angles of the Z need not be equal.
- Figure: Z-plasty in 3 stages — incisions make triangular flaps; flaps moved into new positions; flaps interlocked and wound closed.
- **Purpose and benefit of Z-plasty** — the central limb of the Z is lengthened by transposing the triangular skin flaps; the wound direction changes so the wound becomes longer (not shorter). This change of direction reduces wound tension and corrects wound contracture, so Z-plasty makes the scar smaller.
- **W-plasty** — W-shaped design to correct a long depressed scar lying across skin lines, without increasing scar length.
- **Trap door scar** — seen with avulsion flaps where only the skin was sutured and the deeper layer was not, giving a bulging, ugly scar. Fix: reopen the wound and suture the subcutaneous layer again.

## Excessive Scaring
- (Heading as printed.) Scar follows the healing process and should shrink during remodeling; excess collagen formation and deposition → raised scar.
- **Excessive scarring** — 2 types: hypertrophic scar and keloid scar.
- **Hypertrophic scar** — raised scar confined within the original wound edge; can regress.
  - Treatment: several options, e.g. silicone sheet or pressure garment over the scar, laser, or excision of the raised scar. Intra-lesional steroid ("teroid" as printed) injection is second-line treatment.
- **Keloid scar** — raised scar invading beyond the original wound edge; cannot regress.
  - Treatment: intra-lesional steroid injection is first-line treatment; excision does not work well because recurrence is high.

## Skin Graft
- Epidermis and dermis taken from a donor site and placed on a wound; relies on blood supply from the recipient site.
- First 24-48 hr: graft is fed by passive diffusion of nutrients from the recipient bed.
- After 48 hr: capillary buds from the wound connect with graft capillaries.
- About 5-7 days: neovascularization — arterial circulation matures first, then venous circulation.
- **Graft takes easily if:**
  1. Well vascularized recipient bed
  2. Wound bacteria < 100,000 per gm tissue
  3. Graft has good contact ("contract" as printed) with the bed
  4. Graft near a joint → splint both proximal and distal joints
- Donor site wounds: close them and do not open — unless there are signs of infection, e.g. unexplained fever, local pain & erythema, purulent discharge; then open the donor dressing and look. If only fluid collection under the dressing, do not take the dressing off yet (จำ).
- **Autograft** — graft from oneself.
- **Allograft** — graft from another person (same species).
- **Xenograft** — graft from an animal, e.g. pig skin.
- **Meshed graft** — graft perforated with many small holes to cover a large wound; usually when donor skin is scarce, e.g. severe burn. Disadvantage: heavy scarring.
- **Donor site** — commonly abdomen, buttock or thigh; dress with occlusive dressing or op-site. A partial-thickness donor site heals by re-epithelialization from skin appendages (keratocytes of hair follicles) within 2 weeks, and can be re-harvested. A full-thickness harvest removes all skin appendages, so the donor site cannot heal by itself and must be closed by suturing or a meshed graft.
- **Recipient bed** — best is bare muscle (good blood supply). Do not place a graft on poorly vascularized or avascular tissue: cartilage, bone, fascia, tendon, nerve, and especially chronic wounds from irradiated tissue.
- **Graft taking**
  1. First 24 h: donor skin sticks to the wound by fibrin adhesion.
  2. Then fibrous tissue and existing wound capillaries grow into the graft = **inosculation**.
  3. Finally new vessels form (neovascularization) to feed the graft further.
- **Techniques that promote graft taking** ("premote" as printed)
  1. Bolster dressing or bolus tie-over dressing
  2. Splint the extremity so it does not move much
  3. Careful preparation of the recipient bed
  4. Early inspection of the skin graft — hematoma under the graft is the commonest cause of graft failure; hematoma or seroma must be removed promptly or the graft will not take
  5. Never use heat — neither a lamp nor a hair dryer — heat dries the wound and kills the graft
- **Skin graft types**
  1. Split thickness skin graft (partial thickness)
  2. Full thickness skin graft
- **Split thickness** (partial thickness) — epidermis plus part of the dermis; thin graft, takes easily.
- **Full thickness** — epidermis plus the whole dermis; takes less easily than split thickness, but cosmetic result, colour and texture are better — suits facial wounds, weight-bearing areas, or correction of contracture better than split thickness.
- **Composite graft** — graft containing tissue other than skin, e.g. subcutaneous layer; thicker, so takes poorly.

| | Full thickness | Partial thickness |
|---|---|---|
| 1ry contracture | More | Less |
| 2ry contracture | Less | More |
| Facial wound | Yes | No |
| Maintain normal pigmentation | Yes | No |
| Vascular bed status | Better | Fair (takes easily) |
| Sensory return | Slow | Faster |

- **Primary contracture**
  1. The graft shrinks immediately after harvest from the donor site, thought to be recoil of elastic fibres in the dermis.
  2. Thicker graft (full thickness) → more 1ry contracture, up to 40%; partial thickness only 10% (จำ).
- **Secondary contracture** — contractile myofibroblasts in the bed of a granulating wound contract; a thin partial-thickness graft placed there gets pulled and shrinks.
- Facial wounds: full thickness from the postauricular area (จำ); another usable site is the supraclavicular area.
- Hand and arm wounds: full thickness from the hip flexion crease, inguinal region; small hand wounds may take a graft from the hypothenar ("hypothena" as printed) area.
- Deep pigmentation is a problem with partial thickness, especially 6-9 months after surgery; avoid sunlight during this period.
- Sensation starts returning 10 wk after grafting, up to 2 years.

## Flap
- Tissue used to cover a defect, with its own blood supply, not relying on the wound bed as a skin graft does.
- **Local flap** — flap from tissue next to the defect; e.g. for a pressure sore at the sacrum, nearby tissue is made into a rotating flap to close the wound.
- **Regional flap** — flap adjacent to the wound defect.
- **Distant flap** — flap away from the wound defect.
- **Free flap** — flap far from the defect; its vessels must be anastomosed to vessels around the wound; often used for open tibial fracture wounds.
- Problem with free flap cover: flap necrosis, mostly from venous thrombosis rather than arterial thrombosis.
- **Venous thrombosis of a free flap** — before necrosis the flap turns dark, bruised purple-red, and the flap is still warm.
- **Arterial thrombosis of a free flap** — flap is pale and cold.

## TRAM Flap
- The most-examined flap; follow the trend a bit, especially for breast cancer.
- TRAM flap does not increase local recurrence of breast cancer, and a prosthesis is not needed because the flap itself gives fullness (จำ).
- **Pedicle TRAM flap** — popular for breast reconstruction; blood supply from the superior epigastric artery.
- **Free TRAM flap** — used when a pedicle flap is not possible for any reason; blood supply from the deep inferior epigastric artery, not the superior epigastric artery as in the pedicle flap.
- Figure: TRAM flap — lower abdominal skin island over the rectus divided into zones (from the pedicle side across: III, I over the pedicled rectus, then II, IV across the midline); superior epigastric artery and vein above, deep inferior epigastric artery and vein below.
- If a TRAM flap cannot be used, latissimus dorsi can be used instead.
- **Contraindications of TRAM flap** (จำ)
  1. Too thin or too fat (patient should have normal BMI)
  2. Smoking (more than 20 packs per year, as printed)
  3. Autoimmune disease, e.g. scleroderma, Raynaud ("Raynoud" as printed)
  4. Drug addicts
  5. Psychiatric/nervous disorder (โรคประสาท)
  6. Many comorbidities — cannot tolerate long surgery
  7. Abdominal wall scar that has disrupted blood supply from the superior epigastric artery, e.g. a Chevron incision
  8. Previous abdominoplasty (no fat left, the flap is not full)
  9. Patient with a small, flat abdomen (as printed)
- **Transverse Rectus Abdominis Muscle Flap Reconstruction** (table):

| Category | Items |
|---|---|
| Indications | Breasts of all sizes; breast ptosis |
| Relative contraindications | Smoking; abdominal liposuction; previous abdominal surgery; pulmonary disease; obesity |
| Contraindications | Previous abdominoplasty; patient unable to tolerate a 4- to 6-week recovery period; patient unable to tolerate a longer procedure |

## Pressure Sores
- Caused by continuous pressure above capillary pressure; pressure 40-80 mmHg for more than 4 h starts microvascular change, and more than 8 h causes tissue necrosis.
- Common sites: bony prominences, e.g. sacrum, buttock, heel.

## European Pressure Sore Classification

| Grade | Description (table) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Non-blanchable erythema (redness) of intact skin; discolouration, warmth, oedema, induration or hardness may also be indicators, particularly in darker skin |
| 2 | Partial thickness skin loss involving epidermis, dermis, or both; superficial ulcer presenting as an abrasion or blister |
| 3 | Full thickness skin loss with damage to or necrosis of subcutaneous tissue that may extend down to, but not through, underlying fascia |
| 4 | Extensive destruction, tissue necrosis, or damage to muscle, bone, or supporting structures, with or without full thickness skin loss |

- Stage and treatment:
  - **Stage 1** — only non-blanching erythema ("non-branching" as printed), no wound yet. Rx: turn the patient often, skin care, pressure-distributing bed.
  - **Stage 2** — partial thickness skin loss of epidermis and dermis only; shallow wound, may have a bleb. Rx: as stage 1 plus moist dressing; let it heal by itself.
  - **Stage 3** — wound down to fat but not to fascia. Rx: as stage 2 plus debridement; moist dressing or VAC.
  - **Stage 4** — wound down to muscle or to bone or joint. Rx: as stage 3, then cover with a flap.

## Vacuum Assisted Wound Closure (VAC)
- A porous sponge and occlusive dressing are placed on the wound, then negative pressure is applied to the wound surface. VAC speeds granulation, increases blood flow to the wound and improves wound perfusion → faster healing.
- (Figure: sponge on wound under adherent plastic sheet, egress tube to a reservoir in a programmable pump.)
- Usable for both acute and chronic wounds.
- Post-op open heart wounds with mediastinitis, once debrided clean, can use VAC as a bridge to definitive surgery before flap cover.
- Wounds for VAC must meet these:
  1. No dead tissue, no osteomyelitis
  2. Do not place VAC on wounds with large exposed vessels
  3. Do not place VAC on a skin graft (as printed)

## Congenital Anomalies
- Two conditions surgery residents should know in outline: cleft lip and cleft palate.

## Cleft Lip
- Unilateral or bilateral.
- About 1:700 births. If a parent has a cleft, the child's chance of cleft lip is 4%.
- Problems of cleft lip: feeding and cosmetic.
- **Timing of repair**
  1. Rule of 10: Hb > 10 gm/100 ml, age > 10 wk, BW > 10 lb.
  2. Flap reconstruction with Abbe or Estlander flap.

## Cleft Palate
- About 1:700 births.
- Increased incidence of middle ear infection, possibly causing hearing loss in the child.
- Problems of cleft palate: speech and swallowing.
- **Timing of repair**
  1. Best age 9 months, so speech problems do not develop.
  2. Not later than 2 years; but closing too early is also bad because of midface retrusion.

## Burn Injury
(Inserted article by พรพรหม เมืองแมน, different layout with its own printed page numbers 1071 onward; PDF pages 2110-2125; in Thai.)
- Burns are common in daily life; luckily most are not severe enough to kill. Initial care of burn patients now follows the same principles as general trauma, mainly ATLS.
- This article covers what is needed for hot-water and fire burns: systematic initial planning, assessing burn depth (degree of burn wound) and burn size as % of body surface area (%TBSA burn), initial wound care, burn wound treatment, fluid resuscitation, and indications for referral to a burn unit — essential knowledge for surgeons and doctors for initial burn care.
- Priority in burn care follows ATLS: Airway, Breathing, Circulation (ABC). Airway and breathing problems arise in patients with smoke inhalation injury. Circulation problems arise in large-area burns, which need fluid resuscitation early, otherwise death from shock or acute renal failure may follow.

### Smoke Inhalation Injury (ภาวะการสำลักเขม่าควันหลังบาดเจ็บไฟไหม้)
- Burn patients with inhalation injury (smoke inhalation) have markedly higher mortality. Treatment is not hard if diagnosed early; it is mainly supportive oxygenation and ventilation, usually with good results.
- Patients burned in a closed space and arriving unconscious may have high carboxyhemoglobin: carbon monoxide (CO) binds hemoglobin up to 200 times more than oxygen. CO half-life drops to about 1 h if the patient breathes 100% oxygen. So patients burned in a closed space need closer watch for this.
- **Thermal airway injury** mostly affects the upper airway, from inhaling hot gases. Suspect upper airway injury in patients burned by explosion (propane, natural gas, gasoline, etc.), with burns of the face or upper torso, or loss of consciousness at the scene. Exam is also important: history of burn in a closed space plus oropharyngeal edema, hoarseness and stridor → intubate. In the early phase, patients with upper airway obstruction who are intubated may not all need a ventilator; some need only CPAP to prevent atelectasis.
- **Lower airway** injury usually shows not immediately but about 2-3 days postburn; mostly from inhaling small (non-chemical, as printed "ไม่ว่า chemical particles") particles from burning furniture, leather, plastics, and smoke from burning wood etc. These are inhaled into the lower respiratory tract → mucosal damage along the airway, cytokine release, increased vascular permeability and fluid leakage → adult respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). So a patient who was fortunate enough not to need intubation for upper airway problems in the first 2-3 days may later show lower airway problems; with falling oxygen saturation or ARDS, intubate early to support breathing.
- Doctors must watch and monitor patients suspected of inhalation injury closely and treat promptly; most deaths are from late diagnosis and late respiratory support or intubation.
- Definite diagnosis of smoke inhalation injury is bronchoscopy, but it is not often done: if history and exam fit, treatment is usually started at once. Bronchoscopy is mostly done when heavy soot is suspected, with bronchoalveolar lavage at the same time to wash it out.

### Assessment of Burn Wound (การประเมินบาดแผลไฟไหม้)
1. **Degree of burn wound** — usually 3 main degrees by depth:
   - 1.1 **First degree burn** — epidermis only; red wound, no blister; after a while the outer skin peels off. Common in sunburn or hot-water scald without skin loss. Treatment is symptomatic: analgesia and skin lotion or white petrolatum, e.g. bacitracin ointment, to moisturize. No dressing needed; heals without scar.
   - 1.2 **Second degree burn (partial thickness burn)** — epidermis and part of the dermis; severity depends on dermal depth. Two types:
     - **Superficial second degree burn** — many skin appendages remain (hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands); damage usually only in the upper 1/3 of the dermis. Heals fairly fast by itself; clear blisters, pink skin underneath when peeled; burning pain; heals within 2-3 weeks, usually without scar. Treatment may use topical agents of many kinds depending on indication and the doctor's preference; the most used is 1% silver sulfadiazine. Nowadays another option is covering with commercially available dressing sheets; advantage: no daily dressing changes, less pain.
     - **Deep second degree burn** — most skin appendages destroyed; may heal by itself but slowly; wound is yellow-white, not very painful because most of the true skin is destroyed. Usually takes more than 2-3 weeks and scar formation is quite likely. Usually treated first with silver sulfadiazine; if not improving or not healed within 2 weeks, proceed to surgery: early burn wound excision and skin graft.
   - 1.3 **Third degree burn (full thickness burn)** — epidermis, dermis and all skin appendages destroyed; will not heal by itself; needs early burn wound excision and skin graft.

### Extent of Burn (การประเมินขนาดของบาดแผลไฟไหม้)
- Burn size is calculated as % of total body surface area (TBSA). The most used method is **"Wallace's Rule of Nines"** — convenient, fast, easy to remember and use.
- Whole body surface = 100%: face (head) = 9%, front or back of trunk 18% each, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, perineum = 1%.
- For scattered burns, a simple estimate: the patient's palm with closed fingers, measured from fingertips to wrist, = 1% TBSA.

### Fluid Resuscitation in Burn (การให้สารน้ำในผู้ป่วยไฟไหม้น้ำร้อนลวก)
- The widely used, easy-to-remember formula is the **Parkland formula**. Burns of second degree or more over > 20% TBSA should get fluid by the Parkland formula.
- First 24 h: crystalloid, usually Ringer lactate solution 4 ml/kg/%TBSA burn; give half of the calculated volume in the first 8 h and the remaining half over the next 16 h.
- What matters is the starting fluid volume in the first hours; then adjust resuscitation mainly by urine output. The Parkland number is only a guideline. Target urine output about 0.5-1 ml/kg/hr, or about 30-60 ml/hr; if output is above or below this, adjust the rate.
- Burns > 40% TBSA: consider central venous catheterization early to ensure adequate, timely fluid. Avoid placing venous lines through burned skin — raises wound infection rate.

### Initial Management of Burn Wound (การดูแลบาดแผลเบื้องต้น)
- First, cool the burn as soon as possible and remove heat sources from the burned area, e.g. remove burned clothing — limits tissue damage. Washing with clean water also removes heat from the wound. Cooling helps most if the patient reaches hospital within 30 min of injury: less swelling and pain.
- Cleaning: remove dirt and wash with room-temperature saline, possibly with mild soap; then debridement or removal of foreign material (chemical, tar, debris); then topical treatment or dressings. Change topical treatments 1-2 times a day. Prophylactic antibiotic is not needed.
- **Chemical injury** — wash with clean water, but if it is a dry powder, brush it off first, then wash with clean water for at least 30 min or until irritation improves.
- Second degree burns may have blisters. Blister treatment is somewhat controversial; generally remove blisters if large, or over joints causing functional impairment, because large unremoved blisters make topical treatment messy and the wound looks dirty (photos: large blisters left in place get very dirty with ointment; removing them makes cleaning and burn area assessment more accurate). Small blisters need not be removed and can act as a biological dressing.

### Escharotomy and Fasciotomy (การทำหัตถการ Escharotomy และ Fasciotomy)
- Many surgeons confuse these, but their indications differ.
- **Escharotomy** — for deep circumferential burns around an arm, leg or chest causing vascular compromise or ventilatory problems, usually in flame burns. The incision goes only through to the superficial layer of the dermis; no anesthesia needed; can be done in the ER.
- **Fasciotomy** — usually for deep burns with myoglobinuria not responding to medical treatment; the incision goes down to muscle to relieve compartment syndrome and ongoing rhabdomyolysis. Usually needed in deep flame burns or high voltage electrical injury with myoglobinuria not responding to medical treatment; needs anesthesia before incision.

### Indications for Referral to a Burn Unit (ข้อบ่งชี้ของการส่งต่อผู้ป่วยไปรับการรักษาในหอผู้ป่วยบาดเจ็บไฟไหม้)
- Patients with the following should be treated in a hospital with a burn specialist surgeon, or referred to a burn unit:
  - Second and third degree burn > 20% TBSA, age 10-50 years
  - Second and third degree burn > 10% TBSA, age < 10 or > 50 years
  - Full thickness burn > 5% TBSA, any age
  - Burns of face, hands, feet, eyes, ears, perineum — affect cosmetic and functional outcome
  - High-voltage electrical injury, including lightning injury
  - Inhalation injury or associated trauma
  - Chemical burns
  - Burn with co-morbid conditions (e.g. diabetes mellitus, COPD, cardiac disease)

### Burn Wound
- Shallow second degree burn = **superficial partial thickness burn**: heals within 1-2 weeks, usually without scar; but if poorly cared for may convert to deep partial thickness or full thickness. Treatment: clean, then dress with topical antibiotic — commonly 1% silver sulfadiazine — or various dressings that control pain and promote healing. Popular now: Acticoat, Urgotul, Aquacel Ag, Biobrane, Opsite, Mepitel, etc. (dressing types listed in tables 1-2).
- Deep second degree burn = **deep partial thickness burn**: may heal by itself but slowly, possibly more than 3 weeks; it can be managed like a superficial partial thickness burn, but healing takes longer with more morbidity, e.g. wound infection, scar formation. So now, if conservative treatment of a deep partial thickness burn does not heal it within 2 weeks, early burn wound excision and skin coverage is preferred, as in full thickness burns, because this reduces wound infection and scar formation and shortens hospital stay.
- If the patient has enough normal skin for grafting, use autograft first. In some cases, e.g. burns > 50% TBSA, remaining normal skin may not be enough, so artificial skin may be needed first; the operative steps are more complex and not detailed in this article.

### Debridement of Dead Tissue (การตัดเนื้อตายออกจากบาดแผล)
- Excising deeper than needed loses much good tissue, causing bleeding or contracted wound healing with more scar formation. Experience and knowing when you have reached good tissue is a real challenge for the surgeon.
- Good tissue usually shows punctuate bleeding. Good dermis is white and glossy; good fat is shiny yellow. Orange-tinged fat is usually starting to die and must be removed completely, for the skin graft on the burn to succeed.

### Summary (สรุป)
- Most important: a correct system of initial care for burn patients following Advanced Trauma Life Support. The main problem that quickly makes severely burned patients die early is inadequate fluid → shock.
- Doctors also need basic knowledge of assessing burn severity — type, depth, and % TBSA calculation — and of using topical antibiotics and wound dressings, and when to refer to a burn unit, to reduce disability and increase survival.
