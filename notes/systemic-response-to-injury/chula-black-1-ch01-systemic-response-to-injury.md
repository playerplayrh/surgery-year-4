# Systemic response to injury (การตอบสนองของร่างกายต่อการบาดเจ็บ)

## Key points

**Big picture (p.46 [32])**
- Injury (infection, trauma, surgery, inflammation) triggers a local and systemic response to keep
  homeostasis. A balanced response → patient survives; an excessive one → systemic inflammation,
  remote organ damage, multiple organ failure. Up to 30% in severely injured patients.
- Four systems respond: CNS, hormonal, metabolic, immunologic.

**CNS (pp.46-47)**
- Inputs: pain fibres, cytokines via sensory vagus, baroreceptor / chemoreceptor / thermoreceptor
  (BP, volume, electrolytes, temperature).
- Cytokines → fatigue, sleepiness, anorexia, fever.
- Output via HPA axis and ANS: sympathetic ↑ BP and HR (catecholamines); parasympathetic (motor
  vagus, acetylcholine) keeps inflammation from getting too severe ("inflammatory reflex", fig.1).

**Hormones (pp.47-51)**
- HPA axis after injury (fig.2): ↑ cortisol, ↑ aldosterone, ↓ sex hormones; ↓ FSH/LH, ↑ GH,
  ↓ somatostatin, ↑ endogenous opioids, ↓ TSH/T3/T4, ↑ ADH.

| Hormone | Source | Role in stress |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cortisol | Zona fasciculata | ↑ gluconeogenesis and glucose, anti-inflammatory, lets vessels constrict to catecholamines |
| ADH (vasopressin) | Hypothalamus → posterior pituitary | Vasoconstriction, water reabsorption at collecting tubule; stimulated by volume depletion |
| GH | Anterior pituitary | Protein synthesis, insulin resistance, fat mobilisation (via IGF from liver); ↑ phagocytosis, T-cell proliferation. ↑ by GHRH and ghrelin, ↓ by somatostatin |
| Catecholamines | Adrenal medulla (from tyrosine) | NE: α1 vasoconstriction. EPI: α + β, vasoconstriction + inotropy. Up 3-4× in stress; ↑ glucose (gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, lipolysis), ↓ inflammation |
| Glucagon | Pancreatic α cell | ↑ in stress; catabolic, counter-regulates insulin |
| Insulin | Pancreatic β cell | Anabolic; ↓ in stress |
| Aldosterone | Zona glomerulosa | Salt and water retention at distal convoluted tubule; driven mainly by RAAS (more than ACTH) |

- ADH excess = SIADH → hyponatremia. ADH lack (hypothalamic failure, e.g. severe head injury) →
  diabetes insipidus → hypernatremia.

**Adrenal shock (pp.50-51)**
- Adrenal insufficiency: too little cortisol and aldosterone to meet stress (trauma, surgery,
  infection). Shock from water/sodium loss (no aldosterone) and low vascular tone (no cortisol).
- Suspect it when shock has no clear cause and does not respond to fluid or inotropes.
- Serum cortisol cannot diagnose it accurately. Unstable + suspected → send cortisol and give
  hydrocortisone at once without waiting. Cortisol < 15 µg/dL in a shocked critically ill patient
  may be taken as diagnostic. Whether to continue steroid depends on suspicion and response, not
  the level alone.
- Treatment: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV q 8 h (glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid effect); fluid,
  preferably 5% D/NSS (water, sodium, hypoglycemia), but load with NSS when treating hypotension
  fast; find and treat the precipitating factor (trauma, bleeding, infection).

**Metabolism: phases (pp.51-52, fig.4)**
- Ebb phase: right after injury/shock, ↓ energy expenditure. Inadequate resuscitation → death;
  adequate → flow phase.
- Flow phase: ↑ energy expenditure.
  - Catabolic (hours-days): ↑ catecholamine, cortisol, glucagon.
  - Anabolic (days-weeks): ↑ insulin. Early = corticoid withdrawal, diuresis, appetite returns;
    late = fat and muscle restored, positive nitrogen balance.

**Fasting vs injury (pp.52-54, fig.5)**

| | Fasting | Injury |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy need | 22-25 kcal/kg/day basal | Higher, by severity; maximum in burn and sepsis |
| Liver glycogen | Used up within 24 h | Used up within a few hours |
| Main fuel | Fat > 40%; gluconeogenesis from alanine (glucose-alanine cycle), lactate (Cori cycle), glycerol; fatty acids used directly (75%) or made into ketones (25%) | Fat 50-80%; gluconeogenesis from lipolysis and protein breakdown |
| Does IV glucose spare protein? | Yes: 400 kcal/day (e.g. 5% D 2 L/day) cuts protein breakdown up to 50% | No, not in the early period |

- Long fasting (> 5-7 days): less protein breakdown, more lipolysis; brain adapts to ketones
  (ketoadaptation); renal cortex also does gluconeogenesis.
- Full (100%) protein sparing needs about the total daily calorie requirement, i.e. TPN. Routine
  TPN for every NPO patient is not practical and may be harmful.
- Fig.5 %REE peaks (approximate, read off the graph): major burns ~205%, sepsis/peritonitis ~165%,
  skeletal trauma ~133%, elective surgery ~118%; starvation falls to ~70%.

**Nutrition support (pp.54-63)**
- Goal: prevent or reverse catabolism. Indications: malnutrition, or expected inadequate intake
  > 7 days. Start as soon as the patient is **hemodynamically stable** with no contraindication.
- **Enteral (EN) is preferred**: keeps gut integrity and gut immunity (less bacterial
  translocation), protects liver, may stimulate vagal anti-inflammatory pathways, cheaper, fewer
  complications than PN. Counted as effective infection control in ICU (no translocation, no
  catheter sepsis).
- NG feeding: easy, bolus possible; short-term only. > 30 days → percutaneous (e.g. gastrostomy).
- Post-pyloric / jejunostomy feeding: 25% less aspiration than NG when stomach works; harder to
  place, must be slow continuous drip. Use case by case, e.g. impaired gastric emptying, duodenal
  fistula.
- Feeding regimen: gastric bolus q 6-8 h; GRV > 200-250 mL at 4-6 h → hold feed ± change route
  (post-pyloric or PN); infuse 12-16 h then rest 8-12 h (less malabsorption, diarrhea); small
  bowel feeds start low rate and concentration then advance; head of bed 30-40°.
- PN: when support is needed and EN is contraindicated or inadequate (EN cannot meet > 60% of
  energy and protein after 1 week). Peripheral = partial PN, < 650 mOsm/L (avoid
  thrombophlebitis). Central = TPN, hypertonic (e.g. > 12.5% dextrose, 700 mOsm/L).

**Calories and macronutrients (pp.57-61)**
- BEE: Harris-Benedict, or quick BEE = 25 × kg. Stress multipliers: fever × 1.1 per 1 °C; mild
  × 1.2; moderate × 1.4; severe × 1.6; severe burn × 2. REE = 1.2 × BEE ≈ 30 kcal/kg.
- Practical: **25-30 kcal/kg (ideal body weight)/day**, then adjust for stress and nutrition
  status.
- Count only non-protein calories (NPC): carbohydrate 50-70%, lipid 30-50%.
- Carbohydrate 4 kcal/g (IV dextrose monohydrate 3.4 kcal/g). Cut its share in hyperglycemia or
  COPD with CO2 retention.
- Lipid 9 kcal/g; also prevents essential fatty acid deficiency (linoleic ω-6, linolenic ω-3) →
  scaly dermatitis, cardiac dysfunction, infection. 10% lipid 1.1 kcal/mL, 20% lipid 2 kcal/mL;
  isotonic, so can go peripheral. Fish oil ω-3 (e.g. SMOF) is anti-inflammatory and lowered
  mortality and ventilator use in critical illness; soybean ω-6 adds to inflammation.
- More lipid (e.g. 50% NPC) helps diabetics, fluid-restricted patients, and COPD. RQ: lipid 0.7,
  protein 0.8, glucose 1.0.
- Protein is not a calorie source; give it with enough NPC so it is not burnt.
  Requirements: normal 0.8-1 g/kg; moderate stress 1-1.5; severe stress (burn, sepsis) 1.5-2;
  renal failure 0.8 (more essential amino acids).
- N balance = protein intake (g)/6.25 − (UUN + 4); goal +4 to +6 g/day; monitor once in anabolic
  phase.
- BCAA for hepatic encephalopathy and immunonutrients (arginine, glutamine): no clear evidence of
  lower mortality.

**Immunology (pp.66-77)**
- DAMPs from injured cells (or pathogens, e.g. endotoxin) start the response. Many DAMPs share
  receptors with LPS (toll-like receptors), so severe sterile injury can look like sepsis.
- SIRS (pro-inflammatory) and CARS (counter-regulatory anti-inflammatory) run together; too much
  of either → MOF. Fig.7 outcomes: early MOF death, recovery, late MOF death, or PICS
  (persistent inflammation, immunosuppression, catabolism) → long-term care, poor quality of life,
  indolent death.
- Cell stress: ROS overwhelm scavengers in severe injury or ischemia-reperfusion; HSPs act as
  DAMPs once released; autophagy rises in stress; apoptosis (extrinsic TNF-α/Fas; intrinsic
  cytochrome C → caspase) controls inflammatory cells without inflammation, unlike necrosis;
  necroptosis in sepsis and viral infection.
- Cytokines: pro-inflammatory TNF, IL-1, 2, 8, 12, 15, 17, 18, IFN; anti-inflammatory IL-4, 10,
  11, 13; dual IL-6 and TGF-β.
- Eicosanoids: ω-6 (arachidonic acid) mostly pro-inflammatory; ω-3 (EPA, DHA) anti-inflammatory.
  Corticosteroids act at phospholipase A2. Normal state COX-1 → PGI2 (kidney, GI protection);
  inflammation COX-2 → PGE2 etc.
- Other mediators: complement (alternative pathway 80-90% of activation), kallikrein-kinin
  (bradykinin), serotonin (vasoconstrictor, from platelets), histamine (H1-H4), chemokines.
- Cells: severe injury suppresses TH1 (pro-inflammatory, cell-mediated) and shifts to TH2
  (anti-inflammatory, humoral) (fig.10). Neutrophils arrive first (half-life 4-10 h, longer in
  inflammation): phagocytosis, respiratory burst, granules, NETs. Macrophage M1 inflammatory, M2
  healing and anti-inflammatory.
- Endothelium in sepsis: loses anticoagulant surface → microthrombosis and organ injury. NO
  (vasodilator, from arginine) ↑ in septic shock, but NO synthase inhibitors did not cut organ
  dysfunction or mortality. PGI2 falls in systemic inflammation; giving it in sepsis improved
  oxygenation, cardiac index, splanchnic flow and O2 delivery. Endothelin = most potent
  vasoconstrictor. PAF ↑ in sepsis. ANF/BNP ↑ in cardiac disorder and sepsis.

**Multiple organ failure (pp.77-84)**
- Older definitions: SIRS = 2 of 4 (T > 38 or < 36 °C; HR ≥ 90; RR ≥ 20 or PaCO2 ≤ 32 or on
  ventilator; WBC ≥ 12,000 or ≤ 4,000 or ≥ 10% bands). Sepsis = SIRS from infection. Severe
  sepsis = + ≥ 1 organ dysfunction. Septic shock = severe sepsis + hypotension not responding to
  volume.
- **Sepsis-3 (2016, table 4):** sepsis = life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated
  host response to infection; organ dysfunction = acute rise in SOFA ≥ 2 (baseline 0 if no known
  disease; ~10% mortality). **qSOFA ≥ 2**: altered mentation, SBP ≤ 100, RR ≥ 22. **Septic
  shock**: vasopressors needed for MAP ≥ 65 and lactate > 2 mmol/L despite adequate volume;
  mortality > 40%.
- MODS = dysfunction of > 1 system; MOF = failure of > 1 system: CNS (septic encephalopathy,
  critical-illness polyneuropathy/myopathy), lung (ARDS), CVS (shock), GI (ileus, translocation,
  bleeding, malabsorption), liver, kidney (ATN), coagulation (DIC).
- Mechanisms: inflammatory model (SIRS/CARS imbalance; activated neutrophils release ROM into
  tissue, e.g. lung); circulatory failure (septic shock = hypovolemic from capillary leak +
  cardiogenic from septic cardiomyopathy + vasodilatory; microcirculatory leak and thrombosis);
  ischemia-reperfusion (K, phosphate, uric acid, myoglobin, bacteria, ROM washed out); gut
  hypothesis (ischemic mucosa → bacteria to liver, Kupffer cells → severe SIRS, unexplained
  sepsis); two-hit (PMN priming → second hit causes late MOF, often with liver failure).
- Prognosis: organs failed 1 / 2 / 3 / ≥ 4 → mortality 25 / 50 / 75 / ~100%. APACHE II (0-71;
  10 → 12%, 20 → 40%, 30 → 71%). SOFA (6 systems, 0-24; first-24-h score predicts mortality:
  0-1 → 0%, 2-3 → 7%, 4-5 → 20%, 6-7 → 22%, 8-9 → 33%, 10-11 → 50%, > 11 → 95%; daily score
  tracks the course). Full SOFA table: extraction p.82.
- Management: treat the cause (bleeding, infection) fast; adequate resuscitation; organ support;
  nutrition; targeted therapy, where activated protein C (bleeding risk, no proven benefit) and
  steroid in severe sepsis (no clear benefit) are not routine. Give steroid only for suspected
  adrenal insufficiency or shock not responding to fluid, inotropes and vasopressors.

## Indications / contraindications (or when to use / avoid)

- **Nutrition support**: indicated for malnutrition or expected inadequate intake > 7 days; start
  once hemodynamically stable.
- **EN contraindications**: hemodynamic instability (EN can cause gut ischemia); ileus or
  obstruction; high-output enterocutaneous fistula (early phase); active GI bleeding; peritonitis
  (e.g. intestinal ischemia, early severe acute pancreatitis).
- **PN**: indicated when EN is contraindicated or meets < 60% of needs after 1 week.
  Contraindicated in hemodynamic instability (complicates resuscitation).
- **IV lipid contraindications**: hyperlipidemia / abnormal lipid metabolism (lower the dose when
  TG > 300-400 mg/dL, stop when > 1,000); acute pancreatitis with or caused by hyperlipidemia;
  severe egg allergy. Use with caution in liver impairment, bleeding disorder, severe sepsis,
  severe lung disease, lipid nephrosis.
- **Hydrocortisone**: unstable and suspected adrenal insufficiency, treat before results; steroid
  in sepsis only for suspected adrenal insufficiency or refractory shock.

## Pitfalls and pearls

- Good pain control reduces an excessive ANS response.
- Shock with no clear cause that does not respond to fluid or inotropes: think adrenal shock. Do
  not wait for the cortisol level.
- IV glucose spares protein in simple fasting but not early after injury.
- Refeeding syndrome: fluid/sodium → volume overload, heart failure; carbohydrate drives
  phosphate into cells; ↑ demand for Mg and thiamine; ↑ insulin → ↓ K → arrhythmia. Frequencies:
  ↓ PO4 96%, ↓ Mg 51%, ↓ K 46%, ↓ Ca 27%, ↓ Na 8%; > 1 abnormality in 71%. At risk: start
  10 kcal/kg/day, reach target in 4-7 days; BMI < 14 or no intake > 2 weeks: start 5 kcal/kg/day
  with close arrhythmia monitoring; thiamine 200-300 mg/day + vitamin B complex 1-2 tab daily
  for ≥ 10 days, started with feeding.
- Overfeeding (fig.6): carbohydrate > 4-5 mg/kg/min → ↑ CO2 (delayed weaning), hyperglycemia,
  ↓ K and PO4, ↓ WBC function, fatty liver/cholestasis; fat > 1-1.5 g/kg/day → hyperlipidemia,
  RE blockade; protein > 1.5-2 g/kg/day → ↑ BUN.
- TPN glucose: raise and lower the concentration gradually. Hyperglycemia can be an early sign of
  catheter infection; hypoglycemia comes from weaning too fast.
- Check NG position by exam before every feed; chest x-ray if unsure.
- EN diarrhea: osmotic (sorbitol elixirs such as KCl or paracetamol, hypertonic small-bowel feed,
  malabsorption e.g. short bowel) or C. difficile.
- CRBSI: suspect with high spiking fever or sepsis and no other source. Risk by site: femoral >
  internal jugular > subclavian; by duration: 3-7 days 3-5%, > 7 days 5-10%. Differential culture
  (10 mL from peripheral and central line together): same organism, and central ≥ 3× CFU or
  ≥ 2 h faster. Fever only → culture + antibiotics and watch; septic → remove the line, culture
  the tip, broad-spectrum antibiotics, resuscitate.
- Long TPN → liver dysfunction / steatosis (overfeeding, excess lipid, no EN, bacterial
  overgrowth, nutrient toxicity). Prevent: avoid overfeeding, add EN, cyclic TPN (e.g. 8 h off a
  day).
- Acalculous cholecystitis on TPN: cholecystectomy; very sick patient → percutaneous
  cholecystostomy + antibiotics if no gangrene or perforation.
