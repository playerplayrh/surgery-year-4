## Introduction (book p.698)
- Over the last 3 years the concepts of esophageal disease have changed in staging and treatment, especially GERD and motility disorders; high-tech tools are used for diagnosis and treatment, and there are new guidelines. Cancer management: AJCC TNM staging 8th edition (2017), used from 2018. Minimally invasive surgery has also been added, especially Japanese data for benign and malignant disease.
- The 2020 edition was reviewed and updated from Shackelford 2019 and the latest NCCN (about 30% updated). Schwartz 2019 was of little use as its new data were minimal.

## Anatomy (p.698-701)
- The esophagus is a muscular tube that carries food from the mouth down to the stomach by peristalsis: primary peristalsis pushes food down; food that remains or refluxes back triggers secondary peristalsis, which sweeps it down again.
- Length 25-30 cm, from the lower border of the pharynx at C6 (cricoid cartilage level) to the stomach at T11. Measured from the incisor teeth it is about 40 cm; if measured from the nostril it is longer.
- 3 segments (figure: cervical, upper/middle/lower thoracic, abdominal, EGJ):
  1. Cervical esophagus: from the esophageal orifice to the sternal notch; on the left side of the neck, behind the trachea, in the tracheo-esophageal groove; about 3-5 cm long (the later figure says about 5 cm). By EGD from the incisors: 15-20 cm.
  2. Thoracic esophagus: about 20 cm, from the sternal notch (thoracic inlet) to the esophageal hiatus (EGJ). By EGD from the incisors: 20-40 cm. Divided in 3:
     - Upper thoracic: sternal notch to the tracheal bifurcation, 18-24 cm (earlier text version); in the later landmark version: sternal notch to the azygos vein, 20-25 cm.
     - Middle thoracic: from the tracheal bifurcation, half of the length is divided to 2 equal parts, the upper half being middle esophagus at 24-32 cm (earlier version); landmark version: azygos vein to inferior pulmonary vein, 25-30 cm. In this part the esophagus deviates from the left to the right side.
     - Lower thoracic: lower half down to the EGJ (T11), 32-40 cm (landmark version: inferior pulmonary vein to EGJ, 30-40 cm).
  3. Abdominal esophagus: about 2 cm long; important for GERD surgery.
- Normal diameter 2.5 cm.
- 3 normal anatomical constrictions (figure): 1. cricopharyngeus: only 1.4 cm wide (cervical constriction 14 mm); the narrowest point; beware at endoscopy; most prone to perforation; also where a Zenker diverticulum protrudes. 2. Bronchoaortic: 1.6 cm (15-17 mm), made by the left main bronchus and the aortic arch. 3. Diaphragmatic: at the hiatus, 1.9 cm (16-19 mm). (Between them: superior dilatation 19 mm, inferior dilatation 22 mm.)
- Arterial supply (figure): 1. cervical: from the inferior thyroid artery (not the superior); 2. thoracic: intercostal and bronchial arteries (tracheobronchial); 3. abdominal: inferior phrenic and the ascending branch of the left gastric artery. Pitfall choice: right gastric artery, which is wrong (also splenic artery dorsal).
- Venous drainage: follows the arteries, through the azygos and hemiazygos veins; the azygos vein crosses the esophagus at T5; in esophagectomy this vein must be tied and cut there.
- Nerves: vagus (figure: left and right vagus, esophageal plexus, anterior and posterior vagal trunks).

### Esophageal sphincters (p.700-701)
- Figure: phrenoesophageal ligament, crus of the diaphragm; UES and LES with longitudinal and circular muscle, gastric sling fibers, clasp.
- Upper: at C6, from the cricopharyngeus muscle, functioning as a valve; resting pressure 60 mmHg (figure: UES = 100 mmHg).
- Lower: a high-pressure zone made of circular muscle and gastric sling muscle fibers, functioning to prevent acid reflux from the stomach; resting pressure 13-15 mmHg (figure +20 mmHg); total length about 3.6 cm, with 2 cm in the abdomen.
- Figure of pressures: atmospheric 0; UES 100; intraesophageal -5; LES +20; intragastric +5 mmHg. Muscle type: striated at the top, mixed striated and smooth in the middle, smooth below.
- Seen from the outside, the EGJ is located using the angle of His as a landmark.
- LES factors (important in GERD):
  1. Raise pressure: 1.1 hormones: gastrin, bombesin, motilin, substance P, VIP (as printed); 1.2 drugs: antacid, cholinergic agonist, domperidone, metoclopramide, prostaglandin F2.
  2. Lower pressure: 2.1 hormones: CCK, glucagon, estrogen, progesterone, somatostatin; 2.2 drugs: calcium channel blocker, barbiturate, caffeine, diazepam, dopamine, prostaglandin E1 and E2; 2.3 foods: chocolate, coffee, ethanol, fat, peppermint.

## Histology (p.701-702)
- Cross section: layers from inside: stratified squamous epithelium, lamina propria (with lymphoid tissue), muscularis mucosa (with blood vessels and glands), circular muscle, neural plexus, longitudinal muscle; lumen with longitudinal rugae. Unlike the rest of the GI wall, the outermost layer is not serosa; there is only adventitia (remember).
- EGJ histology (figure): Z-line, esophagus vs stomach with gastric pits; layers: mucosa, submucosa, muscle coat, adventitia.
- Mucosa: squamous epithelium, pale pink band, except the distal 2 cm, which is columnar; the mucosa is fairly smooth, uniformly pink, may have low folds not more than 2 mm; no ulcer or nodule.
- Muscle: upper 1/3 striated, lower 2/3 smooth muscle (remember); 2 layers: OLIC = outer longitudinal and inner circular.
- No serosa (remember): there is only a fibroalveolar adventitia around it, so when cancer arises it spreads to adjacent organs quickly.
- Mucosa and submucosa provide strength and hold sutures; if a stitch does not catch this layer, a leak is certain.

## Imaging studies (p.702-)
- Esophagography: normal = smooth mucosa, with indentations at the 3 normal constriction sites and a straight axis. In esophageal centers a video-esophagogram is used for patients who will need esophagoscopy. Problem: send for barium swallow before scope, or proceed to scope without swallow. These diseases should have a barium swallow first, as a road map before the scope: 1. esophageal web; 2. small diverticulum; 3. Schatzki ring; 4. early achalasia; 5. complex esophageal stricture; 6. cricopharyngeal bar; 7. (author adds) caustic injury, to be done before starting dilatation.
- CT scan: should not be used as the primary imaging for mucosal disease and motility disorder. A normal esophagus on CT is collapsed flat, but may have a little air in the lumen, with diameter not more than 1 cm and wall thickness not more than 3 mm (except the LES, which may be 5 mm); fat planes separate it from adjacent organs.
- If the fat plane between the esophagus and aorta is lost in a cancer patient, suspect aortic invasion; likely unresectable. From the CT also look at mediastinal nodes, lung, liver. (Figures: chest CT of esophageal tumor; MRI.)
- MRI of the esophagus has limits from motion artifact and long study time.

## Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) (p.703-)
- Experts held a meeting in Montreal to define and classify GERD (published in Am J Gastroenterol).
- Definition: a condition that develops when gastric contents reflux up to touch the esophagus, causing symptoms and complications.
- Cause: reflux of gastric content up to the esophageal mucosa, causing troublesome symptoms about 2-3 times per week.
- Body's anti-reflux mechanisms:
  1. LES: a fairly tight valve with no acid reflux; reflux occurs when the total LES length is less than 2 cm, including an average intra-abdominal length of less than 1 cm; the width of the diaphragmatic hiatus does not affect LES function. Normal: LES pressure 13.8 mmHg; overall length 3.5 cm; abdominal length 2.2 cm.
  2. Acid clearance mechanism: even if the LES is strong, some reflux still occurs; normally acid reflux is not more than 4% per 24 hours; the body clears refluxed acid in 5 ways: 2.1 gravity; 2.2 salivation (saliva is alkaline); 2.3 the lower esophagus lies in the abdomen with positive pressure keeping it closed; 2.4 primary and secondary peristalsis (not tertiary peristalsis); 2.5 normal gastric emptying time (helps push acid down into the duodenum).
- Pathophysiology, 3 items:
  1. LES defect: the major mechanism (70%): 1.1 low LES pressure, causing leak; 1.2 reduced overall length; 1.3 abdominal length less than 2 cm (the number to remember).
  2. Inefficient acid clearance.
  3. Gastric abnormality: 3.1 excess gastric pressure; 3.2 gastric hypersecretion; 3.3 gastric dilatation; 3.4 delayed emptying, leaving acid in the stomach for long.
- Risk of esophagitis: 1. pH: not only reflux acid at pH <4; even alkaline reflux at pH >7, e.g. bile salt, can cause esophagitis; 2. timing: the longer the exposure to acid or bile, the more esophagitis.
- Clinical presentation:
  1. Typical GERD: the commonest is heartburn or pyrosis (80%), a burning pain at the epigastrium, worse after meals or at midnight; heartburn is often with regurgitation (54%); regurgitation is acid rising up to the mouth, passive, unlike vomiting, which is active expulsion.
  2. Atypical GERD: nonspecific symptoms: hoarseness, dental erosion, sore throat, chronic cough, asthma; these patients should be evaluated by a medical doctor or ENT first.
  3. Complications of GERD: dysphagia, anemia, Barrett.
- Montreal classification of GERD (figure): Esophageal syndromes: symptomatic syndromes (typical reflux syndrome; reflux chest pain syndrome) and syndromes with esophageal injury (reflux esophagitis; reflux stricture; Barrett's esophagus; esophageal adenocarcinoma). Extraesophageal syndromes: established associations (reflux cough syndrome; reflux laryngitis syndrome; reflux asthma syndrome; reflux dental erosion syndrome) and proposed associations (pharyngitis; sinusitis; idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; recurrent otitis media).
- Approach: with typical GERD symptoms and no alarm symptoms, treat as GERD at once = "PPI test", without need for investigation: start with PPI standard dose together with lifestyle modification (LSM) for about 1-2 weeks; PPI must be taken before meals (30 minutes before eating), especially breakfast, not before sleep; if improved, give maintenance PPI for another 4-8 weeks; then whether to continue or stop:
  1. Mild symptoms, not disturbing = NERD (non-erosive reflux disease, GERD without gross abnormality of the esophageal mucosa; symptoms present but no esophageal lesion on endoscopy, also called ENRD or endoscopy-negative reflux disease): may give PPI intermittently or on demand, taking the drug when symptoms occur.
  2. Severe symptoms recurring: must take the drug long term; if symptoms do not improve, give double dose; if still not better, change PPI from omeprazole to esomeprazole or add H2 blocker at bedtime; if still no response at this level, call it failure of medical treatment and send the patient for investigation.
- Diagnostic studies for GERD: 1. Indications: 1.1 PPI does not improve; 1.2 symptoms longer than 5 years (risk of Barrett); 1.3 age more than 50; 1.4 to differentiate from other diseases; 1.5 atypical GERD; 1.6 alarm symptoms: weight loss, dysphagia, odynophagia, fever, anemia, hematemesis. 2. Diagnostic evaluation consists of upper endoscopy and esophageal pH study.
- Since 2009 some argue that endoscopy should be done before PPI to assess the esophagus and exclude stomach and duodenal pathology; but by 2019 the majority still say give PPI first.
- AGA GERD practice guideline: diagnostic testing sequence: start with endoscopy; if negative and GERD still suspected but no abnormality found, do manometry, followed by pH testing.
- Upper endoscopy may find esophagitis (erosion) or complications of GERD such as Barrett, stricture or cancer; endoscopy should be done in typical GERD patients treated with PPI bid for 4-8 weeks whose symptoms do not improve, or who have alarm symptoms (ACG guidelines 2013). (Figure: normal squamocolumnar junction vs erosions.)

### Los Angeles classification (p.706)
- Endoscopic assessment of reflux esophagitis by severity of mucosal injury, 4 groups; grade A is the least, grade D has stricture already, meaning severe.

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| A | >=1 mucosal break no longer than 5 mm without continuation between mucosal folds |
| B | >=1 mucosal break longer than 5 mm without continuation between mucosal folds |
| C | >=1 mucosal break that is continuous between the tops of two or more mucosal folds but that involves less than 75% of the circumference |
| D | >=1 mucosal break that involves at least 75% of the esophageal circumference |

### Ambulatory esophageal pH testing (p.706-707)
- The single best test and the gold standard, especially in cases where conservative treatment with LSM + PPI does not improve, or endoscopy shows no evidence of esophagitis, including atypical GERD patients; do it to confirm before surgery that it is truly GERD. If a center can do it, it should (the answer in theory), but in practice it need not be routine.
- Before the study, stop PPI about 1 week; then a catheter with a probe at the tip measures acid in the esophagus, positioned 5 cm above the EGJ, recording acid for 24 hours. (Figure: catheter, monitoring device.)
- Nowadays there is a wireless probe attached to the esophageal wall in place of the NG tube, with a digital recorder carried by the patient; the trade name is Bravo pH monitoring: the patient is comfortable (no sore throat), can go to work, and gets pH reports up to 48 hours. Problems of Bravo: expensive, and results are still variable. (Figure: Bravo the size of a pencil tip.)
- Benefits of pH testing in GERD: 1. helps diagnosis; 2. baseline before surgery; 3. evaluate results of drug treatment or surgery.
- In normal people acid reflux in the esophagus is not more than 4% in 24 hours; if more than 4% it is probably true GERD. (Figure: normal vs GERD 24-hour pH tracing.)
- Guideline management (Gastroenterology and Hepatology; figure): typical GERD symptoms (heartburn +/- regurgitation): no alarm symptoms -> PPI therapy; additional alarm symptoms or risk factors -> endoscopy. PPI: persisting symptoms -> double-dose PPI; symptom resolution -> maintenance treatment; refractory GERD symptoms (rGERD) -> endoscopy, manometry, MII-pH monitoring -> (a) positive for acid reflux: optimize acid suppression, consider antireflux surgery, reflux inhibitor therapy; (b) positive for non-acid reflux: consider antireflux surgery, reflux inhibitor therapy; (c) negative for rGERD: functional dyspepsia or functional heartburn probable, consider neuromodulator therapy.

### Treatment of GERD (p.708-)
- Aims: reduce symptoms, heal esophagitis, maintain remission, and prevent complications.
1. Life style modification (LSM): the first-line treatment, needed in everyone whether or not surgery is needed.
2. Medical treatment with PPI, H2 blocker.
3. Antireflux surgery.
4. Endoscopic treatment.

### Life style modification (p.708)
1. Stay in an antigravity position, e.g. sit, stand, or sleep with the head raised 15 cm (figure: sleeping on a wedge makes acid reflux up less).
2. Avoid or stop these: 2.1 do not eat a heavy meal or lie down right after eating; wait at least 2 hours; 2.2 avoid fatty food, tea, coffee, chocolate; 2.3 avoid drugs that lower LES pressure, e.g. calcium channel blocker, nitrite or anticholinergic; 2.4 avoid lifting heavy things; 2.5 avoid tight clothes, especially in women who wear tight bras or girdles, which push acid up.
3. Do: 3.1 chew gum often to increase saliva; 3.2 lose weight; 3.3 wear loose clothes and no belts or tight pants, but do not unbutton the pants.
- Foods and medications that may worsen GERD: Foods: alcoholic beverages, carbonated beverages, citrus fruit drinks, chocolate, coffee, fatty foods, peppermint, spicy foods, tomato products. Medications: anticholinergics, barbiturates, caffeine, calcium channel blockers (e.g. dihydropyridine), nicotine, nitrates, NSAIDs, theophylline.

### Medical treatment (p.708-709)
1. Among acid-lowering drugs on the market, PPIs reduce reflux symptoms best; next is H2 blocker; the worst is antacid, effective in only 20%.
2. Long-term PPI keeps acid reduced all the time; disadvantages: bacterial overgrowth in the intestine, B12 problems, increased risk of community-acquired pneumonia; also long suppression of acid causes calcium malabsorption leading to osteoporosis-related fracture from PPI, common at the hip.
3. Dosage recommendation:

## Antireflux surgery (p.709-)
- Restores the esophagus and EGJ to a normal anatomical position together with creating an antireflux valve.
- Before surgery, confirm it is true GERD: US centers recommend full options before antireflux surgery: endoscopy, biopsy, upper GI, 24-hour pH and manometry.
- Principles of surgery:
  1. Maintain abdominal esophagus length not less than 2 cm, with total LES length not less than 3 cm.
  2. Create a valve at least 3 cm long to build pressure at the EGJ by suturing the fundus around the lower esophagus = fundoplication, to prevent reflux.
  3. Technique: non-absorbable suture, full thickness through the stomach and partial thickness of the esophagus (to prevent slipping), about 3 stitches about 1 cm apart, with the lowest stitch about 2 cm from the EGJ.
  4. For strong pressure needed: wrap 360 degrees = complete wrap; if low pressure is needed: partial wrap.
  5. Length of the wrap about 2 cm.
  6. Make LES pressure higher than resting gastric pressure, about 2 times.
  7. Protect the vagus nerve.
  8. If a hiatal hernia coexists: reduce the hernia and tighten the hiatus.

### Indications for surgical treatment (p.710)
1. Young age; cannot tolerate long-term drugs.
2. Patient wants surgery, and the doctor agrees to operate.
3. Patient has problems with drugs, e.g. vomiting after taking them.
4. Failure of medical treatment: symptom recurrence within 1-2 years; even if heartburn improves but regurgitation remains, consider surgery.
5. GERD complication, e.g. development of a stricture that fails dilatation.
6. Need to treat an underlying esophageal disease: e.g. myotomy in achalasia, or hiatal hernia repair, with partial wrap fundoplication.
7. Symptoms from severe esophagitis such as bleeding, ulcer, stricture, symptomatic Barrett (asymptomatic Barrett is not an indication for surgery; still debated).
8. Atypical GERD with respiratory complications such as recurrent aspiration, asthma not improved with PPI.
- Barrett patients who undergo antireflux surgery still need routine endoscopic surveillance.

### Contraindications for antireflux surgery (p.710)
1. Elevated BMI: bariatric surgery is better. 2. BE with HGD or adenocarcinoma: RFA or esophagectomy is better. 3. High surgical risk. 4. Portal hypertension.
- Before surgery, investigate first that it is true GERD; the best predictor of success is patients who improved on PPI but cannot stop the drug or have drug complications.
- Selecting patients: choose cases for which the surgical technique suits; success rate (symptom improvement) 80-90%.
- Whichever surgical technique is chosen, metaplastic change decreases but the risk of cancer in GERD patients does not change, so surveillance continues as before.

### Nissen fundoplication (p.710-711)
- Figure: wrap around the esophagus over a bougie (50 F bougie, 18 F gastric tube, No. 15 cervical dilator); photo of Rudolf Nissen.
- A complete wrap of 360 degrees, the operation that creates the highest LES pressure; very good long-term results: at 10 years follow-up 80% of patients do not suffer from GERD.
1. Nowadays laparoscopic more popular than open (paper selecting one best: lap preferred).
2. Suture the fundus around the esophagus, full 360 = complete fundoplication, which creates the highest EGJ pressure; remember the wrap is around the lower esophagus, not tied around the stomach.
3. In terms of reflux control, the results are best with this method.
4. Complications of Nissen: several, especially postoperative dysphagia. (Figure: causes of postoperative dysphagia: A = wrap dehiscence; B = recurrent or persistent hiatal hernia; C = misplaced or slipped wrap; D = herniation of entire wrap above diaphragm; also tight wrap/crura.)
   4.1 Wrap too tight, or some think vagal stretch during dissection causes inability to belch, called gas-bloat syndrome; recommend reassurance; the symptoms are temporary, usually resolving within 6 weeks.
   4.2 Dysphagia: usually temporary dysphagia seen in the first 2-4 weeks after surgery; the cause of swallowing difficulty most often is swelling or hematoma at the fundoplication and esophageal hiatus; fix by reassuring; maintain nutrition; keep NPO in the early period then gradually try clear liquid diet; about 97% resolve by themselves within 6 weeks. But if waiting and still cannot swallow liquid diet: send upper GI series and endoscopy to see whether the cause is anatomic abnormality, e.g. early hiatal hernia or slippage of the fundoplication, or just a wrap too tight; if the latter, dilate, with fairly good results; those who fail dilatation go for redo operation.
   - Prevention: before suturing the fundus, place a bougie dilator size 52-56 F in the esophageal lumen together with a cervical dilator size 15 on the outside to leave about 1 cm space, then suture the wrap, giving a complete loose or floppy Nissen fundoplication; Schwartz advises 60 F; this method lowers postoperative dysphagia to only 4%.
   4.3 Recurrent GERD: usually from the fundoplication coming undone or slipping off the EGJ; once diagnosed, treatment is redo: transabdominal or laparoscopic reoperation.

### Partial wrap procedures (p.712-)
- Several, suited for patients with high LES pressure, e.g. after myotomy in achalasia.
1. Toupet: partial wrap, posterior 270 degrees (figure: anterior wall of wrap sutured to esophagus).
2. Dor: anterior 180 degrees (figure).
3. Hill: wrap 180 degrees, an easy method; the author once did a case; the open technique is in Mastery of Surgery (figure: phrenoesophageal ligament divided, median arcuate ligament, celiac trunk, hiatus sutures).
4. Belsey: wrap 270 degrees through the front, a transthoracic operation; suits patients who need a myotomy in the chest already, e.g. DES (diffuse esophageal spasm) or nutcracker, or those with many adhesions in the abdomen; sutures the esophagus and the stomach to the diaphragm, creating fundoplication 240 degrees.
- (Belsey figure continues: wrap sutures a, b and suture technique.)
- Because antireflux surgery gives good results only with suitable patients and an experienced surgeon, less invasive procedures have started to come in, either laparoscopic procedures that place something around the EGJ or endoscopic techniques.

## Less invasive surgical procedure (p.713-714)
- Two ways: 1. surgery that places an "angle click" device (laparoscopic magnetic sphincter, LINX); angle click = something clamped around the EGJ to keep the abdominal esophagus 2 cm long and the click adds pressure at the EGJ; the antireflux effect was poor, swallowing difficult, so it was abandoned (as the author describes).
- Newer reports on the laparoscopic magnetic sphincter called the Linx reflux system: titanium beads like a rosary, with magnetic cores, placed laparoscopically around the LES to add LES pressure; the Linx chain expands to let food through; after food enters the stomach the magnets close the LES at once. (Figure: Linx expanded with food; Linx closed.) FDA approved.
- Results so far: in the first 2-3 years it reduces acid reflux symptoms well; from reports 2007-2013 the longest follow-up is about 6 years; results are still good but patient numbers are few; 3.6% had to have the Linx removed because of Linx-induced dysphagia; erosion and migration are very rare.
- Contraindications to LINX: presence of a hiatal hernia and Barrett esophagus.
- In 2012 Mayo Clinic suggested using Linx in patients who failed medical treatment but do not want fundoplication, or who are in the waiting period (therapeutic gap). (Figure: GERD current treatment options: PPI therapy for mild symptoms; therapy gap where no standard treatment exists = targeted Linx population; fundoplication surgery for severe.)

## Endoscopic treatment (p.714-716)
- A new trend, less invasive; in Cameron's current surgical therapy it is advertised heavily; some say it is just an injection under sedation (excessive), but for safety the patient should still take drugs to prevent aspiration while waiting for long-term results.
- Several methods:
  1. RFA (Stretta procedure): heat from RFA produces thermal burn at the lower esophageal wall, making the LES firm and tight, scar shrinking; results are fairly good at first, but after 4 years some reports say patients start to have symptoms again. (Figure: ablated Barrett metaplasia.)
  2. Enteryx: injection of a polymer, like a spongy black material, into the LES, making it stiffer; reduces GERD symptoms well but long-term safety reports are not yet out. (Figure: Enteryx injection steps A-F.)
  3. Endoscopic luminal gastroplasty or Endocinch plication: an endoscopic sewing device sutures the gastroesophageal junction as a partial plication.
  4. Full thickness plicator (NDO): this method has strong support; results reported fairly good, but awaiting long-term results.
- Just remember: within the next 5-10 years endoscopic treatment is expected to replace surgery; short-term and midterm results are fairly good; if long term is good too, one need not come to operate until the patient is tired of pain.
- Goals: 1. reduce transient LES relaxation; 2. increase baseline lower esophageal length; 3. reduce esophageal luminal diameter at the lower end; 4. recreate the angle of His; 5. form a gastroesophageal valve; 6. reduce compliance of gastric cardia and fundus.
- Indications of endoluminal treatment: 1. high-risk patients; 2. those difficult to operate on, e.g. previous upper abdominal surgery with many scars; 3. failed antireflux surgery.
- Exclusion criteria: 1. GERD symptoms without significant esophagitis; 2. esophageal stricture; 3. esophageal motility disorder; 4. BE; 5. hiatal hernia >3 cm; 6. BMI >35.
- Novel devices: 1. an implanted wire with a pacer device (Endostim): attached at the LES, releasing intermittent electrical current to stimulate the LES to contract, reducing acid reflux; currently being studied in humans. (Figure.) 2. From Japan: antireflux mucosectomy (ARM): using EMR or ESD tools to cut the mucosa at the LES, taking 1 cm of esophagus and 2 cm of stomach, hoping the scar tightens the valve; reports say very short-term results are good, but there is no controlled trial. (Figure: ARM steps.)

## Peptic stricture (p.717)
- Treatment: 1. try dilatation together with high-dose PPI as initial treatment; 2. if dilated 2-3 times and still dysphagic, place a stent together with PPI, although stents cause the esophageal wall to reduce... (as printed: stent use decreases esophageal stricture); 3. if stenting still does not work, antireflux surgery is needed. (Figure: balloon dilation of a peptic stricture.)

## Reflux-induced respiratory complications (p.717-)
- The current gold standard for diagnosing GERD with respiratory complications is ambulatory dual-probe pH monitoring: probe 1 placed distally, 5 cm above the LES; probe 2 placed proximally, 1 cm below the UES; this measures pH of the trachea, pharynx and proximal esophagus.
- In this group, antireflux surgery works well: 90% improve, especially asthma-like symptoms caused by acid reflux improve about 70%.

## Barrett's esophagus (BE) (p.718-)
- An acquired disorder: pathologic change of the squamous epithelium that is normal in the esophagus, becoming intestinal metaplasia: columnar epithelium from the stomach extends up into the esophagus, 3 cm or more [as printed; see also short/long segment below]. The cause of the cellular change is believed to be chronic injury of the distal esophagus from reflux of bile or gastric acid for more than 5 years.
- BE indicates chronic GERD; these patients have a risk of invasive adenocarcinoma (not SCC) up to 50-100 times, or 0.25-0.5% per year, or 10% over a lifetime; cancer risk depends on the pathologic grade of dysplasia; the lower the risk, the more... (as printed); also if biomarker aneuploidy or loss of heterozygosity of p53, outlook is bad.
- Figure: evolution of Barrett and cancer: squamous esophagus -> chronic inflammation -> Barrett's metaplasia -> low-grade dysplasia -> high-grade dysplasia -> invasive adenocarcinoma; driven by injury (acid and bile reflux, nitrous oxide) and genetics (gender, race, other factors e.g. COX-2), accumulating genetic changes.
- American College of Physicians (ACP) advises screening for Barrett in men older than 50 with GERD symptoms for more than 5 years together with any one of: 1. nocturnal reflux symptoms; 2. severe symptoms; 3. hiatal hernia; 4. elevated BMI; 5. tobacco use; 6. intra-abdominal distribution of fat; 7. family history of Barrett esophagus or esophageal cancer. ACP concludes that if Barrett is not found at screening, no further study is needed.

### Diagnosis (p.719-720)
- Upper endoscopy helps diagnosis and surveillance. At endoscopy report the location of Barrett, the length of the highest point from the hiatus (M, in cm) and the highest circumferential extent (C), reported by the Prague criteria. (Figure: proximal margin of the longest Barrett's tongue = M; proximal margin of circumferential Barrett's segment = C; proximal margin of the cardiac folds; example M 2 cm, C 1 cm; also measured from the incisors, e.g. M = 36 cm, C = 39 cm, GEJ 40 cm.)
- Endoscopic finding: gastric mucosa, reddish; the Westerners say salmon colour; this colour means Barrett.
- If Barrett mucosa is not clear, use helpers such as high-resolution endoscopy and narrow band imaging (NBI); NCCN 2019 strongly advises using them if the institution has the tools, since Barrett detection increases.
- Another way: tissue staining with Lugol staining: columnar epithelium has no glycogen so it does not take up stain, whereas squamous cells have much glycogen, so stain brown; or methylene blue sprayed on the mucosa; biopsy at the unstained area, send to an expert pathologist. (Figure: Lugol staining in Barrett's; methylene blue-stained Barrett's with a focal, superficial malignancy unstained.)
- Microscopic finding: intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells in the mucosal layer.
- Novel diagnostic technology: newer endoscopes can magnify the mucosa 150 times; used together with chromoendoscopy such as NBI to see the pit pattern, helping locate intestinal metaplasia for accurate biopsy. Others, such as volumetric laser endomicroscopy (VLE) promoted at US centers, need not be known.
- Biopsy to see the degree of dysplasia, which is the best indicator or marker of future cancer. Number of pieces: NCCN 2019 advises 6-8 pieces with standard-size endoscopy forceps.
  1. Seattle protocol: 4 pieces every 1 cm, four quadrants, from the lower to the upper margin; reduces error; disadvantage: long time, high cost, and increases bleeding risk; whereas NCCN advises 6-8 pieces (any pieces positive counts positive). (Figure: Seattle biopsy protocol.)
- Short-segment BE: the columnar mucosa extends up the esophagus less than 3 cm; if longer than 3 cm it is called long-segment Barrett; short-segment BE treated has a high chance of regression.
- Long-segment BE: found 4 times less than short segment.
- BE itself is not a strong indication for surgery, but there is a trend toward antireflux procedure; surgery helps heal mucosal injury, improve symptoms and prevent Barrett progression, but does not change the natural progression of BE; so once BE is found, the patient should have lifelong surveillance.
- When asymptomatic BE is found: treat as GERD with LSM and PPI; prophylactic antireflux surgery right away is still awaiting answers.
- Endoscopic surveillance: multiple random biopsies with a larger forceps to detect dysplasia better, biopsy per the Seattle protocol: starting from the lower margin of Barrett upward every 1 cm, taking four quadrants, 4 pieces.
- Histological classification: 1. indefinite dysplasia; 2. non-dysplasia: esophageal cancer risk 0.4% per year; 3. low-grade dysplasia (LGD): 0.7% per year; 4. high-grade dysplasia (HGD).
- Meaning of the "-plasia" suffix (figure): anaplasia (structural differentiation loss within cell or group of cells); aplasia (organ or part missing); hypoplasia (below-average number of cells); hyperplasia (proliferation of cells); neoplasia (abnormal proliferation); dysplasia (change in cell or tissue phenotype); metaplasia (conversion in cell type); prosoplasia (development of new cell function); desmoplasia (connective tissue growth). In Barrett pay attention to dysplasia.
- Non-dysplasia BE turns into dysplasia at 5-10% per year; but once dysplasia has developed there is a chance of turning into adenocarcinoma.
- For HGD: in the past it was believed to carry cancer risk of 6% per year with hidden foci of cancer about 20%; now the cancer risk figure is only 0.25% per year (2014 study: HGD 400 patients, only 1 cancer per year).
- Attempts to predict who will progress: studying the role of biomarkers, currently in research, looking for genomic instability such as p53.

### Management based on histology (NCCN V.2 2018 and Shackelford 2019) (p.722-)
1. No dysplasia (non-dysplasia): repeat biopsy with a larger forceps 1-2 more times within 1 year to be sure of no dysplasia (to detect dysplasia better); if truly none, surveillance endoscopy with 4-quadrant biopsy every 2 cm every 3 years, together with treating GERD (PPI or surgery).
2. If the expert pathologist is unsure, reading indefinite (indeterminate) dysplasia, meaning the biopsy was taken while esophagitis was active: treat with PPI high dose 40 mg BID, then repeat scope and biopsy in another 4-6 weeks; send to a new pathologist.
3. If 2 expert pathologists agree on low-grade dysplasia: follow up with scope and biopsy every 6-12 months, the first time at 6 months to reassure there is no HGD, together with treating GERD (PPI or surgery). Currently in LGD RFA is starting to be advised to give new re-epithelialization, with RFA 1-year results being very good (NCCN 2019); but most prefer repeated scope and 4-quadrant biopsy every 1-2 cm per year more (as long as dysplasia persists), stopping annual endoscopy and changing to every 3 years when pathology shows no dysplasia 2 times in a row.
4. High-grade dysplasia (HGD), with 2 expert pathologists in agreement: HGD is still debated: if the doctor has not stopped, the reasoning is that the cancer progression rate that people believed for so long to be high, about 1% per year, is not as much as in earlier reports; the latest figure is 0.25% per year (2014; 1 cancer per year in 400 HGD patients); so treatment has changed from esophagectomy to endoscopic eradication therapy, e.g. RFA, with stronger support than continued surveillance.
- Options for HGD:
  4.1 Endoscopic treatment with radiofrequency ablation using the Barrx RFA catheter or the HALO system; currently endoscopic RFA is the primary treatment: randomized controlled trial found RFA can eradicate Barrett and dysplasia, also reduces progression to cancer, with complete response 76-100% (average 93%) and 5-year survival >80%. Remember endoscopic treatment requires that there is no feature of cancer in Barrett, e.g. ulcer; nodular HGD, which used to be believed not suitable for endoscopic treatment, now: if EUS shows no invasive cancer, i.e. no invasion at the submucosa, treat like carcinoma in situ: EMR.
  4.2 NCCN 2019 favors EMR more than RFA, advising endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) in cases that are well to moderately differentiated lesions, tumor confined to muscularis mucosa (pT1a), size not more than 2 cm, no evidence of lymphovascular invasion (LVI) or lymph node metastasis, clear lateral and deep margin; but if the EMR pathology shows margin not free, RFA can be added (ablation).
  4.3 Some advise scope with biopsy every 3 months until 1 year; if still not cancer, continue every 3-6 months; this method is for patients not fit, unsuitable for endoscopic treatment, or who refuse endoscopic treatment.
  4.4 Esophagectomy: the standard of care in the past; now used only when it cannot be completely eradicated with endoscopic RFA, EUS shows submucosal invasion (T1b), or there is a positive node.
5. If 2 expert pathologists agree on T1a esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC): treat with endoscopic eradication therapy.
- Summary: mucosal ablation or EMR should not be used in cases with a high chance of recurrence, e.g. long-segment BE with multifocal lesions.
- Newest way to detect Barrett: endoscopic wide-area transepithelial sampling (WATS) with 3-dimension cytology, using abrasive brushing at the Barrett mucosa to collect cells for a cytologist to read; increases the chance of finding HGD and adenocarcinoma by another 14.4%; the brushing takes much less time than random biopsy. (Figures: brush; random biopsy vs WATS3D brushing.)
- Surveillance guidelines (figure, 2 guidelines used in textbooks):
  1. Diagnosis of Barrett's esophagus: A. columnar-type mucosa in the distal esophagus on endoscopy; B. specialized intestinal metaplasia on histology. No dysplasia: repeat endoscopy in 6-12 months to confirm lack of dysplasia, then endoscopy every 3 years. Low-grade dysplasia: repeat endoscopy in 6 months and ask an expert pathologist to confirm LGD, then endoscopy every 12 months, endoscopic ablation. High-grade dysplasia: ask two expert pathologists to confirm HGD, consider new imaging techniques; endoscopy every 3 months with biopsy every 1 cm; endoscopic or surgical treatment.
  2. BO (Barrett's) with flat columnar mucosa: 4-quadrant biopsy (Seattle protocol) -> presence of dysplasia confirmed by 2 GI pathologists: No dysplasia: surveillance OGD depending on BO length and presence of IM: 1) max length <3 cm without IM: repeat OGD; if similar findings obtained despite an adequate number of biopsies, consider discharge; 2) max length <3 cm + IM: OGD every 3-5 years; 3) max length >=3 cm: OGD every 2-3 years. Indefinite for dysplasia: optimize PPI therapy and repeat OGD in 6 months; if dysplasia follow LGD/HGD flowchart; if none return to no-dysplasia. LGD: repeat endoscopy in 6 months; LGD confirmed by 2 GI pathologists and MDT discussion: offer RFA; if RFA not performed, 6-monthly endoscopic surveillance is recommended. HGD or T1a OAC: MDT discussion: RFA with 3-monthly follow-up for 1 year and yearly thereafter.

### Role of RFA in BE (p.725)
- A new trend, coming on strong, for eradicating dysplastic BE: using the RFA halo device releasing high-frequency radio waves via a heat-generating balloon; the heat causes re-epithelialization (new squamous epithelium). (Figures: RFA of Barrett's: sizing, catheter positioned over guidewire, balloon expanded and energy applied, circumferential mucosal ablation pattern; see-treat-complete: neosquamous epithelium appears within weeks, complete healing in about 8-12 weeks.)
- Put the scope in and let the balloon contact the metaplastic tissue, releasing energy only as deep as the muscularis mucosa (depth targeted: muscularis mucosae = ablation target depth, controlling ablation depth avoids stricture; submucosa with esophageal glands = EMR depth; muscularis propria = surgical depth) because deeper would cause stricture; currently the stricture rate of this equipment is <1%.
- RFA reverses columnar epithelium back to squamous epithelium up to 97%, especially non-dysplastic BE (NDBE).

## Esophageal motility disorders (p.725-)
- Diseases from uncoordinated function between peristalsis and the sphincters of the esophagus causing dysphagia, regurgitation and substernal pain.
- 2 major groups by cause:
  1. Primary esophageal motility disorders: know only 4: 1.1 achalasia (must know); 1.2 diffuse esophageal spasm (DES); 1.3 nutcracker; 1.4 hypertensive LES.
  2. Secondary: the prominent ones to know: scleroderma, GERD (a consequence after acid reflux).
- Primary motility disorders again are divided in 2: 1. Hypomotility disorder: achalasia; 2. Hypermotility disorders: DES, nutcracker, hypertensive LES.
- Doctors preparing for the exam must remember manometric study for correct diagnosis and treatment (do not be vague).
- Normal: 1. LES pressure 10-20 (HPZ); 2. the LES relaxes on swallowing, but in achalasia there is no relaxation, so swallowing is blocked; 3. during contraction or peristalsis the esophageal pressure is only between 30-100 mmHg; a value above 180 is a disease alone: nutcracker.

### Achalasia (p.726-)
- The most common primary motility disorder, a motor system abnormality; found in people aged 30-50 years, women and men about equally.
- Etiology: selective loss of inhibitory neurons of ganglion cells in the myenteric Auerbach's plexus, so excitatory neurons predominate and stimulate the LES to contract all the time until LES pressure is very high (remember), and there is no relaxation after swallowing; but the cause of the selective loss of inhibitory neurons is unknown; some believe heredity, viral infection, or autoimmune (all unproven).
- Key symptoms: dysphagia 90%, with regurgitation of undigested food, which is primarily related to the failure of LES relaxation; some with aspiration pneumonia or weight loss from difficulty swallowing.
- Dysphagia characteristic: occurs with all types of food, solid or liquid; the symptoms are severe, with cold water making the patient strain, arch the back or jump to help food pass down; the patient feels that when swallowing, food sticks in the chest for a long time.
- Key sign of the disease: no peristalsis (if wrong, wrong) makes swallowing difficult and regurgitating food residue since there is no LES relaxation.
- Food retained in the esophagus long makes the esophagus gradually dilate; measured pressure in the esophagus is higher than normal; with food retained long there is chronic esophagitis; after a long time (said to be 14-28 years) the risk of SCC at the middle esophagus (remember) increases.
- In a patient presenting with aspiration pneumonia, besides infiltration, look at the chest film: if a shadow is seen along the right side of the mediastinum (dilated esophagus), suspect achalasia. (Figure: chest film showing a large esophagus.)
- Barium swallow: fluoroscopy shows a large dilated esophagus with an air-fluid level at the mediastinum, and the lower end tapers to a small point like a bird's beak (bird's beak sign), but the bird's beak sign is positive only 2/3. (Figure: bird's beak.)
- In achalasia type 3, spastic contraction in the lower part is seen as a corkscrew appearance.
- Timed barium esophagography (TBE): technique used in some institutions: the patient drinks 200 cc barium and films at 1, 2 and 5 minutes; if barium that was swallowed remains in the esophagus beyond 5 minutes, the more consistent with achalasia. (Figure: patient swallows in 15-20 seconds, radiographs at 1, 2, 5 minutes; barium sulphate 45% w/v, 100-250 mL.)
- Over time the esophagus becomes progressively large, like a large intestine, called sigmoid esophagus.
- If symptoms fit... barium swallow shows the classic sign ... (asked by the board), the patient needs to be sent as achalasia; the next step is upper endoscopy and biopsy to rule out cancer first.
- Upper endoscopy (figure: normal cardia vs achalasia): 1. NPO and irrigate NG tube 2-3 days, wash out food residue from the esophagus as much as possible, to prevent aspiration. 2. The cardia looks rosette-like, a fairly tight opening, but the scope passes the LES with difficulty, with resistance felt: think stricture with cancer, so biopsy.
- If no cancer is found, do manometric study next.
- Recommendation from SAGES 2013 on investigation: in suspected achalasia, send 3 investigations: barium esophagogram, EGD, esophageal manometry.

### Esophageal manometry (p.728)
- The best diagnostic investigation, as sensitivity is highest and it finds functional abnormality before anatomical abnormality is seen. (Figures: normal cardia vs achalasia tracings; conventional vs HRM.)
- Conventional manometry: the patient swallows a manometric catheter with pressure sensors into the esophagus, then it is pulled up; then wet swallows: 5 cc water, wait 30 seconds, 10 swallows, measuring pressure at the LES, body, UES, at rest and during swallow.
- Normal: on wet swallow esophageal peristalsis starts from top to bottom; on the colour band, the colour band tilts diagonally to the right with each swallow.
- Findings in achalasia:
  1. Lack of peristalsis (always found): the bands lie in the same line, no peristaltic wave.
  2. Non or incomplete LES relaxation during deglutition.
  3. Elevated resting LES pressure: normal <20 mmHg; if >26, LES contracts very strongly; seen in both achalasia and hypertensive LES; currently the mean pressure at the junction between esophagus and stomach, called integrated relaxation pressure (IRP), is used: in achalasia IRP >15 mmHg.
  4. Esophageal pressurization: normally pressure in the esophagus is lower than in the stomach because the esophagus is in the chest (negative pressure); but in achalasia pressure in the esophagus is higher than in the stomach.

### High resolution esophageal manometry (HRM) (p.728-729)
- Studies esophageal motility for each swallow, measuring pressure and translating it into colour; the catheter has about 36 sensors, each level 1 cm apart; much more precise than conventional manometry; HRM is now the standard investigation in diagnosing achalasia. (Figures: normal swallow: UES, body with normal peristalsis, LES with normal relaxation; achalasia: simultaneous contractions and absence of peristalsis in the esophageal body.)
- Colour values for pressure depend on the brand and model: Red = 125; Yellow = 100; Violet = 75; Pink = 50; Purple = 25; Blue = 5; Black = 0.
- Chicago classification (meeting every 4 years; latest version 3 in 2014; V.4 expected soon) divides achalasia into 4 subgroups by function severity using HRM; the classification helps predict treatment outcome (if a resident remembers only one: type 3 has the poorest treatment results).

### Chicago classification of achalasia and EGJ outflow obstruction (p.729-730)
- From HRM, IRP >15 mmHg (normal <5 mmHg) per Chicago V.3: IRP above normal plus weak peristalsis is enough to fit achalasia; then group into 4 types (figure: A EGJ outflow obstruction: impaired LES relaxation, normal or impaired peristalsis; B type II: impaired LES relaxation, absent peristalsis, increased pan-esophageal pressure; C type I: impaired LES relaxation, absent peristalsis, normal esophageal pressure (bird's beak); D type III: impaired LES relaxation, absent peristalsis, distal esophageal spastic contractions).
  1. Type 1: classic achalasia: no esophageal peristalsis and no esophageal pressurization in the body.
  2. Type 2: achalasia with esophageal compression: no peristalsis, but esophageal pressurization in more than 20%; this type has the best treatment result.
  3. Type 3: achalasia with premature (spastic) contractions in more than 20%; some call it spastic achalasia; the type with the worst treatment result.
  4. Type 4 (newly created, "achalasia in evolution", EGJ outflow obstruction): a group with peristalsis that does not fit type 1, 2, 3.

## Treatment of achalasia (p.730-)
- Aim: make swallowing better, reduce LES contraction; symptomatic relief only; the esophagus still has aperistalsis as before.
- Because many treatments exist, a clinical scoring system is used to compare: Eckardt score, whose advantage is that no investigation is needed, only 4 symptoms as parameters: weight loss, dysphagia, retrosternal pain, regurgitation. A successful treatment gives Eckardt score <4; a score of 4 or more means treatment failure.

| Score | Weight loss (kg) | Dysphagia | Retrosternal pain | Regurgitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | None | None | None | None |
| 1 | <5 | Occasional | Occasional | Occasional |
| 2 | 5-10 | Daily | Daily | Daily |
| 3 | >10 | Each meal | Each meal | Each meal |
- Other systems such as the Vaezi score need a barium swallow, so they are not popular.
- 6 methods: 1. drugs; 2. botox injection; 3. dilatation; 4. myotomy; 5. esophagectomy; 6. peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM).

### Pharmacotherapy (p.731)
- Indications: 1. patient refuses all other treatment; 2. other methods tried and not effective; 3. as adjunct together with other methods, or while waiting for other methods; 4. very early case (in reality the dilated esophagus isn't seen yet, so how to diagnose is a question); 5. the highest-risk patients who cannot tolerate even mild sedation (a nitroderm patch is still needed).
- This method should be the last choice, since swallowing improves only in the short term; when the drug wears off the patient has dysphagia as before.
- Commonly used drugs: 1. calcium channel blocker, e.g. nifedipine 10-40 mg sublingual, 15 minutes before meals every meal; symptoms improve 30 minutes after the drug and last about 2 hours in about 70%; beware BP drop. 2. Long-acting nitrates 5-10 mg sublingual 15 minutes before each meal; disadvantages: headache, BP drop, leg edema.

### Intrasphincteric botox injection (p.731)
- Mechanism: botox inhibits release of acetylcholine from the presynaptic nerve ending, so sphincter tone decreases by about 33%.
- Suited to patients at high risk for surgery or dilatation.
- Inject botox (20 units/ml) 80-100 units at 4-8 points around the LES, above the Z line. (Figure: needle injecting botulinum toxin into the LES.)
- Results: early symptoms improve 60-85%; after 12 months the drug starts to wear off, leaving only about 42% who can still swallow; the rest return to dysphagia as before.
- Disadvantages: 1. must inject often, and the drug is expensive; 2. repeated injection causes toxic inflammation followed by fibrosis, so later dilatation or surgery becomes harder.

### Endoscopic pneumatic dilatation (p.732)
- Currently a polyethylene balloon or Rigiflex balloon 10 cm long, 3 sizes, is used, inserted under direct vision or under fluoroscopy; purpose: disrupt the circular muscle at the LES. (Figure: Rigiflex dilator: guide wire, pneumatic dilator, balloon inflated expanding LES, restored flow.)
- Technique: liquid diet about 2 days, NPO 12 hours, sedate the patient, EGD and place a guide wire followed by the pneumatic balloon; when the balloon is at the LES, inflate with a hand-held manometer, and hold dilated for 1 minute; start at 30 mm and increase stepwise up to 40 mm; or use Bougie dilators, the largest 60 French (Fr): dilate to 30-45 Fr but do not exceed 50 Fr, since perforation would occur; after dilatation observe the patient for 2-6 hours; if no problem send home. (Figure: simple bougies, guide wire dilator, balloon dilator.)
- Risk of perforation is often on the left side; if perforated, open a left thoracotomy, close the perforation, followed by a myotomy on the opposite side.
- Complications: perforation 2-6%; reflux 25-30%, so antireflux surgery must go together.
- Figure: barium swallow and endoscopic views before/after dilatation (a-d).
- Contraindications to pneumatic dilatation: 1. uncooperative patient; 2. severe coagulopathy; 3. active bleeding; 4. deep ulcer coexisting; 5. no safe route to pass the dilator.
- Conditions not to dilate (high perforation chance): 1. late-stage achalasia; 2. epiphrenic diverticulum; 3. long hiatal hernia; 4. marked angulation or marked mucosal bridging; 5. previous surgery at this area with adhesions too strong.
- Less success with pneumatic dilatation is seen in: 1. age <40 years (young age, tight LES; do not think deeply); 2. recurrence after dilatation more than 2 times.
- Compared with botox, dilatation is slightly better in the first month (about 1 point...) but beyond 6 months dilatation is clearly better.
- Botox injection or dilatation should not be done more than 2 times, or at most 3 times; if the result is not satisfactory, operate.
- Teachers in earlier times taught that pneumatic dilatation should be initial treatment (present perfect: remember); nowadays surgeons who like operating through the scope have more experience and enjoy myotomy because myotomy gives better long-term results than dilatation; so Heller is advised first, and there are pressures in the doctors' community to prefer myotomy.
- SAGES 2013 classic outcomes:
  1. Botox: early success 80%; at 1 year success 50%; long-term success <20%.
  2. Dilation: early success 80%; 1-year success 50%; long-term success <20% (not very effective, probably because of fear of perforation, so not done vigorously enough).
  3. Myotomy: early success 98%; 1 year 95%; long-term success 92%.
- Large centers advise laparoscopic Heller myotomy as initial treatment in achalasia patients with good fitness; in patients not fit for surgery and expected to live no more than 2 years: botox with nifedipine.

### Surgical myotomy (modified Heller's myotomy) (p.734-735)
- Indications: 1. young patients, especially <40 years: LES is tight and dilatation is difficult (do not think deeply); 2. fit for surgery; 3. esophageal conditions unsuitable for dilatation; 4. botox or dilatation more than 2, at most 3 times with no success; 5. patient wants surgery.
- In the past it was an open abdominal operation: open the abdomen, expose the EGJ, cut the muscle layers until the mucosa bulges out. Nowadays laparoscopic myotomy, not open, is the answer (follow the trend).
- Between the thoracoscopic and the laparoscopic approach choose laparoscopic: GERD after surgery is less since an adequate myotomy can be done via the esophageal hiatus; also the abdominal approach takes less time and the patient stays less time in the hospital.
- Laparoscopic approach: open the hiatus, put in the scope, cut the inner circular muscle of the esophagus going up 6 cm and down 2-3 cm onto the stomach side.
- Especially on the esophagus side where the muscle has 3 layers, with sling fiber muscle adding another layer: if cut too little, recurrence; but if cut too much, reflux appears.
- Trend: some advise cutting longer on the gastric side, at least 3 cm; but more data are needed; keep 2-3 cm for now.
- Because postoperative reflux is found in as much as 20%, it is advised to add a partial-wrap fundoplication at the same time; total wrap is not advised because in achalasia the esophagus is poorly contractile and pressure is high; doing a Nissen fundoplication carries a risk of postoperative dysphagia.
  1. Toupet or posterior 270 degrees: the first choice in theory, but dissecting the posterior side takes more time and is harder than the anterior approach.
  2. Dor or anterior 180 degrees: pressure not as good as Toupet, but advantage: the myotomy is done anteriorly, so Dor covers the myotomy; easier, no need to dissect posteriorly, and if mucosal perforation occurs during anterior myotomy, cover it with a Dor patch at once.
- Current belief that Toupet is better than Dor probably is not true, since reports found no difference in dysphagia or GERD; so in practice Dor is used more often. (Figure: myotomy with Dor: trapped food, split muscle, mucosa, stomach patch sutured over.)
- Antireflux procedure should not be Nissen or a complete wrap, because dysphagia would occur again, since in achalasia there is no peristalsis.
- Preoperative factor that predicts a good myotomy result: resting LES pressure >30 mmHg.
- If myotomy fails, it is often from repeated fibrosis; try dilating first; most improve; if it does not work, redo operation: take down the fundoplication, then do a myotomy at a new location.
- Summary: in young achalasia patients, with no contraindication to surgery, the treatment of choice is laparoscopic Heller myotomy with Toupet antireflux.
- Endoscopic assessment to see whether the myotomy is adequate, including assessing mucosal integrity, should be done during surgery or before leaving the OR rather than afterward; SAGES advises intraoperative EGD.

### Esophagectomy (p.735-736)
- Used in end-stage achalasia, which is usually found about 10 years after diagnosis; about 1% will need this treatment. (Figure: megaesophagus or sigmoid esophagus.)
- Indications: 1. megaesophagus: size >8 cm (some say 10 cm); 2. tortuous or sigmoid-shaped esophagus; 3. failure of myotomy; 4. reflux stricture that fails dilatation.
- For megaesophagus never treated before, when first seen with esophagus >8 cm, most people believe treatment still starts with Heller first; myotomy still gives a success rate of 50-90% (remember), except Schwartz 2015 says no need for myotomy: do esophagectomy straight away; the author believes in the majority view: if myotomy fails, only then do esophagectomy, which is how it is done.
- Transhiatal esophagectomy (THE) is the theory, but beware bleeding in a large megaesophagus; when pulling the esophagus out blindly it may bleed; there is a chance of converting to transthoracic esophagectomy about 6.5%; and if done laparoscopically, reports say MIS can be used.
- Conduit: still stomach; next is right colon.

### Peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) (p.736-738)
- An endoscopic treatment that came on strongly; the most famous is Haruhiro Inoue from Showa University Northern Yokohama Hospital, who used POEM in achalasia, DES and vigorous achalasia; the results made him happy, with a report from Europe 2013 totaling 846 patients having POEM with clinical success 98% at 10 months; up to 2015 almost 3,000 POEM cases found the early results about equal to Heller.
- As POEM became more common, patients who had botox injection or dilatation could still have POEM; sigmoid esophagus, usually ending with esophagectomy, could be treated with POEM.
- Problem everyone awaits: long-term follow-up: from the longest reports, over 5 years, clinical success with Eckardt score <3 was up to 83% with no one needing re-intervention.
- Results look good, but the postoperative problem is post-GERD, found often; Schwartz says post-POEM reflux is up to 40-50%; Sabiston 2017 also raises doubts on this.
- At the SAGES 2018 meeting the issue of reflux after POEM was discussed again; a report from Japan, by Hiru Shiwaku, advised a new POEM technique: open the submucosal tunnel at the lesser curvature and myotomize posteriorly so as not to cut the anterior sling fibers, cutting only the inner circular muscle; with this technique reflux after POEM is much less. Need to keep listening since there are few cases and follow-up is not yet a year.
- Contraindications of POEM: 1. severe cardiopulmonary disease; 2. severe coagulopathy; 3. previous EMR, ESD, RFA; 4. previous RT of the esophagus; 5. achalasia with epiphrenic diverticulum; 6. achalasia with hiatal hernia.

### POEM technique (p.737)
- Figure: A submucosal injection and mucosal incision; B creation of submucosal tunnel; C distal esophageal circular muscle dissection; D LES myotomy; E closure of mucosal incision.
1. Before the procedure, liquid diet about 2-3 days; if the scope shows much food residue, place an NG tube and irrigate with NSS until clear, to prevent aspiration.
2. Place an ET tube, general anesthesia (some use IV sedation); insert the scope, open the mucosa 10-15 cm above the EGJ.
3. Inject saline mixed with indigo carmine dye; use a triangular knife to make a tunneled dissection in the submucosal layer going down to 2 cm below the EGJ; wherever there is bleeding, coagulate.
4. Start the endoscopic myotomy by cutting the inner circular muscle about 10 cm above the EGJ (takes a little time); recent studies compare with full-thickness myotomy, meaning cutting the outer longitudinal layer too; full-thickness myotomy is faster, with results and complications not different.
5. Close the mucosa with clips.
- After surgery NPO about 24 hours, then liquid diet; if able to eat, discharge within 72 hours; beginners do EGD before feeding to check the mucosa at the clipped site.
- Advantages of POEM: 1. long myotomy as wished, so suitable for achalasia type 3, DES, jackhammer esophagus, where the circular muscle must be cut high, perhaps up to the aortic arch; POEM does this comfortably, whereas with laparoscopy one can reach only 8-10 cm above the LES. 2. No injury to surrounding structures, so an antireflux procedure is not needed as with laparoscopic myotomy (in standard textbooks the Western view disagrees). 3. Myotomy can be done in any direction, so repeat is easy. 4. An attractive option in patients who had Heller myotomy before and have treatment failure.
- Complications of POEM: 1. mediastinitis from esophageal perforation; 2. pneumoperitoneum and pneumomediastinum during the myotomy, most absorb themselves; beware compartment syndrome; if pneumothorax, place ICD; 3. bleeding seen during cutting: coagulate; if found postoperatively, EGD then coagulate or clip; 4. GERD seen often, but luckily mostly mild esophagitis by LA classification, not as severe as after Heller, because the angle of His is not disturbed, which helps reduce acid reflux.
- Long-term results are still lacking: SAGES 2017 report: 5-year POEM in 23 achalasia patients: symptomatic success 83%; the number is still small, second to Heller.
- Currently laparoscopic Heller myotomy with partial fundoplication remains the treatment of choice.
- World Journal of Gastroenterology 2016 algorithm (figure): Achalasia -> assessment of life expectancy and comorbidities -> low risk: <40 yr old: Heller myotomy; >40 yr old: pneumatic dilation; failure of one goes to the other (dashed: POEM); Heller failure: repeat Heller or POEM; failure -> esophagectomy. High risk: pharmacologic or botulinum toxin (failure -> expandable stent); POEM option.

### Esophageal stent (p.738-739)
- From failure of dilatation therapy (dilate too much -> perforation, dilate too little -> recurrence): a Chinese RCT compared dilatation with temporary stent: after dilatation a stent is placed, left 4-5 days (temporary stent); 155 patients, followed about 10 years. Result: 5-year clinical success 99-100%; at 10 years clinical success 92%, much better than dilatation alone, and even comparable to lap myotomy.
- SAGES 2012 concluded the Chinese results look too good, and stressed the success must be with Chinese patients and Chinese stents used only in China (not the stents of the U.S.).
- Another Chinese group, led by Zeng, published in Esophagus 2014: stent use had clinical success of only 50% in 36 months.
- Conclusion by the author: this method is not better than dilatation alone, and a stent is expensive; use it with end-stage esophageal cancer.

## Spastic esophageal disorders (p.739-741)
- Motility disorders with abnormal muscle contraction: squeezing too hard, causing pain during swallowing, sometimes thought to be GERD or even heart disease, or neurotic.
- Know: diffuse esophageal spasm (DES), nutcracker esophagus (NE), jackhammer esophagus (JE).
- Currently manometry uses HRM for everything; measure contraction pressure with DCI (distal contractile integral), calculated from amplitude in mmHg, duration of contraction in seconds, and length of the spastic esophagus in cm multiplied together; DCI >450 mmHg/s/cm is considered a true disease in this group.

### Diffuse esophageal spasm (DES) (p.739-740)
- In the past DES was believed to be a disorder of high-amplitude contraction (spasm), but now it is found not true: DES is a disorder of swallow-induced high-pressure contraction while the LES is still normal, which is how we separate it from achalasia.
- DES: chest pain at swallowing is the prominent symptom; dysphagia is secondary.
- DES still has peristalsis; there are tertiary contractions after swallowing with high pressure but not over 180.
- Barium swallow shows the classic corkscrew appearance (twisted like a screw); on video-fluoroscopy: intermittent peristalsis, repetitive tertiary contractions >3 peaks, each peak >6 seconds; food passes but contractions do not stop. (Figure: corkscrew esophagus; corkscrew tool.)
- Manometric study: simultaneous esophageal contraction in >20% of wet swallows with amplitude >30 mmHg; LES pressure is usually normal.
- Management:
  1. Rule out cardiac chest pain first (MI); if the heart is normal, reassure the patient that DES is a benign disease; most DES patients are nervous, some think they will die of heart disease or cancer.
  2. If after explaining thoroughly the pain persists, the next step is to see whether the patient has GERD too.
  3. No one knows the relationship between DES and GERD, but if there is GERD, treating it improves DES chest pain; so for DES with GERD, do 24-hour pH monitoring; if truly GERD, treat aggressively as GERD; if on PPI and not improved, may need antireflux.
  4. For those without GERD who still have pain after good reassurance: calcium channel blocker, nitroglycerine, or the new drug sildenafil first.
  5. Do not forget an antidepressant too; these patients are often depressed.
  6. If the drugs still do not work: endoscopy then try dilatation; then botox injected into the spastic segment, which may be longer; if repeated botox once or twice still does not work (these cases often never get better, doctor shopping), refer.
  7. For a patient who still does not respond to both drugs and dilatation, make up your mind to do a long myotomy, using laparoscopy through the abdomen to do the proximal myotomy as high as possible, since DES often involves distal > proximal esophagus; so doing it through the hiatus is enough; then add Dor antireflux.
  8. A few cases with the spastic part high up: use VATS to help do the myotomy in the chest.
  9. The author thinks POEM for DES should work, since myotomy can reach high; Shackelford 2019 agrees: POEM in DES patients, results at 1 year improved, chest pain less, Eckardt score less than 3; SAGES 2018: the longest follow-up, 5 years, still had symptomatic success (wait for standard textbooks to approve).

### Nutcracker esophagus (NE) (p.741)
- Also called hypercontracting esophagus: normal peristalsis, but each contraction squeezes very hard (hypertensive peristalsis); measured pressure during contraction >180 mmHg; with HRM DCI is higher than 5,000 mmHg/s/min.
- Symptom: chest pain is the prominent symptom.
- Normal peristalsis and LES relaxation, LES normal or slightly high.
- The word nutcracker is the original term from each squeeze of the esophageal muscle, firm like cracking a walnut. (Figures: nutcracker; HRM; pressure tracings.)
- Treatment, along DES lines: main treatment is drugs plus endoscopy and dilatation:
  1. These patients are often more neurotic than DES because the chest pain is severe; reassure that it is not heart disease, and that such contraction is not cancer.
  2. Avoid caffeine, cold and hot foods.
  3. See whether it is GERD; if with GERD, treat GERD, nutcracker improves (Schwartz stresses PPI).
  4. If still not better: try calcium channel blocker, antidepressant, botox, dilatation.
  5. If really not improving, end with long myotomy or POEM, but results are not as good as in DES patients; the author in Cameron says do not try too hard on these cases; the author advises to find rest or refer to another hospital.

### Jackhammer esophagus (JE) (p.741)
- Also called hypercontracting esophagus like nutcracker: normal peristalsis, but each contraction is very strong (hypertensive peristalsis); pressure during contraction >180 mmHg; with HRM DCI higher than 8,000 mmHg/s/min.
- Symptom: chest pain at swallowing is the prominent symptom; often dysphagia too.
- Treatment is along DES and NE; Shackelford 2019 seems to favor POEM.

### Vigorous achalasia (p.741)
- A motility disorder that is difficult: the LES does not relax like achalasia, but achalasia must have no peristalsis; vigorous is the opposite: much peristalsis, like DES.
- Symptoms: chest pain like DES and dysphagia like achalasia.
- Chicago classification puts it as type 3 achalasia.
- Treatment: the author knows only this much; Heller does not work well.

### Pseudoachalasia (p.742)
- Symptoms like achalasia but the LES that does not relax is due to cancer in the lower esophagus invading the inhibitory neurons of the myenteric plexus. (Figure: barium: long tapering; endoscopy.)
- Manometry gives the result like true achalasia.
- Distinguishing true from pseudoachalasia:

| | True achalasia | Pseudoachalasia |
|---|---|---|
| Age | 30-40 years | More than 50 years |
| Duration of symptom | More than 6 months | Less than 6 months |
| Weight loss | Chronic | Rapid, more than 20 pounds in 2-3 months |
| Distal esophageal tapering | 2-3 cm | More than 3.5 cm |

## Scleroderma (p.742-)
- A connective tissue disease affecting smooth muscle; besides scleroderma, SLE also can cause esophageal problems; classed in the hypotonic motility disorder group.
- Systemic disease with small blood vessel inflammation, reducing blood flow to muscle, causing muscle atrophy, replaced by fibrous tissue until the muscle has no force to squeeze; GI involvement in 40-45%, with 80% of those involving the esophagus.
- At the same time the muscle at the EGJ relaxes, LES pressure becomes so low it is almost unmeasurable, so acid refluxes up to touch the esophagus all the time, causing severe esophagitis, and the esophagus becomes shortened.
- Symptoms: clinical triad of dysphagia, heartburn, Raynaud's phenomenon.
- Barium swallow: typically dilated esophagus, stomach and duodenum with peristalsis of the esophagus above the aortic arch still present; this differs from achalasia because the LES stays open all the time, so barium passes easily into the stomach (except when stricture develops: then proximal dilatation with distal stricture).
- Manometric study: normal peristalsis of the striated muscle in the proximal part; the smooth muscle at the distal part usually has no peristalsis or very weak, and weak LES.
- Treatment: the indication for surgery is a problem from severe GERD (the scleroderma esophagus is open all the time, with fairly severe esophagitis, causing stricture and often short esophagus too).
  - Surgery is still usually through the abdomen; in some cases thoracotomy is needed because the esophagus is short and pulled into the chest. Short esophagus is fixed by Collis gastroplasty to create a "new esophagus" that is longer, then a Belsey as the antireflux procedure through the chest.
  - For those doing laparoscopy often: Collis is often done through the abdomen; they advise doing it through the abdomen, then using the lengthened portion to do a fundoplication.
  - Choice of fundoplication: if the esophagus is very floppy, do a Nissen fundoplication, tight; if only slightly floppy, choose Toupet. The operation is called laparoscopic Collis-Toupet or Collis-Nissen fundoplication. (Figures: Collis gastroplasty with 52-French bougie; Collis-Nissen fundoplication.)

## Hiatal hernia (p.745-)
- A defect where the esophageal hiatus is wide and the phrenoesophageal membrane that helps keep the EGJ in the abdomen is weakened or torn, so the abdominal esophagus and other organs in the abdomen gradually slide into the chest, which has negative pressure. 4 types (figure: normal anatomy; types I-IV):
  - Type 1: sliding hernia; the most common, 95%.
  - Type 2: paraesophageal or rolling hernia.
  - Type 3: type 1 + type 2 = combined type.
  - Type 4: type 3 + spleen or colon herniating up as well.
- Diagnosis by barium swallow or upper endoscopy to evaluate the GE junction and the size of the hernia.

### Type 1 or sliding hiatal hernia (p.746)
- The key feature: the EGJ slides up to lie in the chest; generally no symptoms, but if symptomatic they are the symptoms of GERD from a defective LES. (Figures: displaced GE junction, phrenoesophageal ligament, herniated stomach, diaphragm.)
- Treatment: if no symptoms, no surgery; but if GERD symptoms do not improve with PPI, or the patient must take it forever, surgery: open the phrenoesophageal ligament in front of the esophagus, blunt dissect around the esophagus and pull the EGJ down into the abdomen, do a crural repair, then do a floppy Nissen or a partial fundoplication, Dor or Toupet.

### Types 2, 3, 4: paraesophageal hiatal hernia (PEH) (p.746-748)
- Symptoms: from no symptoms to few symptoms to many; the commonly found symptoms are heartburn, dysphagia, regurgitation, nausea, vomiting; the severe problem is that these patients have organs in the abdomen, possibly stomach, spleen, or colon, small bowel, moving up into the chest, causing incarceration and strangulation.
- PEH is associated with anemia, a result of stomach twisting causing mucosal ischemia and ulceration (Cameron lesions). On barium swallow imaging you see the stomach twisted, with the greater curvature rotated up and the lesser curvature down: called an upside-down stomach. (Figure: large hiatal hernia.)
- Treatment of PEH: in the past, no observation because of fear of incarceration, strangulation, perforation, believed from old reports to be as high as 30%; so once diagnosed, if the patient is fit, advise surgery in all: explore laparotomy + reduction + crural repair + antireflux procedure; the operative method is as in the figure. (Figure: port placement, sac dissection, crural closure, mesh.)
- Current concept for PEH management has changed: incarceration is uncommon, not as terrifying as believed; the chance of emergency surgery is only 1.1%, and most PEH patients are old with many illnesses; so if found incidentally with no symptoms, advise observation and regular follow-up; this approach seems strongly recommended rather than operating on everyone.
- Indications for surgical repair:
  1. Medically refractory GERD.
  2. Obstructive symptoms such as dysphagia, regurgitation, early satiety, postprandial chest pain or abdominal pain.
  3. Mild symptoms but progression over time.
  4. Chronic anemia (Cameron erosion).
  5. Decreased cardiac function.
  6. Recurrent aspiration pneumonia.
  7. Shortness of breath or dyspnea on exertion.
  - The age of the patient is not a contraindication to surgery.
- Recent operative approach:
  1. Choose laparoscopic first, then open; open only for PEH type 4 with much herniated organ in the chest, or after a failed laparoscopic operation, especially cases that previously had mesh in the first operation, which creates many adhesions, or very obese patients.
  2. Complete reduction of the stomach and EGJ into the abdominal cavity without tension; need about 3-4 cm [of intra-abdominal esophagus], which often requires mediastinal dissection; after the hernia contents are back in the abdomen, excise the hernia sac completely.
  3. Preserve the anterior and posterior vagus nerves.
  4. Hiatal closure (crural repair): earlier conclusion was that synthetic non-absorbable mesh such as PTFE or prolene mesh should be placed to reduce tension, since primary tissue repair has recurrence up to 20-30%; but now there is an attempt not to use mesh, because of mesh problems, found in about 2%, namely mesh erosion that perforates into the esophageal lumen (may even lead to death) and scar and fibrosis shortening the esophagus causing dysphagia; so biologic resorbable mesh made from pig skin or small bowel submucosa is tried (still being studied) to reduce problems of non-absorbable mesh; but when biologic mesh dissolves, the problem of recurrence comes back. (Figures: mobilization and sac excision; mesh erosion into the GEJ.)
  - Treatment trend now swings back to tissue repair again, by seeking ways to reduce tension: the first way is an intentional pneumothorax to create a floppy diaphragm that relaxes while sewing so there is no tension (tension-free repair); another way is a diaphragmatic relaxing incision to reduce crural tension, with the same principle as the relaxing incision in groin hernia surgery: on the right make the incision in the diaphragm away from the esophageal hiatus, in line with the IVC; on the left, beside the heart; then place a PTFE mesh at the edge of the diaphragmatic incision. (Figure: right and left relaxing incisions, with mesh sewn in place.)
  5. Because GERD occurs after hernia repair in about 50%, a fundoplication should be done at once; the popular one is Nissen, but some do a partial fundoplication (Dor); this is a matter of each surgeon's preference.
  6. There is a concept that fundoplication is already the best gastropexy so gastropexy is unnecessary; but Cameron 2014, Sabiston 2017, Schwartz 2015 and Shackelford 2019 advise anterior gastropexy in selected cases to secure the stomach to the anterior abdominal wall, together with fundoplication as routine.
  7. In the past in cases with short esophagus, extensive mediastinal dissection to lengthen it was not enough; in such cases do a Collis gastroplasty to add length; the Collis technique that used to be advised through the chest is now all done through the transabdominal approach.

## Caustic injury (p.749-)
- In children it is mostly from accident, curiosity-driven sucking; in adults it is often from a suicide attempt, a quarrel with a friend, or true intent to kill oneself.
- Caustic agent: a substance with pH <=2 or >=11.5; at this pH it has corrosive action; agents are usually household products.
- Acid: causes coagulative necrosis (remember): forms an eschar covering the esophageal mucosa, limiting tissue penetration; also acid has a sour taste so if not intentional it is swallowed little because the patient spits it out; but if intentionally drunk, since acid is a watery liquid that passes through the esophagus quickly and flows into the stomach; when acid reaches the stomach it causes pyloric spasm so acid contacts the stomach for a long time; so injury of the stomach is rather severe, but the esophagus has less injury since its squamous epithelium resists acid well.
- Alkali: causes liquefaction necrosis (remember): severity of injury of the esophagus is very high, because alkali has no sour taste, does not irritate the mouth, so is swallowed in large amounts, and is rather sticky so flows down slowly, contacting the esophagus for a long time, whereas the stomach has little injury.
- If the % of caustic agent does not meet the threshold, and we are not sure whether the swallowed acid or alkali injured the esophagus, send for flexible upper endoscopy.

| Alkali burns | Acid burns |
|---|---|
| More esophageal injury | More gastric injury |
| Liquefactive necrosis -> deep | Coagulative necrosis -> limits penetration |
| No immediate burning sensation | Burning sensation in mouth |
| Larger quantities ingested | Small quantities can be ingested |
| Long term: up till 6 months damages are predicted | Within 48 hrs to which extent acid will injure can be determined |

### Pathological phases and clinical manifestations (p.750)
1. Acute or initial phase: seen after swallowing the caustic injury up to 4 days, some a little less than 72 hours; a period with inflammatory reaction; general symptoms: pain in the mouth or retrosternal pain, pain on swallowing, drooling because of inability to swallow saliva, maybe fever or vomiting; but if pain is severe, hypotension, metabolic acidosis: be careful, keep in mind the esophagus may have perforated; watch hoarseness, stridor; if the mouth shows much swelling of the tongue, beware airway problems.
2. Subacute or latent or quiescent or ulcerative phase: seen 4-5 days after, lasting about 10-12 days; some say longer than 72 hours up to 3 weeks; the most feared period because of sloughing and necrosis of tissue together with vascular thrombosis, making the mucosa ulcerate, and collagen has not yet come in; the esophagus is thus softest; one should not put a scope in during this period. In the subacute phase, if no esophageal perforation, the mouth and chest pain gradually decrease.
3. Cicatrization or scarring phase: found in the group where the acid or alkali penetrated deep into the muscle layers, with fibroblasts coming in, forming scar, causing dysphagia, usually beginning after swallowing the substance for about 3 weeks to 3 months, with 60% having difficulty swallowing within 1 month and 80% within 2 months; after 8 months have passed, if swallowing is still comfortable and can swallow saliva, a stricture is unlikely.

| Phase | Timing | Pathology | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute | Day 1-4 | Liquefactive necrosis; vascular thrombosis; progressive inflammation | Sloughing or ulceration may not be apparent during first 24 hr |
| Subacute | Day 5-14 | Sloughing of necrotic area; development of granulation tissue; appearance of fibroblasts | Esophageal wall is thinnest |
| Cicatrization | Day 15-3 mo | Proliferation of fibroblast; deposition of collagen | Reepithelialization complete 4 wks to 3 mos; stricture develops during this phase |

### Management (p.751-)
- Start with ATLS as for a trauma patient: look at the airway first. If a tube is needed, do not place a blind nasotracheal tube, since it may cause more injury; in an emergency case, look at the larynx: if the vocal cord or epiglottis is very swollen, cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy may be needed. (Figure: very swollen vocal cords.)
- Next, examine whether the patient really took a caustic agent: in the mouth there may be white patches in the cheek area or at the lips there may be burns, scald-like. (Figure: white deposits in the mouth and tongue.)
- Then check what substance was taken; remove clothes contaminated with the agent; allow the patient to rinse the mouth or wash skin that touched the caustic agent with clean water.
- Beware: 70% of patients with burns in the mouth have no esophageal injury, and 10-30% of patients with no burn in the mouth at all have esophageal injury.
- If the patient is stable with no indication for urgent operation, e.g. perforation, do a flexible esophagoscopy early to assess severity by grading injury, which can be used as a guide for further treatment.
  - If there is no scope or no one able to do it, there are 2 options: observe symptoms for 24 hours or refer. Generally wait about 12-24 hours to avoid gastric emptying problems, since scoping too early may stimulate vomiting. Endoscopy in children or uncooperative adults should be done under sedation, and should not be done after 48 hours because of the risk of perforation.
  - While passing the scope under direct vision, first look at the vocal cords for swelling; then advance the scope into the esophagus; once an esophageal injury is seen, usually if not an expert, a beginner should stop there; no need to push further (scope should not be introduced beyond the proximal esophageal lesion); avoid retroflexing the scope for fear of perforation.
  - Except: an expert may push down to look at the stomach and duodenum for injury; he is not afraid of perforation since in the past it was a rigid scope; with a small flexible scope this can be done safely; Shackelford 2019 says if the scoper is expert he should look at everything. For those who are not expert, if they push the whole scope down, grade the caustic injury: if the upper part shows no lesion or no more than grade 1 injury, go on; but if severity is greater, a beginner should not push the scope down.
  - A second scope may be needed if the first was incomplete, or scoping during surgery to plan how much to resect and what to remove.
- Contraindications to early esophagoscopy: 1. signs suggesting perforation or peritonitis; 2. airway obstruction; 3. caustic ingestion more than 48 hours earlier.

### Zargar endoscopic grading of caustic injury (p.752)
- Grades the severity of injury from endoscopic findings, comparable to burns of the skin.

| Zargar classification | Description |
|---|---|
| Grade 0 | Normal mucosa |
| Grade I | Edema and erythema of the mucosa |
| Grade IIA | Hemorrhage, erosions, blisters, superficial ulcers |
| Grade IIB | Circumferential lesions |
| Grade IIIA | Focal deep gray or brownish-black ulcers |
| Grade IIIB | Extensive deep gray or brownish-black ulcers |
| Grade IV | Perforation |
- Grade 0: no injury (normal examination).
- Grade 1: injury limited to the mucosa, with superficial mucosal hyperemia, patchy redness and slight edema; no ulcer yet at this grade, which separates it from grade 2. (Figure: grade 1 injury.)
- Grade 2: injury of the submucosa, deep into the muscle layer but not beyond it; bleb, bulging, or bleeding ulcers, superficial ulcers, often with an exudation patch covering.
  - 2A: superficial ulcer, may have bleb or non-circumferential ulcer, like a burn.
  - 2B: deep or circumferential ulcer but no necrosis, no black colour of dead tissue.
- Grade 3: injury to the outer layer of muscle; mucosal slough, deep ulcers, black like charcoal, may swell until the lumen is narrow, or bleeding and perforation can occur.
  - 3A: only focal necrosis, black-brown areas of necrosis, small patches, not wide.
  - 3B: jet black, wide areas of necrosis or extensive necrosis; high chance of perforation.
- Grade 4: perforation.
- Prognosis: 1. Grade 1-2A: prognosis good, no esophageal stricture; 2. Grade 2B-3A: prognosis still OK, stricture 70-100%; 3. Grade 3B: prognosis poor, mortality 50-65%, often needs surgery.

### Radiographic examination (p.754)
1. Plain film: in the early phase start with plain films such as neck and chest; 2. water-soluble contrast esophagogram: in cases suspected of perforation; 3. barium swallow: suitable for assessing stricture in later phase; 4. CT chest/abdomen.
- Role of CT: started to be used in caustic injury patients in whom scoping is unsuitable, and to assess severity better, because the endoscopic image does not show all layers of the esophagus, especially in grade 3B patients, so CT sees depth.
- Benefits of CT: 1. tells severity: swollen esophagus, soft tissue infiltration, air in or around the esophagus, mediastinal air = severe; 2. those with soft tissue infiltration, air around the esophagus: the esophagus will often stricture; 3. helps in deciding to resect the esophagus in grade 3B: CT can tell whether there is transmural necrosis of the esophagus; if so, esophagectomy is needed.
- Criteria of transmural necrosis: must be positive at least 2 of 3: 1. esophageal wall blurring (the esophageal wall is not clear); 2. periesophageal fat blurring (fat around the wall is not clear); 3. non-enhancement of the esophageal wall after IV contrast, or air in the wall.
- Because CT is noninvasive compared with endoscopy, a CT grading system has been proposed to replace Zargar in treatment planning (Chinese study, 2010): grades the CT finding into 4 grades (figure A-D = grade 1-4):

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| 1 | No definite swelling of the esophageal wall |
| 2 | Edematous wall thickening without periesophageal soft-tissue involvement |
| 3 | Edematous wall thickening with periesophageal soft-tissue infiltration + well-demarcated tissue interface |
| 4 | Edematous wall thickening with periesophageal soft-tissue infiltration + blurring of tissue interface or localized fluid collection around the esophagus or descending aorta |
- Because CT grading still has no RCT support, the conclusion is that CT grading is just an adjunct diagnostic investigation and cannot replace endoscopy.
- There is an attempt to use EUS with a miniprobe to assess depth of injury, but results are not better than CT, so it is not the standard.

### Acute phase treatment (p.755-756)
1. Grade 0: endoscopy shows nothing: do not scold the patient that they wasted the doctor's time; in theory can let eat and discharge, but in practice may admit 1-2 days to assess the mental state to see whether they will go jump off the bridge again; in Thailand the doctor cannot make errors; the author advises to treat grade 0 like grade 1.
2. Grade 1: if no pain, no nausea or vomiting, can swallow saliva with no pain, start water, followed by soft diet; IV fluid not necessary if able to eat; antibiotic not necessary; observe 24-48 hours; if no psychiatric problem discharge; for children, the author advises the mother be careful about swallowing acid or alkali again.
3. Grade 2A: NPO, IV, IV antibiotic, IV PPI, observe about 24-48 hours; treat like grade 1; if no problem, patient has swallowed saliva, no pain, no nausea, no vomiting, start water and soft diet; when able to eat and no psychiatric problem to consult, discharge and appoint follow-up 3 weeks after swallowing the caustic, for upper GI to look for stricture.
4. Grade 2B and 3A: admit ICU at once, NPO, IV, IV antibiotic, IV PPI, nutritional support (consider individually); observe about 1 week to see whether perforation; if not perforated, start feeding about 3 weeks after swallowing the caustic; send for UGI study, but nowadays endoscopy is used more. After discharge follow up monthly for 1 year; if stricture appears with dysphagia, consider early dilatation, but only after 3 weeks post-injury.
5. Grade 3B: treat like 2B and 3A at first; in 3B there is a chance of perforation always; when the patient arrives, advise CT chest and abdomen with IV + water-soluble esophagogram; if the wall is only swollen but still enhances, observe further; but if clinical deterioration, or CT shows air in the esophageal wall with no enhancement, showing muscle necrosis, operate at once. But the group of surgeons that believes in the Zargar view (strong): 3B has 85% chance of perforation, so they operate at once rather than wait for perforation then operate, since the chance of death is higher; they advise transhiatal esophagectomy (THE); the trend now seems to be toward operating; Shackelford 2019 also advises operating.
6. Grade 4: perforated: operate at once.

### Indications for emergency surgery (p.756)
1. Clinical mediastinitis or peritonitis (shows perforation); 2. uncontrollable upper GI bleeding, endoscopic treatment fails; 3. grade 3A with clinical deterioration, beginning organ failure; 4. CT consistent with transmural necrosis.

### Surgical technique (p.756-757)
1. Explore the abdomen first: if the stomach is perforated or has full-thickness transmural necrosis, meaning the serosa of the stomach looks discolored; the esophagus may also necrose together; to play safe do intraoperative esophagoscopy to assess esophageal mucosa; for necrotic stomach do wedge, partial or total gastrectomy according to the intraoperative finding in the OR.
2. After resecting the stomach, if the esophagus looks necrotic too, proceed to transhiatal esophagectomy (THE): so do THE when: 2.1 preoperative scope showed 3B lesion confirmed with CT of transmural necrosis; 2.2 if no scope before surgery (often an emergency laparotomy due to peritonitis), look at intraoperative findings; if the distal esophagus is dark, do THE.
3. If necrosis of the pancreas or duodenum is found, just debride; avoid doing a PD.
4. End with cervical esophagostomy together with a feeding jejunostomy.
5. Consult a psychiatrist; if the patient survives, meet again in 6 months to do reconstruction.
- Esophageal perforation from caustic injury should have esophagectomy by the transhiatal method together with cervical esophagostomy and feeding jejunostomy; if the patient does not die, meet again in 6 months for reconstruction.
- Reconstruction: right colon is preferred > left colon, as an isoperistaltic graft.
- For right colon, even if the blood supply seems better from the left side, the ileum is still of rather similar caliber to the esophagus; also there is an IC valve that helps prevent reflux; disadvantage: the right colon is big and fat, and may bulge... (the author's personal joke about the right colon, not study content).
- Laparoscopic surgery is not safe since this is an emergency operation that must be done quickly, and importantly the posterior wall of the stomach cannot be seen.

### Do's and don'ts in the acute phase (p.757)
- Must not do in acute phase (answer = wrong): 1. place NG tube for decompression, feeding or to prevent stricture; 2. gastric lavage; 3. induce vomiting, wash the throat to induce vomiting; 4. send for endoscopy during the subacute phase; 5. send for endoscopy again when signs of perforation or peritonitis exist.
- Things not to do since there is no evidence supporting benefit, and sometimes harm: 1. giving steroid to reduce stricture, injected with triamcinolone into the esophageal wall; but studies found it is not helpful, and rather harmful, since it masks complications such as sepsis; 2. rushing to place a stent to prevent stricture; 3. rushing early dilatation before 3 weeks; 4. giving penicillamine or antifibrotic agent; 5. neutralizing agent, e.g. alkali swallowed then give acid to neutralize; 6. giving milk or activated charcoal, which makes the mucosa difficult to see at endoscopy.
- Figure (algorithm): Caustic ingestion: severe injury -> ABC, upright chest x-ray, abdominal films: perforation -> emergency resection via thorax and/or abdominal approach; no perforation -> esophagogastroscopy +/- bronchoscopy. History of exposure: symptoms -> esophagoscopy; asymptomatic -> <24 hr stay, immediate discharge, follow-up. Injury grade: mild (grade I, grade IIA): 48 hr observation, NPO, advance diet as tolerated; full-thickness (IIB/III): antibiotics, +/- steroids, TPN, close observation for deterioration; worsening symptoms -> emergency resection.

## Management of esophageal stricture (p.758-759)
- Mucosal lesion or injury grade 1, 2A: should heal without complication.
- If injury is deep into the muscle (>2B) or a circumferential burn: stricture likely: 60% appear within 1 month, 80% within 2 months; if 1 year passes and the patient can still swallow, no stricture problem.
- Dilatation should begin after 3 weeks.
- Important before dilating: do a barium esophagogram first to see where the stricture is, how long, multiple sites or just one, and how the stomach is involved or not. (Figure: upper GI showing multiple strictures, fairly long.)
- Target: dilate up to 42-45 F or about 15 mm; but reaching 40 F is already fine, so the patient can swallow.
- Frequency: very frequent: appoint dilatation every 2 weeks; if the stricture is not very tight every 3-4 weeks; when the patient swallows comfortably, review every 1-3 months; dilating too often is not helpful since the inflammatory reaction comes back as a tighter stricture.
- Duration: dilate for 6-12 months; after 1 year, appoint surveillance endoscopy every 1-3 years for life because of risk of cancer.
- Each dilatation: expand about 3 mm, not too much at one point; if the stricture is tight, keep the dilator in place 15-20 minutes each time.
- After dilatation, observe symptoms and chest film each time; do not dilate and then send the patient home right away.
- 2 methods of dilating:
  1. Antegrade dilation: insert the dilator through the mouth from above downward, or pushing technique; if the stricture is tight use an endoscope to help; pass a guide wire first, then follow with dilators of several types: Bougie, Eder-Puestow, Hurst-Maloney (figure B), but the most popular is Savary-Gilliard (figure A), size 7-20 mm (21-60 F), and balloon catheter through the scope (figure C). (Figures: Savary dilator over guidewire, expanding the stricture, larger dilators.)
  2. Tucker retrograde dilation: the principle is to open the abdomen and make a gastrostomy; then swallow a string... [as printed: let the patient swallow a thread from the mouth with the help of an endoscope]; pass a guide wire into the stomach; then tie a thread to a larger-caliber dilator and the guide wire; pull the thread up toward the mouth; when dilating, tie the dilator with the thread then pull the dilator up from the gastrostomy to above.
- If dilatation fails: often a stricture longer than 2 cm, narrow and tortuous; in the past, send to the OR for resection; nowadays after dilatation, place a self-expanding stent for about 2-3 months; reports say patients can swallow up to 80% with good results in the early period; but whether long-term results are better is unreported; another method is endoscopic incisional therapy: send the scope in and cut the stricture with an electric knife or argon at the stricture site.
- Indication for surgery in stricture patients: 1. complications from dilatation, e.g. perforation; 2. failure of dilatation: dilated for a long time, 6 months, or at 1 year the patient still cannot reach 40 F; 3. patient wants surgery, tired of frequent dilatation, especially in children, who cooperate poorly with the doctor.
- Problem to plan: the esophagus must be resected (bypass or resect) since this prevents mucocele and later SCC; those who disagree say it has high operative mortality; currently the conclusion is to consider case by case; for the conduit, if possible choose stomach first unless it is unusable, which is often true; then choose colon.
- SCC in caustic injury patients usually arises on average 40 years after injury; dysphagia appears fast since there is already a stricture; with good surveillance cancer of the esophagus is detected early so prognosis is good, because submucosal lymphatic channels are often burned, so lymphatic spread is less.
- Management of gastric stricture: the patient has fullness and vomiting; if able to dilate, try balloon dilatation first; if no improvement, then operate.

## Esophageal perforation (p.759-)
### Etiology
1. Iatrogenic: from the doctor's hand, especially endoscopy or stent placement, the most common cause, 59%.
2. Trauma: usually penetrating injury of the cervical esophagus.
3. Postemetic or spontaneous or Boerhaave syndrome.
4. Foreign bodies, e.g. dentures, fish bones, or sharp objects swallowed such as pins.
5. Tumor. 6. Caustic injury.
7. Small ones, e.g. weight lifting, straining at stool, seizure.
- There is a report that someone straining during labor ruptured the esophagus; the same applies to doctors in remote areas studying for the board exam: advise to tell the patient to not strain too hard; otherwise a left thoracotomy for closure will be needed (the author's joke).

### Iatrogenic perforation (p.760)
- Figure: scope passes at the cricopharyngeal area together with a cervical spine osteophyte.
1. Occurs most often with upper endoscopy, with the scope pressed against a cervical spine osteophyte at the cricopharyngeal area; compare scope types: flexible : rigid = 0.03 : 0.11.
2. Other less common causes: surgery of the thyroid, tracheostomy, placing a tube, NG tube, Sengstaken-Blakemore tube.
3. Therapeutic endoscopy has 200 times the chance of perforation of diagnostic endoscopy.

| Procedure | Estimated risk of esophageal perforation |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic endoscopy with a flexible endoscope | 0.03% |
| Diagnostic endoscopy with a rigid endoscope | 0.11% |
| Stricture dilation | 0.09-2.2% |
| Sclerotherapy | 1-5% |
| Pneumatic dilation for achalasia | 2-6% |

### Boerhaave syndrome (p.760-761)
- Herman Boerhaave: Dutch doctor and scientist, regarded as the father of modern physiology; first described the case of Baron Jan von Wassenaer in 1724, an admiral who died following a gluttonous feast and subsequent regurgitation.
- Seen after violent vomiting while the upper esophageal sphincter is not open, causing a rapid, severe rise in intraluminal pressure until the esophagus ruptures; the hole is usually on the left side, about 2-3 cm above the EGJ, where there is only the mediastinal pleura covering the lower side and no aorta shielding, while the right side of the esophagus has the mediastinal pleura covering all around. (Figure: Mallory-Weiss laceration of mucosa and bleeding; intramural dissection with hematoma formation (Williams 1957); total rupture (Boerhaave 1724).)
- Treatment: left thoracotomy + suture the perforation, then find something to buttress it, called a buttressing repair, then drain adequately.

### Mortality rate (p.761)
1. If operated within 24 hours mortality about 7%; but if later than 24 hours mortality is up to 20%.
2. By site of perforation: thoracic 11-34%; abdominal 13-25%; neck 6%.
3. Boerhaave has high mortality since diagnosis is late (patients think it is abdominal pain from eating, or pancreatitis), whereas iatrogenic causes have lower mortality. Currently with many experienced doctors the overall mortality is about 10%.

### Clinical manifestations (p.761)
- Symptoms depend on the location of the perforation and the timing of diagnosis, late or early; the doctor should always think of it when a patient is being endoscoped and then has severe pain; blood tests show leukocytosis.
- The earliest and most prominent symptom: sudden severe pain; fever is often found after perforation about 2-3 hours; some have pleural effusion (think of it rather than pneumonia); when an ICD is placed, you may get food-like or saliva-like fluid.
- Cervical perforation: sudden severe neck pain in a case of difficult upper endoscopy, followed by fever, difficulty and pain on swallowing, stiff neck; neck crepitus may be palpable in 60%; seen after endoscopy, so the doctor suspects it.
- Thoracic perforation: sudden severe chest pain radiating to the back, followed by fever, rapid breathing, dyspnea, tachycardia, hypotension, vomiting blood in 30% of patients; neck crepitus from air passing up to the neck, or Hamman's sign: a systolic crunching sound at the apex related to myocardial contraction.
- Abdominal perforation: peritonitis: abdominal tenderness, rigid abdomen like PU perforation, and sepsis.

### Diagnostic studies (p.762)
- Start with plain film and water-soluble contrast swallowing; barium swallow is used less; nowadays CT and flexible endoscopy are used increasingly.
- Current concept: no single diagnostic study is most suitable; choose what to order case by case.
- Plain film: if suspecting cervical perforation send a lateral neck film; if suspecting chest or abdomen send chest AP/lateral upright and an acute abdomen series as the first step, since easy; findings: air in soft tissue, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, widening of the mediastinum, pneumomediastinum, mediastinal fluid. (Figures: subcutaneous emphysema; retropharyngeal air.)
- Water-soluble contrast swallowing under fluoroscopy: when plain film is abnormal or suspicious that the esophagus may have perforated, send water-soluble contrast to confirm and locate the leak: positive result 50% in neck perforation, in the chest about 80%.
  - While swallowing contrast, take 2 positions: upright or semierect with right and left lateral decubitus; in the upright position contrast passes quickly and may not show the perforation; Schwartz 2015 advises the decubitus view.
  - Advantages: 1. if it leaks into the mediastinum, not much problem of chemical mediastinitis; 2. good reabsorption; repeat film when needed.
  - Disadvantages: 1. sensitivity: image is less clear than barium; 2. it is hypotonic, so if aspirated into the lungs it may cause pulmonary edema or necrotizing pneumonitis.
- Barium swallowing: thin barium has a better positive result than water-soluble: cervical perforation positive about 60%, chest about 90%.
- Advantages of barium: 1. clearer and more sensitive than water-soluble contrast; 2. aspiration does not cause pulmonary edema.
- Disadvantages: 1. very irritating, especially a leak in the chest causes severe fibrosing mediastinitis; 2. barium remains for a long time, so follow-up or repeat film cannot be done.
- Problem with contrast swallow: the patient must be able to cooperate and swallow; for those with an ET tube, put contrast through a nasopharyngeal tube; and a negative result does not rule out perforation (false negative 20-30%).

### CT chest followed by water-soluble esophagography (p.763)
- In the past CT was not routine because it localizes the perforation no better than water-soluble or barium swallow; now CT has become extremely useful for diagnosis and treatment planning.
- CT is suitable when the other tests are unclear, the patient is not alert or intubated on a ventilator or cannot swallow contrast (place an NG tube under fluoroscopy then feed contrast), or when a collection is suspected; if there is a collection it is a guide for intervention.
- Positive CT findings: mediastinal or extraluminal air/fluid collection, esophageal thickening, pleural effusion, etc. (Figure: CT cervical perforation.)
- Limitation of CT: it tells the location of the perforation and concomitant esophageal pathology only imprecisely.

### Flexible esophagoscopy (p.763)
- In the past absolutely forbidden because air pumping during scoping worsens soilage by contents; now not forbidden: when a patient is suspected of perforation but water-soluble or barium swallow shows no hole, an experienced endoscopist may scope, insufflating as little as possible, with an ET tube and general anesthesia, in the OR; if worried about pneumothorax, place a prophylactic ICD. (Figure: tear in esophagus.)
- Advantages: from the scope we can tell the location and size of the perforation, tissue viability and extent of mucosal injury, concomitant disease such as cancer or stricture; also used in cases treated through the scope with a clip or stent covering the hole.
- Limitations: invasive, needs anesthesia or sedation, and problems from air insufflation.
- Summary: if not necessary do not scope (the author's advice to residents, except experts).

## Treatment of esophageal perforation (p.764-)
- 5 main principles: 1. stabilize the patient; 2. debride all infected tissue; 3. close the perforation to prevent further contamination; 4. wide drainage; 5. maintain GI continuity.
- General treatment: 1. NPO, IV, IV antibiotics, IV PPI, oxygen, foley, CVP assessment; 2. nutritional support; 3. almost 100% need surgery; 4. if thoracic esophageal perforation place an ICD at once (may need to place on both sides).
- Two approaches: operate or not.
- To choose non-operative management the patient must be selected carefully: 1. all indications for non-operative treatment must be met; 2. usually a patient whose perforation is caused by the doctor's endoscopy or dilatation, as the patient was NPO so little contamination; 3. treatment: NPO, IV, IV antibiotic, PPI, improve nutrition, and closely observe often; 4. in cases perforated by endoscopy such as dilatation or removing a foreign body with little contamination, may try endoscopic treatment with clipping or stent to convert free perforation into contained perforation (highly selected cases); 5. if the patient is stable, before feeding send for water-soluble contrast on day 3-5; if no leak start oral feeding (Schwartz 9th: NPO 7-14 days); 6. if symptoms worsen with sepsis or mediastinitis, rush to emergency operation at once.
- Indications for non-operative management: 1. diagnosed early, symptoms not severe; 2. no evidence of sepsis from mediastinitis (HR <100, normotension, WBC <12,000); 3. usually a patient whose esophagus was perforated by a doctor's endoscopy since NPO; 4. contamination limited to a contained perforation in the mediastinum or between the mediastinal pleura and visceral pleura, and importantly the content can flow back into the esophagus well; a free perforation needs surgery; 5. the surgeon looking after the patient must understand the esophageal perforation well; 6. the surgeon should be able to operate on the esophagus with confidence and skill, not shy away; 7. Griffin's added criteria: 7.1 no underlying esophageal disease or loss of tissue and no distal obstruction; 7.2 not perforated by caustic injury; 7.3 should be cervical perforation, not used in abdominal perforation; 7.4 the hospital capability must be good enough to give intensive care, with enough doctors on all teams to pick from.
- Even with selection this fine, there is still failure in about 20% that must go to the OR.

### Surgical approach (p.765-767)
- Do not place an NG tube before anesthesia.
- Considerations in choosing the surgical method: 1. patient condition: still stable or sepsis or shock; 2. esophageal tissue: still good or inflamed; 3. time from perforation to surgery: is there much mediastinal infection; 4. cause of perforation and the underlying disease, e.g. perforation in achalasia: besides closing the perforation, do a myotomy to fix the main problem of obstruction at the EGJ at the same time.
- Incision:
  1. Cervical esophagus: left cervical neck incision along the anterior border of SCM, long from the cricoid cartilage to the sternal notch. Go in to the thyroid then mobilize the thyroid, usually needing to tie the middle thyroid vein, pull the thyroid medially, and reach the esophagus through the trachea-esophageal groove; beware injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve here. (Figures: left cervical incision; mobilization of left lobe of the thyroid.)
     - Feel the cervical spine; the esophagus lies right on the spine, or when starting anesthesia put in an NG tube then palpate the NG tube. If the hole is found, close it; if not found, no matter, because what matters for treating the perforation is adequate drainage; that alone lets the hole close.
     - Drain placement: open the prevertebral fascia, wash the perforation site clean; then place a penrose drain 2 strands along the length of the esophagus, letting the drain exit under the incision. (Figures: bulge created by diverticulum; closure.)
  2. Thoracic esophagus: 2.1 upper 2/3: right posterolateral thoracotomy through the 5th space; 2.2 lower 1/3: left posterolateral thoracotomy through the 6th or 7th space.
  3. Abdominal esophagus: upper midline, close the hole and wrap the stomach.
- Perforation not more than 24 hours: the tissue is usually still good, contamination and inflammation little, patient hemodynamically stable: primary closure is the best, with little chance of leak.
- Perforation more than 24 hours: tissue is usually friable, contamination and inflammation heavy: primary closure alone has leak chance not less than 20-50% (average 39%); may need diversion + exclusion, or a T-tube placed into the perforation, or even esophagectomy.

### Technique of closure of thoracic esophageal perforation (p.766-767)
1. Currently timing no longer matters: even if more than 24 hours, if the tissue is closable, not friable and not too inflamed, advise primary closure of the perforation, then find vascularized tissue to reinforce, called buttressing or reinforced repair, suited to perforation in the chest and abdomen; it reduces leak rate from 39% to 13%; the issue of whether reinforced repair is necessary in every case is starting to be debated, but the author still does it. (Figure: extent of mucosal tear, pouting mucosa, normal submucosa; pleural flap.)
2. Debride the wound clean, cut away all necrotic tissue, open the mediastinal pleura along the length of the esophagus; when the hole is found, do a myotomy at both ends of the wound so the mucosa of the whole tear is seen; close the perforation mucosa to mucosa without tension.
3. Close in one layer or 2 layers: if 2 layers, interrupted: inner layer Vicryl 3/0, outer layer silk 3/0.
4. Tissue used for reinforcing has many choices, e.g. pleura, pericardium, omentum, fundus; the author likes the intercostal muscle flap, a vascular flap that is thick and holds sutures well, does not cut through when tied. (Figure: A pedicled pericardial fat graft; B gastric patch; C pleural flap; D intercostal muscle bundle graft; E omental onlay graft (subdiaphragmatic); F diaphragmatic pedicle graft.)
5. Late case: delayed more than 24 hours, tissue is often friable; even with buttressing repair there is still a chance of leak; before, diversion and exclusion were advised as protective procedures; currently this method is losing popularity since results are no better than inserting a T-tube into the perforation, and it also needs another operation to close the esophagostomy and fix the EG junction; this issue is worth noting because in standard textbooks almost every book still advises diversion and exclusion (the author still does it).
6. If the perforation is large, inflamed, so that after closure the sutures cut through, or the esophageal wall is very friable, especially a perforation of more than 50% of the circumference or longer than 3 cm: do not close; place a T-tube size 16 F into the perforation to make a controlled fistula; find good tissue to wrap around the T-tube; place 2 chest tubes. (Figure: aorta, chest wall and ribs; T-tube inside esophageal lumen; general pleural drain; drain to tear.) After surgery NPO; keep the T-tube in; after 1-2 weeks swallow water-soluble contrast; if no leak allow oral feeding; keep the T-tube about 6 weeks, then pull out; this is now used more than diversion + exclusion.
7. Must have mediastinal and pleural drainage.
8. Must place a feeding jejunostomy.
9. Consideration of reconstruction after about 6 months.
10. But if the esophagus is necrotic, inflamed, or friable, or has a disease below the perforation, esophagectomy may be needed.

### Diversion and exclusion technique (p.768-769)
1. Diversion: purpose: prevent saliva from flowing down, with an esophagostomy, then use a large-caliber absorbable (Vicryl or Dexon) tied around the lower end as a banding.
2. Exclusion: purpose is to prevent acid reflux flowing up: close the cardia by cutting and closing, or banding the cardia, or a noncutting stapler across the cardia, together with a gastrostomy for gastric decompression and reducing acid reflux.
- In standard textbooks most advise diversion & exclusion in cases with perforation delayed more than 24 hours.
- 3 types of esophagostomy:
  1. End esophagostomy: preferred as proximal diversion: leave as much esophageal length as possible, tunnel and open the end at the upper chest. (Figure.)
  2. Cervical loop esophagostomy or Menguy technique with distal banding with a large absorbable thread; easy to do, like a loop colostomy, when closing it is not like the end type. (Figure: loop esophagostomy: cervical esophagostomy, esophageal band.)
  3. Tube esophagostomy: put a T-tube or Malecot tube into the hole to make an ostomy: also called the Ergin technique.
- For repair in an early case: after surgery NPO about 7 days before feeding; swallow contrast first; if no leak allow eating; if leak, keep NPO and start enteral feeding; if fever or sepsis, CT to look for an undrained collection.

### Minimally invasive treatment (p.769-770)
- New approach but not standard treatment; several types:
1. Thoracoscopic repair (VATS): used little; suited to cases with a small perforation not more than 1 cm and very little contamination; if the hole is larger than 1 cm or there is much contamination, place a T-tube into the perforation; the person doing MIS must be skillful, doing many cases; if experience is little, or the surgeon is not skilled, open is advised, since through the scope the closure may not be complete and drainage not adequate; leak could even cause death.
2. Endoscopic repair (figure: Ovesco clip): with clip or stent: suitable for perforation found while endoscopy is being done (iatrogenic perforation): if the hole is 1.5 cm but not more than 2 cm and a clean perforation, use an easy clip or ovesco clip to close the hole; for patients whose hole is larger than 2 cm or who were diagnosed after delay (72 hours or longer), or poor general condition with high operative risk (e.g. cirrhosis with ascites, portal hypertension), place a stent and leave it about 4-6 weeks, then remove.
   - Stent issue: the location of the perforation must not be near the neck or EG junction, and the esophagus must not be too wide; so not suitable for achalasia; the usual choice is a self-expanding plastic stent longer than the perforation by at least 3-4 cm; a plastic stent, like a covered stent, has no tissue ingrowth problem; when no longer needed, scope and pull the stent out.
   - Recent reports: stent placement for leakage after esophageal surgery and sleeve gastrectomy at the staple line succeeded in 50-85%; the disadvantage or major complication is stent migration.
- Indication for endoscopic treatment: 1. diagnosis made during the offending procedure; 2. poor surgical risk with perforation in benign disease; 3. late diagnosis precluding surgical repair; 4. perforation at the site of esophageal carcinoma; 5. anastomotic leakage.
- Update: endoscopic vacuum therapy (EVT) using the vacuum therapy principle of skin and soft tissue wounds: for cervical or EGJ leaks, all can use EVT; promising for the future. (Figures: endoscopic vacuum therapy steps: wound cavity, overtube, polyurethane foam sponge, drainage tube; NG tube, sponge, abscess cavity, vacuum canister; see esophageal cancer post-esophagectomy anastomotic leakage.)

### Esophageal perforation associated with distal pathology (p.771)
- Upper part perforation, lower part obstructed: close the perforation and fix the lower part obstruction; if the distal obstruction remains, a leak is certain.
- If the patient is unstable or highest risk: maybe only a stent or diversion & exclusion while waiting for time.
- Esophageal cancer: if the patient is in good condition and perforation occurred within 6-8 hours, do esophagogastrectomy; if the patient is not fit, insert a T-tube to drain the perforation together with diversion and exclusion; or worst condition, weak, or spread far: place a covered stent to close it.
- Achalasia: often occurs during pneumatic dilatation then perforation; besides closing the perforation, do a contralateral myotomy and partial wrap; but if the esophagus is out of condition, e.g. megaesophagus, in a patient fit enough, esophagectomy should be done.
- Benign stricture, e.g. after myotomy in achalasia with stricture from reflux, usually short segment, treated by dilatation, but dilating can perforate the esophagus: 2 approaches: 1. if it can still be dilated, repair the perforation and then appoint dilatation periodically; 2. if the stricture is tight, non-dilatable, and the patient condition is fairly good, do esophagectomy.

## Non-reflux esophagitis (p.771)
- A group with symptoms of dysphagia, odynophagia, chest pain, cough, hoarseness; many were treated as GERD or even had antireflux surgery with no improvement; when worked up, the esophagus is inflamed, not from acid reflux.
- Esophagitis in this group has many diseases, most being medical diseases; know a few to distinguish diseases:
  1. Eosinophilic esophagitis; 2. Candida esophagitis; 3. Drug-induced esophagitis; 4. Radiation esophagitis.

### Eosinophilic esophagitis (p.771-772)
- A disease Schwartz 2015 promoted so that it is even on the cover; so must know a little.
- The cause of inflammation is still unclear, but autoimmune and food allergy are suspected to be strongly involved. (Figure: eosinophils invade the epithelium of the esophagus possibly in response to allergens in food and the air, in a process mediated by type 2 helper T cells which release IL-5, IL-13 and eotaxin-3; barium studies may show focal narrowing and subtle concentric rings (trachealization); endoscopy may reveal mucosal fragility, rings, strictures, linear furrows and a narrow caliber.)
- Symptoms: postprandial chest pain with dysphagia, often with solid food, often confusing, thought to be GERD, but PPI does not improve it.
- Investigation:
  1. In the past barium swallow showed esophagus ring-shaped (classic ringed or felcine esophagus); now ACG diagnostic criteria say do not use.
  2. EGD: must do: the esophageal mucosa is seen as multiple concentric rings, or a series of stacked mucosal rings, or an appearance like the trachea rings (trachealization), found in 95%.
  3. Biopsy shows eosinophils at the base of the epithelium >15/HPF. (Figure: basal zone hyperplasia; superficial layering of eosinophils.)
- Treatment:
  1. First line: try PPI for 2 months (strangely some respond to PPI) together with swallowed topical steroid (aerosolized fluticasone); if not improved use systemic steroid.
  2. Second line: dirt therapy (as printed: elimination diet): test for what allergens then avoid them.
  3. Third line: dilatation to relieve dysphagia from severe fibrostenotic complication; beware the surgeon's role: dilating can perforate.

### Candida esophagitis (p.773)
- One of the infectious esophagitis group (written briefly since few students ask).
- Seen in immunocompromised patients, e.g. HIV, steroid use, hematologic malignancy.
- Prominent symptom: odynophagia (painful swallowing); if the mouth shows thrush; at scope, white or yellow plaque-like lesions of the mucosa; biopsy not necessary. (Figure: white plaque.)
- Treatment: systemic antifungal; symptoms improve in 3-5 days; then continue the drug another 2-3 weeks.

### Drug-induced esophagitis (p.773)
- Over a hundred drugs can cause esophagitis; the common ones: NSAIDs, tetracycline, vitamin C, potassium chloride tablets, which when in contact with mucosa at sites where the esophagus is narrowed or at a diseased narrowing, injure it.
- Symptoms: chest pain, odynophagia, dysphagia.
- Endoscopy: ulcer, erosion or bleeding.
- Treatment: just stop the offending drug; symptoms disappear within 7-10 days; in a case that bleeds and does not stop, stop bleeding through the scope; if stricture, dilate.

### Radiation esophagitis (p.773)
- Seen often in patients who had RT to head, neck and chest; inflammation depends on the dose.
- Mucosa swells, inflames, erodes, with microvascular damage followed by obliterative endarteritis.
- Symptoms: odynophagia, dysphagia, chest pain.
- Treatment: if just swelling or inflammation, not much pain, give liquid or soft diet, local anesthetic; if severe with ulcer or bleeding, admit and feed through a jejunostomy tube or gastrostomy tube; in the long term, if stricture arises, dilate with good results.

## Esophageal diverticulum (p.773-774)
- A disease associated with esophageal motility disorder.
- Found often in older people with comorbidities; common locations, 3 sites:
  1. Upper, at the neck: Zenker diverticulum: found in 70%.
  2. Body or mid-thoracic diverticulum: found in 25%.
  3. Lower or epiphrenic diverticulum: found in 5%.
- (Figure: pharyngoesophageal diverticulum (Zenker), traction diverticulum (midthoracic, azygos vein), epiphrenic diverticulum viewed from right side; barium images.)
- In general they are false diverticula, except the traction diverticulum, the only true diverticulum, found at the body; traction diverticulum is a consequence of mediastinal lymphadenitis, e.g. from TB, then inflammation around the esophagus; the scar that forms pulls the wall of the esophagus, all layers, out as a pouch; so traction diverticulum is a true diverticulum like Meckel's diverticulum.
- Most esophageal diverticula are found incidentally or during workup for motility disorder.
- Principles of treatment: 1. if no symptoms at all, follow up only; 2. but if symptomatic, it is usually from an underlying motility disorder, so the surgery must include myotomy; 3. size of the pouch is not an indication for surgery.

### Zenker's diverticulum (p.775-777)
- The first to report was Frederick von Zenker, who reported 27 patients with pharyngoesophageal diverticulum in 1877; the most common type of esophageal diverticulum, resulting from incoordination of esophageal motility; found 60-65%; it is a false or pulsion diverticulum (not traction): the mucosa and submucosa herniate out, directly at the posterolateral wall of the neck above the cricopharyngeus muscle, the weakest point; triangular area called Killian's triangle. (Figure: muscular architecture of upper esophageal sphincter: inferior constrictor, raphe, cricopharyngeal muscle, Killian's and Laimer's triangles, longitudinal muscle of esophagus; sequence of pouch formation above the cricopharyngeus.)
- A motility disorder is always associated; found in men about 3 times more than women; age usually over 70 years.
- In this disease the pharyngeal muscle and UES do not work in coordination: when swallowing, the muscle contracts, pushing pressure but the sphincter does not open to let food pass.
- Symptoms: small ones usually have no symptoms until large; most patients have symptoms, the classic: dysphagia with gurgling sound at the neck, or regurgitation of food retained in the diverticulum, a lump at the side of the neck, halitosis, bad breath, some with cough.
- Most serious complication: aspiration pneumonia and lung abscess.
- Best diagnosis: video barium swallow shows a diverticulum as an outpouching above the cricopharyngeus or upper esophageal sphincter, at the posterior wall of the neck; endoscopy if possible, to see the pouch and its neck, besides excluding other esophageal diseases above. (Figure: barium swallow showing diverticulum at the neck.)
- Treatment: operate in a symptomatic case no matter what size (most patients have symptoms):
  1. The heart of treatment is to correct the motility disorder by an open cricopharyngeal myotomy: vertical incision at the esophagus about 3 cm both above and below the diverticulum. (Figure: myotomy.)
  2. If the diverticulum is larger than 3 cm, add a diverticulectomy; if smaller than 1 cm, myotomy alone is enough; for 1-3 cm the author does not say but thinks it should be removed.
  3. Poor risk: may do only diverticulopexy: suture the bottom of the sac, hang it up at the top, or upside down, to prevent food from being retained in the sac, so the patient can lie flat without regurgitating. (Figure: diverticulopexy.)
  4. At large centers, transoral endoscopic stapled diverticulotomy or endoscopic Dohlman procedure: put an endostapler through a Weerda scope to cut the septum between the septum and esophageal lumen, accepted as the standard treatment, which looks easy and little pain, especially for Zenker diverticulum of size >=2 cm; some say 3 cm up. Shackelford 2019 advises that if Zenker is larger than 2 cm, do it by scope because instruments can be placed to cut in the pouch, and endoscopic surgery has lower mortality and morbidity than open surgery (remember); Schwartz 2015 says size only 2 cm should already use the endoscopic cutting; but if the size is less than 2 cm, or larger than 8 cm, this method should not be used; open is better. (Figures: Weerda scope; septum, sphincter, upper blade, lower blade, diverticulum.)
  5. Pharmacotherapy or botox injection does not help much; dilatation is wrong.
- Between open and endoscopic surgery choose by the risk of the patient: if low risk, choose open since myotomy can be adequate; if high risk, choose endoscopic.

### Midesophageal diverticulum (p.777)
- Traction diverticulum is a true diverticulum from mediastinal inflammation, while pulsion type is a false diverticulum from motility disorder.
- Location is usually within 4-5 cm of the tracheal carina.
- Investigation like epiphrenic diverticulum but should add CT chest, especially in patients with chronic cough, to evaluate mediastinal lymphadenopathy and rule out a small fistula, so bronchoscopy needed too.
- Most have no symptoms, diagnosed from barium swallow for investigation of motility disorder.
- Treatment: only in patients with symptoms, by right thoracotomy through the 5th space: suspended diverticulum + myotomy; only suture and hang up as in diverticulopexy without cutting, because if leak, the whole chest has pus and the patient dies; except when a fistula has formed, then the diverticulum must be removed.

### Epiphrenic diverticulum (p.777-778)
- A false diverticulum above the diaphragm; most have no symptoms, found incidentally on imaging; but if symptomatic, they are symptoms of motility disorder (achalasia, DES).
- Diagnosis: video barium swallow, upper endoscopy to help rule out cancer, and manometric study; beware of pitfalls in exams: normal chest x-ray; and do HRM to define the underlying motility disorder and to guide the length of the myotomy.
- Also should send CT to see how high the upper border of the diverticulum goes: if up to the inferior pulmonary vein, a transabdominal resection may not be enough and VATS is needed to assist.
- Treatment is still not conclusive, but most believe that if no symptoms or few symptoms, regular follow-up. Indications for surgery: 1. symptomatic; 2. some say even without symptoms, if larger than 5 cm, symptoms usually appear, so operate.
- Operation (figures: laparoscopic diverticulectomy; contralateral myotomy; relation of the pulmonary vein to the esophagus):
  1. Laparoscopic diverticulectomy together with contralateral esophageal myotomy; except when the diverticulum reaches as high as the inferior pulmonary vein, use a combined VATS-laparoscopic approach.
  2. For epiphrenic diverticulum, add an antireflux procedure, i.e. a partial fundoplication such as Dor.

## Vascular ring (p.778-779)
- The name suggests a vessel disease, but the symptoms are dysphagia and difficulty breathing; may be found from birth or discovered in adult life.
- Cause: some abnormality of the aortic arch vessels from birth, which wrap around or compress the esophagus and trachea: 1. double aortic arch (the most common; figure); 2. right-sided aorta; 3. retroesophageal right subclavian artery (figure).
- Investigation: 1. barium swallow shows esophageal obstruction at the site of the aortic arch; 2. then confirm with CT-A or MR-A.
- Treatment: if no symptoms or few symptoms, no need to treat; severe cases need surgery to fix the abnormality found, e.g. double aortic arch: division and revascularization. (Figure: small anterior arch divided; liberated trachea and esophagus; left common carotid, left subclavian artery.)

## Esophageal web (p.779-780)
- Patients present with dysphagia; diagnosis by endoscopy and barium swallow; generally 2 sites: upper and lower web.

### Upper esophageal web
- A submucosal circumferential ring at the upper esophagus; often one of the findings in a syndrome (sideropenic dysphagia), but most know it as Plummer-Vinson syndrome. (Figures: koilonychia, glossitis, upper esophageal web; barium swallow showing classic hypopharyngeal webs.)
- Found in women with poor nutrition, age >40 years.
- Besides dysphagia from the web there is chronic iron deficiency and chronic atrophic oral mucosa, with glossitis as a companion.
- The nail abnormality is spoon-shaped nails or koilonychia with brittle nails.
- 10% of patients have associated SCC at the oral cavity, hypopharynx and esophagus.
- Treatment: 1. dilation to rupture the web together with treating GERD; 2. give iron, FeSO4; 3. surveillance for oral cancer.

### Lower esophageal web
- A submucosal circumferential ring at the lower esophagus at the squamocolumnar junction.
- Old name: Schatzki ring. (Figures: endoscopy; barium.)
- Associated with sliding hiatal hernia and GERD.
- No one knows the pathogenesis or whether it carries cancer risk.
- Symptoms: most have none until the hole is smaller than 20 mm, then dysphagia begins; the classic symptom is meat dysphagia: eating steak, a big piece of meat, swallowing it gets stuck at the web, lower down.
- Treatment: no symptoms: observe; treat only symptomatic cases with dysphagia: 1. dilation is the treatment of choice; 2. if there is an underlying GERD problem, treat GERD too.

## Submucosal tumors (p.780)
- About 50% have no symptoms and are found incidentally from imaging or endoscopy.
- Many types, but know leiomyoma, GIST, polyp and duplication cyst.

### Leiomyoma (p.780-782)
- The most common benign esophageal tumor (60%), but only 0.5-1% of esophageal tumors.
- Male : female = 2 : 1, average age 38 years (remember).
- A submucosal mass often at the lower esophagus (lower 2/3), usually an incidental finding on EGD, with few symptoms unless the mass is large enough to cause dysphagia; believed to have no malignant transformation; point 3 is worth some attention because it is asked fairly often, both diagnosis and treatment.
- Diagnosis (figures: barium; endoscopy; EUS):
  1. Barium swallow: the most useful diagnosis; classic smooth mucosa, semilunar shape, and moves with swallowing, with no involvement of the mucosa, so can be separated from cancer.
  2. Upper endoscopy: should be done in every case: mobile, bulging submucosal lesion with intact mucosa is enough for diagnosis; in the past biopsy was forbidden because it makes later enucleation difficult and also may bleed or perforate; but currently this issue is debated again since EUS-guided FNA biopsy can be done safely, with sensitivity 95% to 100%, giving a definite answer what the mass is.
  3. EUS: helps confirm the mass originates from the submucosa layer, clear margin, no node enlargement.
  4. CT: not necessary routinely, but if sent it shows a hypodense mass.
- Indications for surgery: 1. symptomatic; 2. increase in size; 3. mucosal ulceration; 4. rule out malignancy.
- Treatment:
  1. Size <2 cm and no symptoms: currently debated whether to operate or surveillance follow-up every 6 months with endoscopy + EUS for 2 years; but the trend favors operating.
  2. Size 3-5 cm: use MIS with VATS, or if the mass is near the EGJ use laparoscopic extramucosal enucleation to remove the mass through the muscle layer without perforating the mucosa.
  3. Size >5-8 cm: try putting a scope in first; if doable, enucleation using the scope; if not, open thoracotomy.
  4. Size >8 cm or annular character: should open thoracotomy, with a chance of esophagectomy. (Figures: leiomyoma specimen; barium; enucleation through muscle then muscle closure.)
- New trend: endoscopic surgery has started to remove the mass using EGD, whether EMR or ESD, opening the mucosa then scooping the mass out; it can be done, and follow-up at 12 months shows no recurrence; with enough numbers and long-term follow-up the conclusion should come. (Figure: EMR of submucosal tumor.)
- The newest, reported in 2017 and mentioned in Shackelford 2019: removing the mass by submucosal tunnel endoscopic resection (STER), using the same principle as POEM but scooping the tumor out instead; advantage: the esophageal mucosa defect can be closed, not left open, less bleeding, heals quickly. (Figures: STER procedure.)

### Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) (p.782)
- Found less than leiomyoma; patients are usually over 60 years, and rarely under 30.
- Presentation like leiomyoma: small mass, no symptoms; large mass, dysphagia.
- Endoscopic EUS-guided FNA is very useful for diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and tells the risk of recurrence, which guides treatment (read more in the GIST chapter).
- Principle of surgery for GIST of the esophagus: must resect to a histology-free margin; no need to take nodes; organ-sparing surgery, especially at the GEJ, avoiding esophagectomy.
- Approach: MIS can be used for a low-risk GIST; but a high-risk GIST should have open wide local excision.

### Esophageal fibrovascular polyp (p.783)
- Usually seen in men aged about 60, at the cervical esophagus; the mass gradually grows until dysphagia and problems with breathing arise. (Figure: a giant polyp regurgitated out of the mouth.)
- A very large mass may even be regurgitated out of the mouth.
- Diagnosis: advise operating on all, because it grows and surely causes symptoms.
- Treatment: 1. mass <2 cm and little blood supply at the stalk: ligation lets it fall off by itself; 2. mass >2 cm to 8 cm: endoscopic resection; 3. mass >8 cm or with a rather large vascular supply at the pedicle: open resection through a lateral neck approach, open the esophagus, cut the polyp off.

### Esophageal duplication cyst (p.784)
- A congenital lesion arising in the embryo at about 6-8 weeks, when the esophagus begins to form as a primitive tube; epithelium grows, forming vacuoles that release secretion as the lumen forms; the vacuoles then disappear, but if they persist, the vacuole keeps growing and becomes a cyst. (Figures: endoscopy; specimen.)
- 80% have no communication with the esophageal lumen, while 20% do.
- Symptoms are like a small leiomyoma, usually none, except a large one, which causes dysphagia.
- Most are found incidentally on chest film as a posterior mediastinal mass, or CT shows a clear-bordered cyst at the esophageal wall.
- Endoscopy + EUS shows a submucosal lesion; if it is a pure cyst, no FNA is needed since there is a risk of infection, unless a solid component is present.
- Treatment: 1. operate even with no symptoms (surgical resection), because complications can arise: infection, ulceration, perforation, bleeding, and there are reports of malignant transformation; 2. technique like leiomyoma.

## Esophageal cancer (p.784-)
- Updates from Schwartz 2020, Shackelford 2019 and NCCN V.1/2019.
- Most are diagnosed as locally advanced disease; about 30% already have metastasis.
- In the past 95% were SCC, most often at the cervical and upper thoracic esophagus.
- Currently the most common type in textbooks that come from the USA is adenocarcinoma, since GERD and Barrett are common, with adenocarcinoma at the lower esophagus; for Thailand or Asia the type found most is still SCC.

### Suspect esophageal cancer in patients with (p.785)
1. Old man, smokes cigarettes, drinks alcohol.
2. Progressive dysphagia from solid to soft and then liquid; when swallowing begins to stick, it shows the lumen is narrowed >70%; later, whatever is eaten, everything is swallowed and vomited out.
3. Rapid weight loss 10-20% in 1-2 months.
- If symptoms are suspicious, the next step is a confirmed diagnosis by upper endoscopy; when a lesion is seen that looks like cancer, take 6-8 biopsies at different positions; brush cytology is used in cases with a tight stricture that is hard to biopsy.
- Tactic during upper endoscopy: 1. before pushing the scope into the esophagus, look at the vocal cords briefly to see if there is cord paralysis; 2. if cancer is found, measure the distance from the incisors; may try gentle dilatation, then slowly pass the scope past the cancer to look at the stomach and duodenum, to see whether the stomach is usable as a substitute for the esophagus; do not forget to record the length of the cancer; if the stricture is truly tight so the scope cannot pass, do not force it, or there will be perforation.

### Early detection of esophageal cancer (p.785-787)
- Narrow band imaging (NBI) and mucosal dye staining (chromoendoscopy) are used as in the colon, to help decide where to biopsy for the highest positive yield.
- NBI: normal endoscope light is white light; narrow band imaging emits light of wavelengths 415 and 540 nm that penetrates the esophageal mucosa, showing fine vessels at the submucosa and rising to the mucosa called intraepithelial papillary capillary loops (IPCL); with a magnifying endoscope (about 85 times) the IPCL are seen clearly; where there are many vessels, suspicious: biopsy there. (Figures: penetration depth of light by wavelength; white light vs NBI; IPCL in normal squamous epithelium; IPCL changes in SCC: dilatation, tortuosity, caliber change in one IPCL, various shapes in multiples of IPCLs.)
- Chromoendoscopy: use Lugol's solution (iodine) sprayed on the mucosa: normal cells have glycogen that takes iodine and turn brown; cancer cells have no glycogen so do not take up iodine, making a pink color sign; biopsy at the pink-stained site (see Barrett). (Figure: cancer area vs normal area.)

### Staging (p.787-)
- Workup uses the following tools:
  1. Start with chest x-ray; if the lung is not seen as invaded, proceed with CT chest/abdomen with IV and oral contrast (NCCN V.1 2019 says pelvic CT with contrast is an option; Greenfield asks for pelvis in every case, which differs from NCCN). Look at lung, liver, lymph nodes, and fat planes around the esophagus, trachea, aorta, pulmonary artery, pericardium, bone.
  2. If no N or M is found on CT, the next step is to check thickness, i.e. T. Currently radiologists who are expert at CT use CT to assess TNM staging, which we may apply where there is no EUS; use EUS especially to separate T3 from T4: look whether it has invaded adjacent organs or spread far into the lung already.
- TNM staging by CT (figure):

| Finding | Stage |
|---|---|
| No wall thickening present | T1 |
| Localized wall thickening with preservation of periesophageal fat | T2 |
| Increased density of periesophageal fat | T3 |
| Invasion of adjacent structures | T4 |
- T4 invasion of adjacent structures: (a) diaphragmatic crus: absence of intervening fat plane; (b) tracheobronchial tree: tumour displacing and indenting +/- partially enveloping trachea; tumour displacing and indenting main bronchi; (c) aorta: tumour encasing >100 degrees of aortic circumference; (d) pleura: focal thickening and enhancement of pleura +/- effusion; (e) pericardium: tumour pressing against pericardium on three contiguous images without intervening fat plane +/- pericardial effusion.
- Nodes: no nodal metastasis: nodes <10 mm short axis; regional nodal: >=10 mm short axis for thoracic/upper abdominal nodes, except coeliac nodes where 8 mm short axis was used; coeliac nodes, upper paraaortic nodes and hepatoduodenal nodes were considered M1.
  3. EUS: used in cases with no evidence of M1 disease; it is the best tool for assessing T. (Figures: layers of the esophageal wall on EUS: mucosa, submucosa, circular and longitudinal muscle (muscularis propria), connective tissue; T1 invades submucosa, T2 invades muscularis, T3 invades adventitia, T4 invades adjacent structure.) Newer, thinner probes can separate T1 into T1a and T1b much better, and if enlarged regional nodes are found, FNA can be done through EUS; in a tight stricture, dilate gently then use a wire-guided EUS probe instead of a large-head probe.
  4. For cancer at or above the carina with no evidence of M1: do bronchoscopy in every case; if the scope shows features suspicious of bronchial invasion, biopsy at once: 4.1 bulging of tracheobronchial mucosa; 4.2 swollen, red mucosa; 4.3 contact bleeding; 4.4 mucosal fixation.
  5. Laryngoscopy: Siewert says if SCC, send to ENT in every case to look at the throat to exclude synchronous carcinoma of the upper airway.
  6. MIS for staging: diagnostic laparoscopy with peritoneal washing, and thoracoscopy: generally used in cases where CT suspects occult metastasis, especially cancer at the EGJ (Siewert type 2, 3), which is adenocarcinoma, and clinical T3 or N+ disease; NCCN 2018 considers laparoscopy depending on histology: if SCC, do not; some centers in the USA do laparoscopy routinely, but the view of most cancer centers is that it is not worth it besides high cost, and results are no better than non-invasive studies; conclusion: not routinely used.
  7. Barium esophagogram: [item 6 in the book's second numbering] will be swallowed away; some say no need to do it, go to EGD + biopsy directly; the author's practice is still to use it, since it shows location and length well and shows the angulation and axis. (Figure: barium esophagogram of a stricture.)
  8. MRI: not better than CT, except to see invasion in cancer of the cervical esophagus.
  9. PET/CT skull base to mid-thigh: to assess metastasis and to see the result of giving neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment; NCCN V.2 2018 advises doing it in patients with no evidence of M1 (preferred). (Figure: para-aortic nodes of 5-8 mm with increased FDG uptake, may be considered benign on CT by size criteria.)
- NCCN workup summary (figure): H&P; upper GI endoscopy and biopsy; chest/abdominal CT with oral and IV contrast; pelvic CT as clinically indicated; PET-CT evaluation if no evidence of M1 disease; CBC and comprehensive chemistry profile; EUS if no evidence of M1 disease; endoscopic resection (ER) is essential for the accurate staging of early-stage cancers; biopsy of metastatic disease as clinically indicated; HER2-neu testing if metastatic adenocarcinoma is documented/suspected; bronchoscopy if tumor is at or above the carina with no evidence of M1 disease; assign Siewert category; nutritional assessment and counseling; smoking cessation advice, counseling, and pharmacotherapy; screen for family history.

### Staging classification (p.790)
1. Clinical staging (c staging): from physical exam, imaging, endoscopy and findings before surgery, with nothing yet cut out as the main source: reported as cT, cN, cM.
2. Pathological staging (p staging): from pathology: pT, pN, pM.
3. Post-therapy staging: after neoadjuvant treatment, restage; reported as posttreatment clinical staging ycT, ycN, ycM and posttreatment pathological staging ypT, ypN, ypM.
- R categories of the primary tumor: R0 = no residual tumor; R1 = microscopic residual tumor; R2 = macroscopic residual tumor.
- Histologic grade: G1 = well differentiated (low grade); G2 = moderately differentiated (intermediate grade); G3 = poorly differentiated (high grade); G4 = undifferentiated (high grade).
- The benefit of staging is choosing the treatment.

### Choosing treatment depends on (p.793)
1. Staging.
2. Nutrition status before surgery: must improve nutritional status: in a case that can still swallow, give by mouth; if the stricture is tight so it will not go down, try a covered silicone stent then feed by mouth; if oral feeding does not work, feed by NG or NJ (preferred), if the oral route is not possible then give by jejunostomy tube; PEG is a secondary option, but do not do open gastrostomy since the stomach must be kept as the gastric conduit; albumin before surgery should be more than 3.4, but beware dehydration, which makes albumin appear higher than the truth.
3. Cardiopulmonary status: stop smoking before surgery, practice deep breathing exercises. 3.1 Lung: look at FEV1, the test most associated with ability to tolerate esophagectomy; if >=2 L/s it is OK to operate, but if FEV1 <1.2 do not operate. 3.2 Heart: look at symptoms, EKG with echo or thallium scan; if ejection fraction <40% do not operate.
4. Hepatic reserve: Siewert believes cirrhosis patients should avoid esophageal surgery.

### Early disease (Tis-1 N0 M0) (p.794)
- Tis-1a: lesion above the basement membrane; chance of positive node <5%. Treatment (NCCN V.1/2019): (figure: EMR: saline elevates esophageal cancer; cap and snare placed over cancer; snare cuts)
  1. If no contraindication, choose endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) as the preferred treatment; NCCN still accepts it in T1b but it must be sm1.
  2. Ablation such as PDT or laser for residual disease after mucosal resection.
  3. In cases unsuitable for EMR, do esophagectomy.
- T1b (sm2, sm3): chance of positive node 3-20%. Treatment: should do esophagectomy.
- T1b N+: treatment: 1. preoperative chemoRT followed by esophagectomy for non-cervical esophagus; 2. definitive chemoRT for cervical esophagus.

### Locally or regionally advanced disease (T2-4a N0-3 M0) (p.794)
- Principle of treatment: should be a combination of surgery, RT, chemo.
1. T2-4a SCC: should get neoadjuvant chemoRT first, then wait 6-8 weeks, then operate.
2. T2-4a that is adenocarcinoma or cancer at the EGJ: should get preoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery.
3. Schwartz 2015 believes T2 N0 is still confined disease, so esophagectomy can be done at once without neoadjuvant treatment (the reference cited is rather old), whereas Sabiston 2017 p.1035 believes, from the CROSS trial comparing neoadjuvant chemoradiation followed by surgery with surgery alone, that the neoadjuvant arm had greater survival benefit; the author agrees with the majority: it is more correct to give neoadjuvant treatment before operating.

### Advanced disease (T4b or M1 disease) or cervical lesion (p.795)
- Treatment: definitive chemoRT.
- But when looking at Japan, they still operate as the main treatment, because they believe 3-field LN dissection is adequate treatment, so chemoRT is not needed.

#### Symptoms or findings that mean do not operate (p.795)
1. Persistent bone or spinal pain; 2. hoarse voice from RLN palsy; 3. phrenic nerve palsy causing paralysis of the diaphragm; 4. malignant TE fistula (symptom: coughing with meals); 5. Horner syndrome; 6. palpable liver mass; 7. palpable enlarged supraclavicular node; 8. malignant pleural effusion; 9. aortic invasion (look at the fat plane on CT, which disappears); 10. the doctor confirms the patient will live no longer than 3 months.

### Japanese classification (p.795)
- The Japanese classify by depth of invasion (T), ignoring lymph node spread (N):
  1. Early esophageal cancer: cancer in the mucosa layer only.
  2. Superficial esophageal cancer: cancer in the submucosa layer only.
  3. Advanced esophageal cancer: cancer penetrating through the muscle layer, or muscularis propria.
- For lymph node grouping use Japanese numbering (Japanese Esophageal Society): numbers 100-104 are in the neck; 105-114 in the chest; 1-20 in the abdomen; do not memorize, just look at the picture; the only abdominal nodes to care about if they must be removed are nodes along the lesser curve. (Figure: 101 cervical paraesophageal; 102 deep cervical; 104 supraclavicular; 105 upper thoracic paraesophageal; 106-rec recurrent nerve; 106-tbL left tracheobronchial; 107 subcarinal; 108 middle thoracic paraesophageal; 109 main bronchus; 110 lower thoracic paraesophageal; 111 supradiaphragmatic; 112 posterior mediastinal; TD along thoracic duct; 1 right cardial; 2 left cardial; 3 lesser curvature; 7 left gastric artery; 8 common hepatic artery; 9 celiac artery; 11 splenic artery.)
- The Chinese also use their own, changed to groups of 200 (just for listening, not memorized); figure: Chinese node stations C201-C209.

### Guideline from Schwartz (figure, p.796)
- Selection for surgery: Age >75 years -> palliation. Physiologic fitness: FEV1 <1.25, ejection fraction <40% -> palliation. Clinical staging -> palliation if: recurrent nerve paralysis, Horner's syndrome, persistent spinal pain, paralysis of diaphragm, fistula formation, malignant pleural effusion, endoscopic tumor length >9 cm, abnormal esophageal axis, multiple enlarged nodes or distant organ metastasis on CT, more than 20% weight loss, loss of appetite (relative). Endoscopic ultrasound -> palliation: transmural tumors with >4 enlarged nodes. Intraoperative staging -> palliation: unresectable primary, cavitary spread, distant metastasis, extension through mediastinal wall, multiple gross lymph node metastases, microscopic nodal metastasis at margins of the en bloc dissection; otherwise curative en bloc resection. Palliative symptoms: dysphagia, obstruction, pain of ulceration, bleeding, infection, anxiety; requirements for palliative transhiatal resection: free of distant organ metastasis; complete excision of primary tumor possible; else nonsurgical palliation. (*Could include combined Rx and chemo/neoadjuvant therapy prior to resection to increase resectability and potential survival in patients 75 or under.)
- This guideline looks old; if the patient is operated and has some condition that makes cure seem unlikely, the Western surgeons do not forbid surgery; but warn to think carefully before setting the OR: 1. tumor length >8 cm (think of difficulty eating); 2. barium swallow shows abnormal axis; 3. CT chest shows nodes >4; 4. age, or anorexia and weight loss >20%. Note: these 4 are not absolute contraindications since neoadjuvant can be given.

## Surgical treatment (p.796-)
- A fit patient with a cancer located lower than 5 cm below the cricopharyngeus should get curative esophagectomy; palliative esophagectomy has been dropped since stent and laser have replaced it.
- However, only about 40% of patients are candidates for esophagectomy.

### Curative surgical therapy (p.797)
1. Transthoracic esophagectomy (TTE): 1.1 Ivor Lewis operation; 1.2 McKeown's operation; 1.3 en bloc esophagectomy; 1.4 left thoracoabdominal esophagectomy.
2. Transhiatal esophagectomy (THE).
3. Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR).
4. Vagal sparing esophagectomy (VSE).
- Resection margin: 1. proximal margin should be at least 5 cm; but if possible 10 cm, the best, because recurrence at the anastomosis falls from 20% to 0%; 2. distal margin at least 5 cm; 3. do not forget the circumferential margin: should be cut en bloc (read at the back, on radical esophagectomy).
- Reconstruction technique: hand-sewn or stapler: no difference in leakage and stricture; depends on the surgeon's experience, skill and preference.

### Esophageal substitution (p.797)
- Stomach: the highest choice (>95%), because simple with a single anastomosis, short operative time, and fewer leaks because of good blood supply, with the main supply from the right gastroepiploic artery; disadvantages: acid irritation, aspiration, and the cancer site with a recurrence is tighter than using colon. (Figure: esophagogastric anastomosis, stomach, vascular pedicle, pyloromyotomy.)
- (Figure: gastric substitution: gastric conduit, anastomosis, azygos vein.)
- Tips and technique: 1. to reduce tension do maximum Kocherization; 2. make a gastric tube about 4 cm wide, cutting the lesser curve about 4 cm above the pylorus.
- Left colon or right colon is the second choice: suitable for patients who cannot use the stomach, e.g. cancer near the cardia, especially Siewert type 3, or involvement of the lesser curve, a contracted stomach, or a case with previous gastrectomy; connect it isoperistaltically to prevent aspiration.
  - Disadvantages of colon: difficult operation since there are 3 anastomoses, and blood supply is not as good as the stomach.
  - Do not use colon in cases with: diverticular disease, unrecognized carcinoma, colitis (IBD), previous colon surgery, and AAA or extensive atherosclerosis of the SMA.
  - Comparing right and left colon: 1. advantages of left colon: reliable blood supply, size not too fat, length enough to use as a conduit with no tension; disadvantage: in the elderly there is often atherosclerosis of the IMA, which affects the conduit's blood supply. 2. Advantages of right colon: has an IC valve that helps prevent reflux; the cecum acts as a food reservoir; the size of the terminal ileum is close to the esophagus, easy to join; disadvantages: blood vessels vary a lot, especially the middle colic artery, and the cecum is big, tight in the chest. (Figures: left colon segment with vascular pedicle and esophagocolic anastomosis; right colon with ileogastric, ileocolic anastomoses.)
- 3. Jejunum: not popular, because of the blood supply pattern as segments, so the conduit cannot be long, and when joined to the esophagus it is very tight. (Figure: jejunal free flap in neck.)
- Stomach: in the form of a gastric tube, narrow and slender, preferred over using the whole stomach, with less stasis.
  - Disadvantage of the gastric tube: more leakage and anastomotic stricture than using the whole stomach.
  - For blood supply of the gastric tube, preserve the right gastroepiploic artery; the right gastric artery may be preserved if possible, since it adds a little blood supply, but the disadvantage is it limits mobilization, tight when connected at the neck. (Figure: creation of gastric tube: divided left gastric, 5 cm from pylorus, division parallel to greater curvature, gentle traction.)

### Lesser curve resection (p.800)
- When making the gastric tube, remove the lesser curve up to the 4th branch of the left gastric artery because: 1st branch has positive node 14%; 2nd branch 10%; 3rd branch 3%; 4th branch 0%.
- So when making the gastric tube, cut the lesser curve until the 4th branch, about 4 cm above the pylorus; cut the esophagus at the EGJ, the esophageal hiatus usually must be cut further around 11 o'clock to widen the hole (11 o'clock is the landmark since in papillotomy at the ampulla of Vater the cut is at 11 o'clock too); then push the stomach made into a tube up into the chest or neck.

### Route of substitution (p.800)
1. Posterior mediastinum: the most popular since it is the original route and the shortest; limitation: if there is much scar, the conduit cannot be placed.
2. Retrosternum or substernum: used when the original position cannot be used; disadvantages: longer than the posterior mediastinal route by about 5-10 cm, and has angulation at 2 positions, at the xiphoid and at the neck, which can make swallowing difficult.
3. Subcutaneous route: used when there is no space left for the conduit; advantage: detect graft obstruction easily; disadvantage: not cosmetic. (Figure: subcutaneous route.)

### Pyloroplasty (p.801)
- Although not mandatory as routine, since there is a chance of gastric stasis from vagal denervation, in only 13% of patients; Akiyama, the father of esophagectomy, though he spoke softly, said that if you do not do pyloroplasty and stasis occurs, one can use balloon dilation to help; the author advises pyloroplasty at home since the cost is low and takes only 10 minutes. (Figure: pyloromyotomy, duodenum mobilized.)
- Since 2000 many RCTs have compared gastric drainage procedures (pyloroplasty, pyloromyotomy, or botox injection into the pylorus) against none; the results do not conclude as before; the present view leans toward not doing them, because if delayed gastric emptying or gastric outlet obstruction appears, balloon dilation can fix it >90%.

### Lymphadenectomy (p.801-802)
- Concept of lymphatic drainage (figure: lymphatic return from esophagus: cervical, thoracic, terminal esophagus; thoracic duct; celiac lymph nodes; cisterna chyli):
  1. Above the tracheal bifurcation drain to the neck.
  2. Below the tracheal bifurcation drain to the lower mediastinum and celiac axis.
  3. At the tracheal bifurcation it drains in 2 directions, upward and downward.
- Risk of lymph node involvement: 1. lymphatic drainage prefers to spread along the length of the esophagus more than across; 2. divided into 3 major groups: neck, chest, abdomen (3 field); 3. in surgery, if nodes are removed from all 3 groups (all 3 fields) it is called 3-field lymphadenectomy; if only 2 groups, 2-field.
- 4. Tumor thickness and nodes: 4.1 T1a (not past the basement membrane): positive node <5%; 4.2 T1b (not past the submucosa): positive node 10-30%; 4.3 if penetrating the submucosa but not yet the muscle: positive node 20-40%; 4.4 if invasion to muscle: positive node 60%; 4.5 if invasion to adventitia: positive node 80%.
- 5. From item 4, EMR is suited only for T1a. (Figure: lymphatic vessels in the wall layers: epithelium, basement membrane, lamina propria, muscularis mucosae, submucosa, inner circular muscle, outer longitudinal muscle, adventitia, thoracic duct; direction of lymph flow from the esophagus.)
- Currently the extent of lymphadenectomy is still debated as to how much; we divide the lymph node dissection into 3 fields:
  1. Upper abdominal lymph node dissection: remove the superior gastric group, celiac trunk, and common hepatic node.
  2. Thoracic lymph node dissection: remove mediastinal nodes, pulmonary hilum nodes on both sides, nodes at the carina, nodes at both bronchi, and paratracheal nodes. 2.1 Extended mediastinal dissection: also take the right paratracheal nodes. 2.2 Total mediastinal dissection: remove the nodes along the left recurrent laryngeal nerve and subaortic nodes.
  3. Cervical lymph node dissection: remove nodes along the recurrent laryngeal nerve on both the right and left, deep external nodes beside the internal jugular vein, and deep internal nodes.
- From the classic study of Akiyama in 1994, showing % of positive nodes at each site for cancers of the neck, chest and abdomen: no matter where the cancer lies, positive nodes are found in all 3 fields, with: 1. upper 2/3 mostly spreading upward, though a small portion spreads downward; 2. lower 1/3 mostly spreading downward, with a small portion spreading upward; 3. surely the Japanese surgeons, who are strict and diligent, support 3 fields.
- Figure (Akiyama: % positive nodes by field, from top neck to abdomen, for upper thoracic tumor n=54: 16.7, 38.9, 11.1, 5.6, 5.6; middle thoracic n=680: 4.0, 3.8, 32.9, 7.1, 17.1; lower thoracic n=343: 1.5, 3.0, 22.7, 37.0, 33.2). The text beside it mentions that nodes far from the tumor can be positive in as much as 74% in one group and as little as 12.2% in another [unclear which groups]. This is the reason Akiyama advised 3-field lymphadenectomy; the problem is Akiyama's data are rather biased and cannot change opinions, though even the Japan Esophageal Society edition 11/2017 still follows Akiyama.
- In the USA, in standard textbooks and NCCN: 3-field lymphadenectomy should be done for lesions in the middle 1/3 and above; for the lower 1/3, which is usually adenocarcinoma, only 2-field (chest and abdomen). (Figure: 2- and 3-field lymphadenectomy.)
- Number of nodes that should be removed for adequate nodal staging: >=15 nodes. The role of SLN is still under study.

## Transthoracic esophagectomy (TTE) (p.803-806)
- 4 procedures to know; choose by tumor location. (Figure: location by distance from teeth: cricopharyngeus 15 cm; sternal notch 20 cm; azygos vein 25 cm; inferior pulmonary vein 30 cm; LES 40 cm; EG junction/cardia 45 cm: cervical 15-20, upper thoracic 20-25, middle thoracic 25-30, lower thoracic 30-40, EGJ/cardia 40-45.)

### 1. Ivor Lewis (p.804)
- Suited for distal 1/3 lesions.
- Uses 2 incisions: patient supine first, open the abdomen with an upper midline incision, mobilize the stomach, make the gastric tube, remove celiac and left gastric nodes, place a jejunostomy tube; then turn the patient to left lateral decubitus, right posterolateral thoracotomy; anastomosis in the right chest above, or at the level of, the azygos vein. (Figures: incisions; anatomy of azygos vein: left and right vagus nerves, superior vena cava, arch of azygos vein, right superior lobar bronchus, posterior intercostal vessels, esophageal plexus, thoracic aorta, anterior and posterior vagal trunks.)

### 2. McKeown (p.804-805)
- Suited for middle 1/3 and upper 1/3 lesions.
- Original McKeown or 3-hole esophagectomy: first open the abdomen to mobilize the stomach, followed by right thoracotomy to mobilize the esophagus, then open the neck to bring the stomach up and join.
- Currently the modified McKeown is popular, using 3 incisions: lie the patient left lateral decubitus, right posterolateral thoracotomy first; then supine, open the abdomen with an upper midline; finally open the left neck to bring the gastric tube up and anastomose there. (Figures: modified McKeown incisions; incision for cervical anastomosis, closure of gastrotomy for EEA stapler, right and left crus.)
- Advantage of this method: can do 3-field lymphadenectomy, and can cut the proximal margin high enough for certain.

### 3. Radical (en bloc) esophagectomy (p.805-806)
- Since Ivor Lewis and McKeown often have local recurrence because the circumferential margin is not free, expert surgeons believe that if the patient is selected well, esophagectomy en bloc, taking the tissue around the esophagus too, gives good results: 3.1 remove mediastinal pleura; 3.2 remove pericardium; 3.3 take the structures between the esophagus and spine too: thoracic duct, azygos vein, intercostal vessels, adjacent diaphragm; whether to take the spleen is still controversial; 3.4 prefer 3 incisions as in McKeown to clear all 3 fields of lymph nodes.
- Even though radical esophagectomy has higher mortality and morbidity, this method gives the best survival. (Figure: en bloc specimen.)

### 4. Left thoracic or thoracoabdominal esophagectomy (Sweet) (p.806)
- Used for low lesions (distal esophagus) with a large mass, by opening the abdomen and chest at the 8th space together, bring the stomach up for anastomosis in the left chest above the inferior pulmonary vein. (Figure: Sweet esophagectomy.)

## Transhiatal esophagectomy (THE) (p.806-807)
- The type of esophagectomy that does not enter the chest. (Figure.)
- Uses 2 incisions: upper midline at the abdomen and left cervical incision at the neck.
- Blind esophagectomy: dissect from below, dissect from above, then pull the esophagus out through the posterior mediastinum via the neck, and join it with the stomach at the neck.
- Advantages of THE: 1. no chest opening, so suitable for patients with lung disease or low FEV1; 2. the anastomosis is in the neck, so if leakage occurs it is easier to treat than a leak in the chest; 3. less operative time; 4. believed to have fewer respiratory complications; 5. less pain; 6. can be used for cancer at any site of the esophagus.
- Disadvantages of THE: 1. blind dissection cannot complete lymphadenectomy, so it is suitable for T1 lesions only; 2. complications of blind esophagectomy: bleeding, tumor rupture, tracheobronchial injury, RLN injury, chylothorax, Horner's syndrome.
- Orringer, who did very many THE, reported with a famous voice that his finger-sweep technique (blunt) had thousands of patients with good results; the author tells a story of a young staff who tried THE once and had massive bleeding and the patient died, after which they never dared do THE again.
- Summary of comparisons TTE vs THE: 1. TTE can do lymphadenectomy better; 2. TTE has more chance of R0 resection than THE; 3. the belief that THE has fewer respiratory complications looks untrue since studies found THE has fewer respiratory complications than TTE [as printed: the book says the belief seems not true]; 4. TTE has better survival than THE since it can be more radical; 5. strangely THE has more wound dehiscence-related complications than TTE; 6. SCC arising after caustic injury should use TTE since adhesions are strong; blind THE is difficult and bleeds a lot; 7. among TTE, radical en bloc gives the best survival.
- Postoperative care: take care of breathing; ask the anesthesiologist to help with epidural anesthesia.
- If a feeding jejunostomy was placed, start feeding from POD 2 and feed until the patient can eat by mouth, then remove the jejunostomy.
- If the ICD drain is not feverish and the drain fluid is clear by about POD 7, do barium swallow; if no leak, start liquids then step diet to soft and regular diet.

## Management of early disease limited to mucosa (p.807)
- Now early cases are found more from surveillance, and high-tech EUS probes can separate the esophageal wall into 9 rings, not the usual 5, with T1a vs T1b clearly separated.
- Mucosal lesion can be cured with EMR, THE, VSE (vagal sparing esophagectomy).

### Vagal sparing esophagectomy (VSE) (p.807)
- To reduce problems from the vagus nerve, which is usually cut in esophagectomy, a technique like THE: open the neck, insert a venous stripper down, then open the abdomen, cut the esophagus at the EGJ, tie the lower end of the cut esophagus to the wire, then pull the esophagus out through the neck or pull it down out through the abdomen, then use the stripper wire to pass a new wire down and pull the gastric tube up to join at the neck.
- It leaves the adventitia layer of the esophagus behind (not curative), and the lymph nodes cannot be removed either.
- VSE is therefore suited to benign processes that need esophagectomy, e.g. 1. end-stage achalasia; 2. HGD; 3. esophageal cancer limited to the mucosa, since there are very few positive nodes. (Figures: VSE steps with vein stripper, divided cervical esophagus.)
- VSE was promoted about 15 years ago; now it has dropped out of circles and few people mention it.

## Endoscopic treatment (p.808-809)
- NCCN advises use in early-stage disease (Tis, T1a), with 2 methods: 1. endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR); 2. endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD).
- Both have the same selection criteria: T1a, G1-2, no lymphovascular invasion; the difference is tumor size: EMR: tumor not more than 2 cm; ESD: tumor not more than 3 cm. (Figures: EMR steps A-D with snare and cap; ESD steps: marking, injection, cutting mucosa, dissection, proceeding dissection, complete resection.)
- Japanese data differ slightly from NCCN: the Korean and Japanese groups divide the mucosa and submucosa into 3 sub-layers each: m1, m2, m3 and sm1, sm2, sm3 (the submucosa is divided by depth of invasion). Asian reports say: if cancer is at m1-m3 the chance of positive node is 1.4-4%; at sm1 5-18%; at sm2-sm3 26.5-53.9%. (Figure: subclassification of depth of invasion of superficial carcinoma, Japan Esophageal Society: m1 epithelium; m2 lamina propria; m3 muscularis mucosae; sm1, sm2, sm3 thirds of submucosa.)
- Looking at risk of lymph node metastasis, some Japanese centers believe tumor invasion not deeper than sm1 can still be treated endoscopically, which NCCN accepts too.
- From the above spread data, most Japanese doctors believe if cancer is only at: 1. m1-m2: the treatment of choice is endoscopic treatment (EMR or ESD); 2. m3-sm1: endoscopic treatment is a relative indication; 3. sm2-sm3: do not use endoscopic treatment; if N0 do esophagectomy; if sm2-sm3 N+, give neoadjuvant CRT followed by esophagectomy.
- Summary per American and European references: 1. EMR or ESD should be used for T1a or HGD in Barrett; 2. T1b or deeper: should do esophagectomy.

## Minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) (p.810-811)
- Suited to surgeons who enjoy scope work.
- Advantage: less pain, no chest opening, since reports show the open thoracotomy group has frequent complications of pneumonia and respiratory failure, and pneumonia carries mortality up to 20%.
- Summary of comparison of MIS vs open esophagectomy: 1. MIS vs open: lymph node removal, leakage and mortality, and treatment results are the same; 2. MIS is better than open in shorter ICU and hospital stay and less blood loss, but lung complications await study results.
- Large centers in the USA such as UCLA and Pittsburgh have reported it is safer and less painful than open, conversion rate to open about 5%, and with good case selection results are not different from open; can do Ivor Lewis or McKeown.
- Steps of minimally invasive operation: 1. MIS McKeown: start with VATS (right thoracoscopy) then mobilize the esophagus in the chest together with clearing nodes; 2. then laparoscopy or limited laparotomy to mobilize the stomach, make the gastric tube, and remove the specimen; 3. last step open incision at the neck, then bring the gastric tube up to join at the neck. 4. MIS Ivor Lewis: do laparoscopy first, make the gastric tube, then scope into the chest and bring the gastric tube to join in the chest. 5. Now robots are being used (not popular). (Figures: port positions and incisions.)
- Contraindications of minimally invasive surgery: 1. previous thoracotomy or laparotomy with severe adhesion; 2. large tumor, e.g. T4 lesion stuck to the trachea or great vessels; 3. possibly unusable gastric conduit; 4. difficulty with lymph node dissection.

## Anastomotic complications after esophagectomy (p.811-)
- Know anastomotic leakage and conduit necrosis.

### Anastomotic leakage (p.811-812)
- Diagnosis depends on location and size of the hole: bile coming out through the drain, fever; if at the neck skin, inflammation, swelling and redness; at the chest, fever, rapid breathing, fast heart rate, raised white count and CRP.
- If a leak is suspected, send water-soluble contrast esophagogram first; but some send CT with oral contrast right away, since CT is more sensitive; endoscopy is used to confirm diagnosis and in cases treated by intervention.
- Found in about 12%, and serious ones surely go into the drain, from 21 high-volume centers collected into a group called Esophagectomy Complications Consensus Group (ECCG) in 2015, which defined and typed 3 types and the treatment approach: defined as full thickness GI defect involving esophagus, anastomosis, staple line, or conduit irrespective of presentation or method of identification: Type I: local defect requiring no change in therapy or treated medically or with dietary modification; Type II: localized defect requiring intervention but not surgical therapy, e.g. interventional radiology drain, stent, or bedside opening and packing of incision; Type III: localized defect requiring surgical therapy. (Figure: ECCG 2015.)
- But the classification used popularly is the Lerut classification of anastomotic leakage, 4 grades:

| Leak class | Definition |
|---|---|
| Radiological | No clinical signs, identified on radiologic study only, requires no intervention |
| Clinical minor | Local inflammation of cervical wound, fever, elevated WBC, managed by local drainage |
| Clinical major | Severe disruption of anastomosis with sepsis, managed by percutaneous drainage or reoperation |
| Conduit necrosis | Endoscopic evidence of necrosis, mandates reoperation |
- Treatment: use the same principle as treating perforation written above.
- Now covered expandable stents are used together with percutaneous drainage in a leak not more than 30% of circumference, found to close; and newer, endoluminal vacuum therapy (EVT): a sponge wrapped around a drain tube is put via scope into the leak site, connected to continuous vacuum suction pressure 125 mmHg, replacing the sponge every 2-3 days; the perforation closes too; EVT suits a leak with a small fluid collection; the advice today is that both stent and EVT are only alternatives in treatment. (Figure: EVT steps (a)-(j).)
- Summary guideline (figure): Anastomotic leak, clinically evident -> CT scan / contrast swallow: septic patient: shock -> surgery; absence of shock -> control of contamination (percutaneous drainage, thoracoscopy) + EGDS. Non-septic patient: conservative treatment -> EGDS defining dehiscence degree: <10%: NG tube; <30%: clips (Ovesco); >30%: stent, VAC, stent + VAC.

### Conduit necrosis and anastomotic stricture (p.812)
- If only ischemia: stricture arises; but if the conduit rots, it leaks.
- Suspect stricture in cases with frequent difficult swallowing, swallowing sticking: scope and dilate repeatedly.
- But if the patient has fever with severe sepsis, rapid heart rate, raised white count, suspect a leak: give water-soluble contrast swallow or CT with oral contrast; if leak, treat as leak.
- If suspecting conduit ischemia but imaging is negative, the next step is endoscopy to see mucosal color: if only mucosal ischemia, conservative observation; or if only a small hole, place a stent to close the leak; but if mucosal color is purple-black, conduit rot is certain, rush to the OR: cut out the necrotic conduit, make diversion of saliva and exclusion of gastric acid feeding, place a feeding jejunostomy, NPO for a long time; once recovered and out and in good condition, if surviving without sepsis, and the patient is up to another operation, do colon interposition.

## Palliative treatment (p.813-815)
- Goal: to be able to swallow before death, in the remaining time of about 6 months; used for T4b, M1, positive cytology, EGJ cancer with positive supraclavicular lymph node.
- Methods:
  1. Dilation and stenting with a covered expandable metal stent under fluoroscopy: mean survival 6 months, the same as palliative esophagectomy; so stenting suits patients with liver or lung metastasis; nowadays palliative resection is no longer done.
     - The stent popularly used is the fully covered SEMS (self-expandable metallic stent), which prevents tumor ingrowth well, but the disadvantage is stent migration; so a double-layer stent was developed: the inner layer fully covered to prevent tumor ingrowth, the outer layer uncovered to prevent migration, with an anti-reflux valve/sleeve at the lower end to prevent acid reflux. (Figures: SEMS with anti-reflux valve; SEMS with anti-migration double layer and anti-reflux sleeve; stent brands Alveolus, WallFlex, Polyflex, Ultraflex; deployment: scope, Savary dilator, expandable metal stent deployed restoring patency.)
     - Contraindications for stent placement: 1. distance between the upper edge of the stent and the upper esophageal sphincter <2 cm; 2. (as printed) sepsis, coagulopathy; 3. obstruction lower down below the stent; 4. life expectancy not more than 1 month.
  2. Photodynamic therapy (PDT): chemical ablation: inject a photosensitizer agent, then light of the right wavelength is delivered and accumulates in cancer cells; the PDT laser is shone, the heat causes tissue destruction; after PDT some patients with serious illness live as long as 9 months. Disadvantages of PDT: early fistula; late stricture. (Figure: PDT steps.)
  3. Chemoradiation. 4. Laser. 5. RT alone: solves dysphagia in 2-3 months.
- Palliative options: 1. bleeding: choose between endoscopic electrocoagulation and APC; 2. obstruction: 2.1 extrinsic: treat with a covered stent; 2.2 intrinsic from endoluminal tumor: choose between PDT, YAG laser, brachytherapy; 3. malignant tracheo-esophageal fistula.
- A fully covered stent is very suitable for malignant TE fistula and is good palliation in cases with obstruction; but do not use a stent with cancer at the LES because awful acid reflux occurs, until anti-reflux stents were developed, though the result is still not better than ordinary stents; so for palliation at the LES, RT +/- chemotherapy seems better. (Figure: malignant tracheoesophageal fistula on barium, CT and stent.)
- In cases with invasion of the trachea, great vessels, heart: give chemotherapy only.
- Targeted therapy: used in advanced cancer with HER2-neu positive, using trastuzumab as in breast cancer, with fairly good response in NCCN; if biopsy is adenocarcinoma, they advise testing several biomarkers; HER2 alone is not enough, and testing at the gene level, e.g. microsatellite instability (MSI) and mismatch repair (MMR), is advised, plus RCT phase 3 trials with VEGFR and EGFR drugs being awaited, and clinical trials of gene therapy and vaccine are coming.
- Japan guideline (figure, Esophagus Cancer Handling Standard Ver. 10): work-up: esophagography, endoscopy, pathology exam, EUS, CT. Stage 0: endoscopic therapy. Stage I: endoscopic therapy, surgical therapy (with postoperative therapy), or chemoradiation. Stage II, III (T1b-T3): surgical therapy preceded by preoperative therapy, or chemoradiation. Stage III (T4), IVa: preoperative therapy then surgery (dashed), chemoradiation, chemotherapy/radiation therapy/chemoradiation/symptomatic therapy. Stage IVb: chemotherapy, radiation therapy, chemoradiation therapy, symptomatic therapy.

## Surveillance (p.816)
- 90% of relapses are found within 2 years after surgery.
  1. T1a treated with endoscopic resection: appoint follow-up, history, physical examination, upper endoscopy every 3 months in the first year, then every 6 months in the second year, then once a year for life; no CT needed.
  2. T1b-T4a any N after esophagectomy: appoint follow-up, history, physical exam, every 3-6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months in years 3-5; then once a year; labs, CT chest/abdomen or endoscopy depending on symptoms.

## Points to remember before finishing (book p.816)
- Typical reflux syndrome in a GERD patient: no need to investigate; give LSM + PPI first; if still heartburn, double the PPI dose.
- Barrett with severe dysplasia: choose endoscopic treatment before surgery.
- Antireflux procedure: choose laparoscopic more than open.
- Length of fundoplication: use 2 cm as the main principle; the stitches must wrap the lower esophagus, not the stomach; if afraid of tightness, use a bougie before suturing.
- Short esophagus: do not forget Collis gastroplasty.
- Achalasia: in good-risk patients choose surgery, start with laparoscopic myotomy + Toupet fundoplication; do not wrap Nissen: too tight, after surgery food cannot get out, difficulty swallowing.
- Nutcracker: has pressure over 180.
- Caustic injury: when answering the oral exam do not forget to build a picture for the examiner that we will treat the patient with a holistic view (physical and mental health), so do not forget a psychiatric consult and things not to do or forbidden to do, e.g. do not give steroid.
- Perforation: answer: go in and operate more than 99%; and if delayed over 48 hours, the teacher probably wants us to add diversion & exclusion.
- If EGD is successful but then the esophagus perforates, in a good-risk patient who has fever, operate right away.
- Hiatal hernia: if no symptoms, no operation. Diverticulum: if no symptoms, no operation. Duplication: if no symptoms, usually operate (see above: should be resected).
- TE fistula from cancer: needs only a stent.
- T2 cancer (cancer deep to the muscle layer) has high chance of spreading to nodes, so before surgery give neoadjuvant treatment.
- T4a cancer can still be operated.
- Cancer invading the trachea causing tracheo-esophageal fistula: treatment: place a stent to close the fistula.
