# Lung & Tracheobronchial Injuries
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 278-295 (PDF pages 2412-2429) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated ๒๒ พฤศจิกายน ๒๕๕๘)

## Introduction
- Two parts: lung parenchyma, and trachea and bronchus; kept simple, not deep — enough to manage what you find when you open the chest.

## Pulmonary Contusion
- More common in blunt than penetrating injury; usually with other injuries, e.g. multiple fracture ribs or flail chest.
- **Symptoms** — hemoptysis, dyspnea, tachypnea, cyanosis; crepitation may be heard on auscultation.
- **Imaging study**
  1. Film chest — about 2-3 hours after injury shows patchy infiltration or opacification of that lobe.
  2. CT scan — same findings as plain film, but clearer.
- Severity of pulmonary contusion correlates poorly with x-ray findings; about 70% show abnormality on chest film within 1 hour, the remaining 30% only after 4-6 hours.
- **Treatment**
  1. Admit; analgesics, chest physiotherapy, clear sputum with intensive spirometry (as printed).
  2. Observe breathing; beware hypoventilation. In a conscious patient try mask continuous positive airway pressure first; if respiratory failure → intubate and ventilator support.
  3. Fluid — emphasised: **no overhydration** (ห้าม overhydration).
  4. In theory, antibiotic and steroid are not needed.
- If a thoracotomy finds lung contusion or lung hematoma, leave it alone — it resolves by itself within 2 weeks; only watch for lung infection.

## Pulmonary Laceration
- More common in penetrating than blunt trauma.
- Small superficial tears seldom cause problems and heal themselves. Large deep wounds raise concern about bleeding and air leak. Previously, suturing both entry and exit holes closed was advised, but it still caused bleeding and air embolism → this method should no longer be used.
- **Operative approach**
  1. Superficial wound: suture with atraumatic 3-0 chromic or vicryl — interrupted first, then reinforce with a continuous over-and-over layer.
  2. Continuous bleeding that leaves the surgeon at a loss → stapler wedge resection.
  3. Deep or through-and-through ("through and though" as printed) injury → **pulmonary tractotomy**: pass a linear stapler (or a long non-crushing clamp) along the wound track to divide the lung; oversew the edges with monofilament non-absorbable suture in 2 layers; then open the lung to selectively ligate bronchioles and suture bleeding points.
  4. Lung shredded and mangled, tractotomy not possible → lobectomy.

## Tracheobronchial Injury
- Suspect when there is hemoptysis, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, or persistent air leak after ICD; most end in surgery.

## Anatomy of the Trachea
- Midline; length 11-15 cm.
- 2 parts:
  1. **Cervical part** — from the cricoid cartilage down to the thoracic inlet
  2. **Intrathoracic or mediastinal part**
- Anterior and lateral walls: 18-22 horseshoe-shaped cartilage rings that keep the trachea from collapsing; posterior wall: muscle.
- Tracheal bifurcation (carina):
  1. Anteriorly — at the sternal angle of Louis
  2. Posteriorly — at T4
- **Blood supply** — from neighbouring organs:
  1. Inferior thyroid artery
  2. Thyrocervical trunk
  3. Bronchial artery
- From the carina the right and left main bronchi branch: right at an angle of 30°, left at 45°.

## Anatomy of the Bronchus
- Bronchus has 2 parts:
  1. Mediastinal part
  2. Intrathoracic part
- Left main bronchus: 4 cm long, behind the pericardium and aortic arch.
- Right main bronchus: 2 cm long, medial and posterior to the azygos vein.
- **Blood supply** — bronchial arteries (branches of the aorta) enter from the side, like the bile duct → lateral dissection should be avoided.
- Right side has 2 bronchial arteries; left side has only 1.

## Location of Tracheobronchial Injury
- Tracheal injury from blunt trauma: most tear about 2.5 cm above the carina.
- Penetrating injury usually perforates the trachea in the neck.
- Bronchial injury in blunt trauma: tear is usually in the intrathoracic part.

## Clinical Presentation
- 2 patterns:
  1. **Communicating with the pleural cavity** — air leaks and compresses the lung; fairly severe symptoms, some die at the scene. If a resident has inserted an ICD and there is persistent air leak with the lung remaining collapsed → suspect tracheobronchial injury.
  2. **Not communicating with the pleural cavity** — milder presentation.
- **Symptoms and plain film**
  1. Dyspnea — the most common **symptom**; some have hemoptysis or hoarseness
  2. Subcutaneous emphysema — the most common **sign**
  3. In the neck: pathognomonic sign = air escaping from the penetrating wound
  4. Mediastinal emphysema on chest film
  5. Pneumopericardium — a crunching sound in time with the heartbeat on auscultation = positive **Hamman's sign**
- Chest film plus C-spine film AP and lateral may show pneumothorax, mediastinal emphysema, pneumopericardium and subcutaneous emphysema.
- CT scan helps little, except 3D-CT.

## Bronchoscopy
- Best and most effective; **gold standard** for diagnosing tracheobronchial injury.
- Use flexible bronchoscopy; once the tear is found, stop — do not pass the scope through it.
- The patient must be reasonably stable for bronchoscopy.
- **Advantages**
  1. Shows the location of injury, so the repair incision is correct
  2. Shows severity — partial or complete tear

## Management of Tracheobronchial Injury
- **Initial treatment**
  1. Airway management — intubation is fairly difficult; use flexible bronchoscope as a guide, which can pass the tube into the distal trachea and mainstem bronchus
  2. ICD
  3. Bronchoscopy as soon as the patient is ready — definitive diagnosis, and helps choose between nonoperative treatment and going to the OR

## Nonoperative Management
- **Indications**
  1. Mucosal injury < 1/3 of circumference — usually small tears with edges not far apart
  2. Minimal tissue loss
  3. No persistent air leak
  4. No other indication for thoracotomy
- **Treatment**
  1. Pulmonary toilet
  2. Antibiotic
  3. Follow with serial bronchoscopy
- Tear > 1/3 of circumference, large tissue loss, or tear of the intrathoracic trachea → operate.

## Operative Approach
- After localising the tear by bronchoscopy, anesthesia must place a long cuffed tube past the tear so the cuff lies below it.
- **Incision** — depends on location:
  1. Cervical collar incision (as for thyroid surgery), may extend downward as a T — injury at the proximal 1/3 to proximal 2/3 of the trachea
  2. Left posterolateral thoracotomy via the 5th ICS — left mainstem bronchus
  3. Right posterolateral thoracotomy via the 4th ICS — lower 1/3 of trachea, carina and right mainstem bronchus
- Median sternotomy and clamshell give poor exposure for tracheobronchial injury.

## Operative Repair
- **Principles**
  1. Limited debridement — keep as much good tissue as possible
  2. Tension free anastomosis, mucosa to mucosa
  3. Resection of ≤ 5 cm of trachea still allows end to end anastomosis
  4. Limited debridement — keep as much good tissue as possible (repeated as printed)
  5. Tension free anastomosis, mucosa to mucosa (repeated as printed)
  6. Resection of ≤ 5 cm of trachea allows end to end anastomosis (repeated as printed)
  7. Suture trachea and bronchus with monofilament absorbable, e.g. vicryl 3-0 or 4-0 (as printed), interrupted, knots tied outside; after anastomosis cover with tissue such as pleura or pericardium for reinforcement.
  8. Anastomosis feels very tight → tension-reducing manoeuvres:
     - 8.1 Neck flexion — suture the chin to the chest for about 1 week
     - 8.2 Supralaryngeal release and right hilum release gain about 2 cm; dissect anteriorly and posteriorly only, not laterally, because the blood supply enters from the side
  9. Large tissue loss that cannot be bridged → cover with a vascularized muscle flap.
  10. Injury of the distal bronchus within the lung with badly torn parenchyma → lobectomy.
  11. Note: injury < 50% of circumference → usually primary repair; > 50% → usually resection with end to end anastomosis.
  12. Associated vascular or esophageal injury → after repair, interpose nearby muscle between the suture lines. Late cases with a collapsed, mushy lung → resect that lobe.

## Algorithm for Diagnosis of Tracheobronchial Injury
Figure: Diagnostic algorithm, as steps —
1. Pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, subcutaneous emphysema, "fallen lung" sign → tube thoracostomy.
2. If persistent atelectasis or persistent/massive air leak → bronchoscopy → operative repair, nonoperative management, or occasionally stent.
3. If re-expansion:
   - Late atelectasis, hemoptysis or recurrent pneumonia → bronchoscopy, CT ± CT bronchography → operative repair / nonoperative management / occasionally stent.
   - Tube removed → later new onset "asthma", recurrent pneumonia or atelectasis → bronchoscopy, CT ± CT bronchography → as above.
4. Options for evaluation: flow-volume loop, pulmonary function tests, chest radiograph, CT, bronchoscopy.

## Algorithm for Management of Tracheobronchial Injury
Figure: Management algorithm, as steps —
1. Criteria: < 1/3 circumference (preferably mucosal only), no persistent air leak, minimal tissue loss, no other indication for operation.
2. Criteria met → nonoperative management: suctioning, pulmonary toilet, ± antibiotics with coverage of oral flora, early repeat bronchoscopy → follow-up.
3. Criteria not met → operative management; approach by site:
   - Upper 1/2 (trachea) → collar incision
   - Left main stem (not immediately at carina) → left 5th intercostal posterolateral thoracotomy
   - Lower 1/2, carinal, right main stem → right 4th intercostal posterolateral thoracotomy
4. Technique:
   - Mucosal injury alone → reattach, tissue flap
   - Injury < 50% → if not devascularized, consider primary repair; otherwise tissue flap vs end-end resection and reconstruction
   - Injury > 50% → resection and end-end reconstruction; if distal (lobar) with significant parenchymal destruction, consider lobectomy
5. Follow-up: chest radiograph, bronchoscopy, CT scan, flow-volume loops.
