# Arterial Disease 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 459-627 (PDF pages 463-631)

- Intro: vascular surgery changes fast, with new diagnostic and treatment technology; the author collected material to help residents answer questions on arterial disease in papers or orals; his own training mostly involved open vascular surgery, with little endovascular experience, so for endovascular topics one should read further or consult a vascular fellow.
- Arteries are tubes carrying oxygenated blood; their diseases are aneurysm (outpouching) and stenosis/occlusion; the principle of treatment: aneurysm: resect or exclude; stenosis: dilate, stent, or bypass.

## Basic Anatomy
- Learn the main arteries and key branches only; the rest comes with each disease.
- Arterial wall, 3 layers:
  1. Intima: innermost; endothelium on a basement membrane.
  2. Media: middle; inner and outer elastic lamina, with elastic tissue and smooth muscle.
  3. Adventitia: outermost; connective tissue, fibroblasts, nerve fibres and vasa vasorum, which feed the vessel itself; some vasa vasorum penetrate into the media.
- Autonomic: sympathetic causes vasospasm, so sympathectomy dilates the vessels; parasympathetic has a minor role.

## Definitions
- Ectasia: dilation under 50% of normal diameter.
- Aneurysm: localized dilation over 50% of normal diameter.
- Arteriomegaly: diffuse dilation over 50% of normal diameter.
- Arteriosclerosis: hardening of all layers of the wall of small arteries.
- Atherosclerosis: hardening and thickening from accumulation of fatty plaque in the intima, losing elasticity in some layers of the wall of large and medium arteries; Greek roots: athero = gruel (fatty mass in the plaque centre mixed with macrophages), sclerosis = hard (the fibrotic cap over it).

## Atherosclerosis
- It is an inflammatory response from lipid deposition within the arterial wall.
- Stages:
  1. Stage 1 (fatty streak): endothelial injury from shear stress, HT, DM or age; lipid enters the subendothelial layer, forming a fatty streak.
  2. Stage 2 (fibrous plaque): the lipid streak triggers inflammation; monocytes release cytokines, macrophages release growth factors, smooth muscle proliferates and joins lipid and white cells forming a fibrous cap that thickens into a fibrous plaque attached at the intima.
  3. Stage 3 (complicated lesion): calcification and rupture of the fibrous cap so the lipid core contacts blood, activating clotting and forming thrombus; the wall becomes hard and thick and the lumen narrows.
- Figure (timeline): foam cells, fatty streak, intermediate lesion, atheroma, fibrous plaque, complicated lesion/rupture; endothelial dysfunction from the first decade, lipid accumulation from the first decade, smooth muscle/collagen and thrombosis/hematoma from the fourth decade.
- Greenfield: atherosclerosis is a disease of the intima, while hypertension is a disease of the media.
- Atherosclerotic vessels: firm outside with a fibrous cap over a soft centre containing platelets, lipid, cholesterol and triglyceride; thrombus formation on it is called atherothrombosis.
- When the plaque ruptures, debris goes to end organs: coronary: MI; carotid to brain: stroke; leg: limb ischemia; renal artery: kidney failure and high BP.
- Basic risk factors: 1. DM (about 30% of atherosclerosis patients are diabetic); 2. smoking (vasoconstriction, hyperviscosity, vessel inflammation, platelet aggregation); 3. HT; 4. dyslipidemia, especially high LDL-C and VLDL-C; 5. hyperhomocysteinemia; 6. hereditary factor; 7. geographic; 8. old age, obesity.
- Atherosclerosis causing aneurysm results from degeneration.
- Atherosclerosis causing stenosis: blood flow falls only when diameter decreases over 50% from normal; so diameter of stenosis is the most important contributor to flow.
- So if narrowing is under 50%, blood flow is unchanged and no treatment is needed.

## Hypercoagulable Disorder
- Congenital: the most common is resistance to activated protein C (factor V Leiden).
- Acquired: the most common is smoking.

## Principles of Open Arterial Procedure
- Similar to principles for vascular trauma:
  1. Vascular exposure: incision allowing easy access, extendable; cut across vessels with care, gently, keep nerves away, protect collateral pathways.
  2. Proximal and distal control: vascular clamp, vascular loop, or endovascular balloon occlusion.
  3. Vascular preparation: choose good inflow; for outflow choose a soft arterial wall site; keep the distance between inflow and outflow as short as possible.
  4. Arteriotomy: open along the length of the vessel, about 2 times the diameter of the inflow vessel; if a hard plaque is found, tack the plaque to the wall with tacking sutures or do endarterectomy; suture inside-out to prevent plaque dissection.
  5. Vascular conduit: autogenous such as GSV or artificial such as PTFE; if the distal anastomosis is above the knee, place the conduit in a subcutaneous plane (easy to check); if below the knee, place it in the deep anatomic plane along the native artery to prevent kinking.
  6. Vascular anastomosis: not too tight, no leak; ensure the adventitial layer is incorporated (the strongest layer), with eversion of the endothelium. Small vessels: bevel or spatulate ends; connections end-to-end, end-to-side or side-to-side as appropriate. If GSV is too short, use artificial vessel for extension, with Miller cuff or Linton/Taylor patch. Vascular sutures must be non-absorbable.

## Endovascular Repair
- With modern technology and experience, the indications for endovascular repair are widening, gradually replacing open repair. Examples:
  1. Ruptured AAA: after vascular imaging, with a hybrid OR, EVAR results show that if ready with an appropriate landing zone and no renal artery involvement, endovascular repair has lower mortality than open.
  2. Traumatic aortic injury: even with the subclavian artery nearby, a stent graft can be placed even covering the left subclavian artery with few problems; if arm ischemia develops, correct with carotid-subclavian bypass.
  3. Renal artery stenosis: angioplasty with stent is first-line treatment instead of open revascularization.
  4. Blunt cerebrovascular injury grade 3: intervention is useful; for grade 1 and 2 it is not needed; anticoagulation is wanted.

## Consensus (frequently cited)
1. TASC: Transatlantic Intersociety Consensus for PAD. 2. NASCET: North America Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial. 3. ECST: European Carotid Surgery Trial.

## Cerebrovascular Disease
- Brain circulation:
  1. Carotid system (anterior circulation): supplies 85% of the brain, the cerebral hemisphere; the common carotid divides at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage into external and internal carotid; this chapter emphasises extracranial carotid disease.
  2. Vertebrobasilar system (posterior circulation): formed from the 2 vertebral arteries; know it only roughly. Figures: vertebral artery parts and circle of Willis (skip).
- Anatomy: 1st branch of ECA is superior thyroid A; 1st branch of ICA is ophthalmic A; ECA to ICA communication in 2 places: 1. via the ophthalmic A (1st branch of ICA) with the periorbital branch of ECA; 2. via internal maxillary A, a branch of ECA.
- This section focuses on carotid occlusive disease; the main exam interest is the indication for carotid intervention in symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid stenosis, whether carotid endarterectomy or stenting.

## Carotid Artery Stenosis
- A disease causing disability or death; classic: walking back and forth or the eyes going dark, falling over, then recovering; mouth drooping, face weak, arm weak.
- Causes of ischemic stroke: cardiogenic emboli 35%, carotid artery disease 30%, the rest idiopathic and miscellaneous.
- About 7-10% of men and 5-7% of women over 65 years have carotid stenosis over 50% but no symptoms.
- Etiology of carotid stenosis:
  1. Atherosclerosis: most common, 90%, usually at the carotid bifurcation; the problem is embolization of plaque fragments, or stenosis of 70% or more, a diameter that reduces blood flow considerably.
  2. Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD): found in women around age 40, often bilateral; associated with intracranial aneurysm, vertebral artery lesions and FMD of the renal artery; treatment of FMD: responds to PTA usually; surgery is often wrong. Figure: atheromatous plaque specimen (skip).
  3. Others: Takayasu; carotid coiling: the internal carotid is too long so it loses elasticity, coils and kinks.
- Risk factors: same as coronary or chronic limb ischemia: age, diabetes, high lipid, smoking; must be known because treatment includes controlling risk factors.
- Most common cause of cerebral ischemia: arterio-arterial embolization from plaque fragments.
- 75% of patients with cerebral ischemia have a surgically accessible lesion.

## Clinical Manifestations
- Symptoms of cerebral ischemic events, mostly from plaque embolization into the ICA; the main symptoms are cerebral symptoms and eye symptoms.
- Cerebral symptoms:
  1. TIA (transient ischemic attack): temporary focal loss of brain function without aura beforehand: hemiparesis of face, arm or leg on the side opposite the stenosed vessel, dysphasia (garbled speech); TIA symptoms resolve within 24 hr without persistent deficit. Figure: FAST (face, arms, speech, time) (skip).
  2. Crescendo TIA: TIA occurring more than 2-3 times a day; these have a high chance of complete stroke soon, so urgent surgery is needed.
  3. RIND (reversible ischemic neurologic deficit): symptoms improve within 3 weeks, on average about 3 days.
  4. Complete stroke: symptoms last longer than 3 weeks and improve slowly over a long time; in the history of this group, TIA is present in about 30-50%. "Stroke in evolution" or progressive stroke means a stroke that has occurred and is recovering, then pushes into a repeat stroke.
- Note: dizziness is not found in TIA, because it is a symptom of vertebrobasilar insufficiency.
- Eye symptoms:
  1. Amaurosis fugax: from yellow cholesterol emboli passing the ophthalmic A to block the retinal A; this plaque in the retinal artery has a famous name: Hollenhorst plaque. Figure: branch retinal artery occlusion (skip). Symptom: sudden transient monocular blindness like a grey curtain shade, on the same side as the stenosed vessel; the blindness is temporary, lasting under 5 min, then vision returns as before; found in 24% of TIA cases, a warning of carotid stenosis.
  2. Permanent visual loss: emboli block the central retinal A.
- Natural history of TIA: risk of stroke: 1. 4-8% in 30 days; 2. 10-15% in 3 months to 1 year; 3. 24-29% in 5 years.
- Severity of ICA stenosis is the most important predictor of stroke:
  1. Severe stenosis (over 70%): risk of ipsilateral stroke 40%, of which 26% occur within 2 years.
  2. Moderate stenosis (50-69%): risk of ipsilateral stroke 22% in 5 years.
  3. Minimal stenosis (50% or less): risk of ipsilateral stroke 2-3% in 6 years.
- Measuring degree of carotid stenosis (figure: ECST, NASCET, CC methods):
  1. Europe (ECST): the narrowest diameter, divided by the widest diameter at the stenosed site: (C-A)/C x 100%.
  2. USA (NASCET): same numerator but divided by the widest diameter of the normal carotid above: (B-A)/B x 100%.
  3. Common carotid (CC) method: (D-A)/D x 100%.
- In patients with symptoms and stenosis over 70%, the chance of stroke is higher with: 1. male more than female; 2. older age; 3. cerebral symptoms more than eye symptoms; 4. many comorbidities, e.g. DM, smoking; 5. recurrent events; 6. ruptured plaque, e.g. irregular surface, ulcer.
- Plaque morphology is an important predictor of stroke: calcified plaque and dense plaque carry stroke risk 3-10%, while soft echolucent plaque carries stroke risk 21%.
- Physical examination in suspected carotid stenosis:
  1. Palpate peripheral pulses; measure BP in both arms; look for PAD.
  2. Auscultate the neck for bruit.
  3. Eye examination (fundus).
  4. Directed neurologic examination.
  5. Cardiac evaluation.
- Audible bruit: heard in about 10%; location tells the lesion: best heard at the angle of the jaw behind the SCM means the lesion is at the carotid bifurcation; clearly heard above the thyroid cartilage means the lesion is at the common carotid; ask the patient to stop breathing briefly to hear it; bruit is a marker of atherosclerosis: if heard, it shows stenosis over 50%, but it does not tell the degree of severity: if the vessel is almost completely occluded (percentage cannot be told) there will be no bruit.

## Investigation
- When history, symptoms or examination suggest carotid stenosis, e.g. fugax or bruit: investigate starting with duplex US to screen; if true stenosis is found, send carotid imaging, then plan treatment.
- Duplex scan: the preferred initial diagnostic study, best for screening carotid stenosis and also for follow-up after treatment; sensitivity and specificity about 80-90% (83% and 89% as printed). Figure: duplex (skip).
  - Advantages: 1. noninvasive, no contrast; 2. tells degree of stenosis (percentage); 3. tells plaque morphology: whether it needs urgent treatment, e.g. plaque hemorrhage, plaque ulceration or plaque rupture, with high chance of embolization; such plaque must be treated even if degree of stenosis is under 70% and there are no symptoms.
  - Disadvantages: 1. cannot assess lesions above the angle of the mandible; 2. operator dependent: the person who does it often is more reliable; 3. tortuous vessels and excessive calcification are hard to read.
- Carotid imaging: the most prominent and popular, done together with duplex, is MRA, next CTA, and last conventional cerebral angiogram.
- MRA: good in carotid stenosis, suits looking at brain lesions and sees plaque rupture clearly. Figure: MRA with right ICA 100% stenosed and left ICA 80% (skip).
  - Advantages: 1. noninvasive, very little nephrotoxicity; 2. low false negative; 3. sensitivity 95%, specificity 91%.
  - Disadvantages: 1. often overestimates, since it is very motion sensitive: a slight movement makes the vessel read as stenosed; 2. MRI general drawbacks: expensive, long time, claustrophobia, obese neck, implanted devices.
- CTA: inject dye into a vein then put in the machine.
  - Advantages: 1. shows intracranial stenosis well; 2. takes less time than MRA and cheaper.
  - Disadvantages: 1. contrast nephrotoxic; 2. thick or many calcifications are hard to interpret; 3. metallic dental implants or surgical clips at the neck obscure the carotid.
- Conventional cerebral angiography or DSA: still the gold standard, very accurate, especially for the aortic arch lesion; disadvantage: risk of embolization then stroke about 0.5-5%, guaranteeing losing the patient next year; so now used much less, only in situations where CTA or MRA cannot reach a conclusion, and during intervention.

## Management for Carotid Stenosis
- Goals: 1. prevent TIA or stroke; 2. prevent progression of carotid atherosclerosis; 3. prevent MI and death.
- Three main treatments: 1. control and risk factor modification: stop alcohol, stop smoking, exercise, control sugar, BP, lipids; 2. medical treatment; 3. surgical treatment.
- Control and risk factor modification: for arterial disease, use the ACC/AHA 2016 guideline (details in chronic limb ischemia): 1. stop smoking, lose weight and control BMI not over 25, exercise; 2. treat DM, BP, lipids, take low dose ASA.
- Medical treatment:
  1. ASA low dose: taking it daily helps, but high dose is not much more helpful and causes empty stomach pain.
  2. Clopidogrel (75 mg OD), cilostazol: useful for secondary prevention, i.e. after TIA or stroke has occurred, to prevent recurrence.
  3. Statin is also secondary prevention: if no symptoms yet, taking it does not help prevent stroke.
- Surgical treatment: 1. Carotid endarterectomy (CEA); 2. Carotid artery stenting (CAS).
- We operate or stent to prevent embolization, not to add blood flow to the brain, hoping that more blood flow makes the patient smarter [humorous aside].

## Carotid Endarterectomy (CEA)
- The standard treatment of carotid stenosis if chosen well, definitely beneficial, whether symptomatic or not.
- Patients who benefit from CEA (Stroke Council of the American Association):
  1. Symptomatic patients:
     - 1.1 Severe stenosis (70-99%) and symptoms: the most accepted.
     - 1.2 Moderate stenosis (50-69%): benefit if: 1.2.1 the surgeon's complication rate (morbidity and mortality) is under 6%; 1.2.2 plaque morphology has problems, e.g. ulceration or hemorrhage with easy embolization.
     - 1.3 Recovering stroke or stroke in evolution that can come back to help themselves; for TIA, not more than 6 months.
     - 1.4 Difficult anatomy for stenting.
     - 1.5 Good surgical risk.
  2. Asymptomatic patients:
     - 2.1 Stenosis over 60% benefit if: 2.1.1 the surgeon's complication rate is under 3%; 2.1.2 life expectancy over 5 years; 2.1.3 good risk patient.
     - 2.2 Men under 75 years or women under 70 years with stenosis over 60%.
- Remarks:
  1. Symptomatic with carotid stenosis under 50%, or asymptomatic with carotid stenosis under 60%: no benefit from CEA.
  2. High risk patients, or those expected to live not more than 3 years: should not be operated.
  3. Total or complete occlusion: carotid 100% occluded, which shows that collateral is enough: no need for CEA.
  4. Recent complete stroke that is not complete ICA occlusion: wait 4-6 weeks before doing CEA, for fear of bleeding.
  5. Asymptomatic carotid stenosis undergoing CEA: studies found men benefit more than women.
- Surgeon who should do CEA: 1. morbidity and mortality of CEA under 6% in symptomatic patients, or under 3% in asymptomatic ones; 2. experience over 100 cases, or in the last 2 years not fewer than 50 cases (hard in Thailand; the author helped 3 cases and operated 1, scared of postoperative stroke, so now prefers wiring and breast biopsy).
- Bilateral carotid stenosis: if both sides need CEA, consider:
  1. Do one side at a time, because doing both at once the neck swells from tissue trauma or postoperative bleeding compresses the trachea, which is dangerous.
  2. Before the second operation, send the patient for laryngoscopy first, even with no hoarseness, to rule out occult unilateral vocal cord problem, e.g. RLN neuropraxia from the first operation.
  3. Which side first: 3.1 the symptomatic side first, regardless of % stenosis; 3.2 if both symptomatic, choose the more stenosed side first; 3.3 if all same, do the dominant side first.
- Emergency CEA: may be used in: 1. fluctuating neurological symptom; 2. crescendo / evolving TIA.

## CEA Technique
- Figure: incision line, hypoglossal nerve descending branch, ECA, ICA, vagus nerve, CCA, internal jugular vein; steps A-C (clamps, plaque removal, closure).
- Remove the plaque and the inner wall of the artery, leaving only the outer layer: the media and adventitia.
- Before surgery check with duplex and MRA so errors are fewer; safe; though some centers in the USA, as in Cameron 10th, do CEA on duplex alone, up to 95% at the centre, but with low volume do not copy: duplex reading is operator dependent.
- Need to stop ASA? Answer: can continue low dose ASA, no need to stop.
- Anesthesia: choose between locoregional anesthesia (LRA) or GA; advantage of LRA: can check the patient at any time, especially for stroke; deep cervical block is also included; criteria for patients: must be cooperative.
- Incision: longitudinal along the anterior border of the SCM, then dissect medially to the internal jugular vein.
- Finding the CCA at the bifurcation: use the facial vein as landmark.
- Clamping control: control CCA and branches; ask the anesthetist to look at the vessels first; place the clamp above the plaque; open the artery and use an endarterectomy spatula or elevator to remove only the plaque; do not take the intimal layer out with it, which would cause dissection of the internal carotid until blood cannot enter.
- Do not forget: before clamping the artery, give systemic heparinization; and if a shunt is to be placed, insert it now.
- After removing the plaque, place tacking sutures at the distal end.
- Closing the artery: may use a patch to reduce re-stenosis, but with a patch beware patch blowout, found in 1%; so use a patch in cases where the vessel is small, which is often found in women.
- Shunt: whether to shunt routinely or selectively is still debated, since only about 10% truly need one, and the downside is embolization risk 0.002-5%; some shunt routinely, others believe good monitoring means no shunt is needed.
- Those who do not shunt must assess in the OR during the operation: 1. use local anesthesia; 2. try temporary clamping of the carotid and watch for symptoms; 3. measure stump pressure: if under 25 mmHg, shunt; 4. use aids such as intraoperative EEG or Doppler.
- The author suggests shunting routinely, to avoid the fuss.
- Drain: not necessary routinely; if used, Radivac No. 8, kept 1-2 days then off.
- Follow up after surgery with duplex surveillance once a year; if stenosis starts to appear without symptoms, observe and follow.
- Recurrence before 2 years: usually from intimal hyperplasia; treatment: CAS (carotid stenting) or redo surgery; the trend favours CAS.
- Recurrence after 2 years: usually progressive atherosclerosis; treatment trend: redo surgery, e.g. patch angioplasty alone, or new CEA then patch closure, or interposition vein graft, more than CAS; (the author notes the 2014 answer would be CAS, following the trend).

## Complications of CEA
- Perioperative stroke (2%): the most serious complication, during surgery or after; most common cause: plaque embolization; next: technical problems such as occluding intimal flap, or distal carotid dissection with thrombosis.
  - Management:
    1. Recognized while still in the OR: re-explore right away, since usually a clot sits at the endarterectomy site; open, do thrombectomy and corrective repair of the intimal flap; at the end do on-table angiography to check patency and no residual emboli.
    2. Recognized early, within 3 hours in the recovery room: in theory do duplex scan first; if clot found, re-explore; if no clot, rule out ischemic brain infarction with CT; but in practice, re-explore at once, since technical problems like intimal flap are met more often.
    3. Delayed recognition, over 3 hours: do CT first to see whether CVA such as brain infarction or intracerebral hemorrhage has occurred; if CT normal, go on to duplex then treat by cause, e.g. thrombus or intimal flap means reopening.
- Cranial nerve dysfunction: the commonest is the hypoglossal nerve (4.4-17.5%, Cameron 12th 2017); if injured, the tongue deviates to that side, with difficulty speaking and chewing (a problem for those who like to chew); the most serious CN injury is the vagus nerve, caught when clamping the vessel, since if both sides (RLN) are injured, airway obstruction results; other nerves that may be injured: marginal mandibular branch of CN7, glossopharyngeal and spinal accessory nerve; most are temporary. Figure: nerves near the carotid (skip).
- Myocardial infarction: the most common cause of death after CEA.
- Airway obstruction: usually from hematoma compressing the airway; a serious problem; these patients are usually hypertensive and on anticoagulant, so bleeding is more likely; management: open the neck and evacuate the clot at once.

## Carotid Artery Stenting (CAS)
- Though CEA is still the preferred treatment, in some cases CEA is not suitable; CAS is a very good option for patients with significant comorbidities not fit for GA; technology has developed so that today's stents are self expandable and the stent tip has a filter acting as embolic protection so debris does not go to the brain; CAS results are now so good that some centres do CAS more than CEA.
- Comparison of CEA and CAS from pooled 12 RCTs with 4-year follow up: CEA had lower stroke rate and lower death rate than CAS; while the incidence of cranial nerve injury and postprocedure MI is less in CAS than CEA; among carotid stenosis patients with or without symptoms, CEA has higher efficacy with fewer complications such as periprocedural stroke than CAS. Figure: CEA vs CAS with distal embolic protection (skip).
- Indication:
  1. High risk patients (poor condition): 1.1 age 80 or more (not 70); 1.2 heart disease, e.g. recent MI, CHF class 3, 4; 1.3 lung disease FEV1 under 1; 1.4 kidney disease creatinine over 3.
  2. Anatomical problem: 2.1 lesion too high, so proximal control is hard; the cut-off is C2: if above C2 use a stent; 2.2 distal lesion lower than the clavicle; 2.3 contralateral ICA occlusion; 2.4 previous CEA on the same side then recurrent stenosis, hard to redo, risk of plaque dislodging; 2.5 hostile neck, e.g. previous RT to the neck, previous neck surgery such as MRND, previous tracheostomy tube in place or scar; 2.6 contralateral RLN palsy.
- Figure (table, conditions qualifying patients as high surgical risk for CEA): Anatomic factors: high carotid bifurcation (above C2 vertebral body); low common carotid artery (below clavicle); contralateral carotid occlusion; restenosis of ipsilateral prior CEA; previous neck irradiation; prior radical neck dissection; contralateral laryngeal nerve palsy; presence of tracheostomy. Physiologic factors: age 80 or more; LV ejection fraction 30% or less; NYHA class III/IV CHF; unstable angina CCS class III/IV; recent MI; clinically significant cardiac disease (CHF, abnormal stress test, or need for coronary revascularization); severe COPD; end-stage renal disease on dialysis.
- Physiologic factors, small changes from the old numbers: 1. LVEF under 35% (as the author states; the table says 30% or less); 2. MI: wait 6 weeks: if within 6 weeks, do not choose CEA; 3. CHF NYHA class 3, 4: do not choose CEA; 4. previous contralateral RLN injury.
- Contraindications to CAS:
  1. Inability to achieve safe vascular access. 2. Severe tortuosity of aortic arch, CCA, ICA. 3. Intracranial aneurysm or AVM requiring treatment. 4. Heavy or extensive carotid calcified lesion. 5. Extensive middle cerebral artery atherosclerosis. 6. Visible intraluminal thrombus (angiographic defect). 7. Complete occlusion. 8. Long segment stenosis over 2 cm. 9. Life expectancy under 5 years or ECOG 3, 4. 10. Major stroke within 4 weeks. 11. Renal dysfunction (cannot inject contrast). 12. Cannot give ASA (a stent just put in would clot).
- Currently tortuous vessel and long segment stenosis have become relative, because new stents have come out so CAS can be done.
- Summary: in carotid stenosis, with or without symptoms, vascular guidelines still hold that CEA seems safer and more effective than CAS; but the choice of method should also consider other factors, especially lesion character and location and the experience of the operator.
- Complications to watch with a femoral sheath puncture site: 1. about day 4 after the stent, fever with red skin at the groin like cellulitis, may palpate a nonpulsatile mass at the puncture site: this is a deep infection involving the closure device; 2. after femoral access, pain and numbness in that leg, pulse not palpable distal to the puncture site, leg cold: think of thrombotic occlusion of the common femoral artery from local intimal injury.
- Figure (algorithm, Management strategy for patients with carotid stenosis), as steps: carotid stenosis -> duplex ultrasound -> symptomatic stenosis 50% or more, or asymptomatic stenosis 80% or more? No: conservative management: antiplatelet therapy, BP under 130/80 mmHg, LDL cholesterol under 100 mg/dl. Duplex inconclusive: MR angiography, CT angiography or contrast angiography. Yes: assess risk for surgery: low risk: endarterectomy or carotid stenting within a trial/study + antiplatelet, BP, LDL as above; high risk: carotid stenting or endarterectomy based on local expertise + antiplatelet, BP, LDL as above.

## Carotid Artery Aneurysm
- Patients are usually obese, hypertensive older women; present with pulsatile neck mass, local compression, or some with TIA or stroke.
- Commonest site: bifurcation of the CCA; beware misdiagnosis with a dilated tortuous right subclavian artery and proximal CCA.
- Causes: 1. degenerative; 2. pseudoaneurysm; 3. infected aneurysm; 4. post carotid dissection.
- Investigation: start with duplex, follow with MRA: 1. duplex US screens and helps diagnosis; 2. CTA and MRA help diagnose and separate a tortuous vessel. Figure: aneurysm pre- and post stenting (skip).
- Treatment: once diagnosed, should treat: excision; ligate or stent, choose as convenient.

## Carotid Dissection
- Causes: 1. trauma (a blow, or sometimes truly minor, just coughing or neck twisting causing dissection); 2. spontaneous, found rarely.
- Symptoms: cerebral ischemia such as weakness of one side, aphasia, amaurosis fugax; also neck pain and headache. Figure: dissection with tear in inner layer (skip).
- Investigation: start with CT brain to rule out hemorrhage first; if not, follow with duplex at the neck; if flap or thrombus is suspected, confirm with MRA.
- Treatment: heparin followed by warfarin; choose the length of follow up; but if progressive, e.g. TIA occurs, choose between PTA + stent, or open and ligate the ICA, or bypass.

## Vertebrobasilar Insufficiency
- Site of occlusion: usually near the origin of the vertebral A; if only one side is stenosed, symptoms are few; those with symptoms probably have both sides stenosed; causes: trauma, or a fibrous band, or bone spur.
- Symptoms: dizziness, ataxia, disequilibrium, vertigo, blurred vision, diplopia, some with tinnitus.
- Visual symptom commonly found: bilateral homonymous hemianopsia, unlike carotid disease, which is usually unilateral amaurosis fugax.
- Figure: circle of Willis and major arteries serving the brain (skip).
- Investigation: 1. MRA or 4-vessel and arch angiogram, which are very good for diagnosis and planning treatment; 2. duplex often gives poor anatomic detail.
- Treatment: 1. unilateral, not severe, nondominant site: give antiplatelet therapy; 2. bilateral with symptoms needing revascularization: choose PTA + stent, vertebral transposition to subclavian A, or transsubclavian endarterectomy.
- Key: symptoms of dizziness and diplopia, beware this group of diseases.

## Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)
- Definition: focal dilation of the aorta over 50% of normal size; normal aorta is 2 cm; over 3 cm diagnoses AAA.
- M : F = 5 : 1; age over 60 years.
- In people over 50 years the chance of AAA is 3%, rising with older age, smoking, male sex, family history of AAA.
- Normal average annual growth of AAA about 0.2 mm per year [as printed].
- Causes: 1. atherosclerosis, the most common, from degeneration of connective tissue; 2. trauma; 3. arterial infection or inflammation.
- Shape: 1. fusiform: found most; dilated all around like a spindle; usually from degenerative change (atherosclerosis); 2. saccular: found less; dilated on one side like a sac; usually caused by infection; saccular ones rupture easily. Figure: fusiform vs saccular (skip).
- Associated disease:
  1. Multiple aneurysm: 1.1 40-50% of popliteal aneurysm have AAA; 1.2 50-90% of femoral aneurysm have AAA; 1.3 20-25% of thoracic aneurysm have AAA; 1.4 conversely, AAA patients have iliac aneurysm 25%, and thoracic, femoral or popliteal aneurysm 3-12%.
  2. Renal artery stenosis 20-30%. 3. Celiac or SMA stenosis 22%. 4. IMA occlusion 80%.
- Risk factors: the most important are older age and smoking; next COPD, female sex, high BP, high lipid, DM, carotid artery stenosis, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, kidney disease with creatinine over 2, and a family member with AAA.
- Medical disease most associated with AAA: coronary artery disease.
- AAA classification, by location related to the renal artery (figure: suprarenal, pararenal, juxtarenal, infrarenal):
  1. Infrarenal: found most, 90-95%; the AAA lies below the renal artery.
  2. Juxtarenal: the AAA's infrarenal neck is less than 10 mm from the renal artery, not yet at the renal artery orifice.
  3. Pararenal: the infrarenal neck is right next to the renal artery.
  4. Suprarenal: AAA with involvement of at least 1 main renal artery arising from the wall of the AAA.
  5. Thoracoabdominal: aneurysm involving both thoracic and abdominal aorta, i.e. suprarenal AAA.

## Clinical Manifestations
- Asymptomatic: found most often, frequently by incidental US.
- On abdominal examination it may be hard to feel in obese patients; in thin patients, a pulsatile mass may be palpated that may just be a tortuous aorta, but CT confirms; AAA size is rather large.
- Classic physical finding: a pulsatile expansile mass in the epigastrium; pulsatile = beating mass; expansile = expansion to the sides, upwards and downwards, in all directions. Figure: pulsatile abdominal mass and CT with extensive mural thrombus (skip).
- Routine physical examination has low sensitivity; the smaller the AAA, the harder to detect: 1. size 3-3.9 cm: palpable 29%; 2. 4-4.9 cm: 50%; 3. over 5 cm: 76%.
- So most asymptomatic AAA is diagnosed by imaging studies such as US or CT done for other reasons, then an AAA is found by chance.
- Symptomatic cases: may come with pain from inflammation or impending rupture; some with local compression: compressing the stomach and duodenum causing GOO, or the ureter causing hydronephrosis; some with distal embolization; symptomatic AAA is an indication to treat, whether open repair or EVAR.

## Investigation
- Ordered by ease/difficulty; our job is to choose appropriately.
- General labs: assess the patient's state before treatment: kidney, lipids, diabetes; help diagnose infection.
- Plain abdomen: detects occult AAA in about 20% by seeing wall calcification, displacement of adjacent structures, or loss of psoas shadow. Figure: films with calcified aortic wall (skip).
  - Advantage: kept as differential for suspected rAAA but cannot rule out other surgical abdomen such as PU perforation.
  - Disadvantages: 1. cannot tell the location of AAA exactly; 2. cannot tell whether rAAA occurred; 3. does not show iliac aneurysm.
- US: noninvasive, no radiation, no contrast, cheap and very available; US should be the initial investigation for diagnosis or screening for AAA, looking at AP diameter; with duplex even better. Figure: US of AAA (skip).
  - Advantages: 1. initial investigation to confirm when AAA is suspected; 2. screening and follow up of AAA size; 3. look at associated disease, e.g. gallstone.
  - Disadvantages: 1. operator dependent; 2. obese people or bowel gas make diagnosis difficult; 3. does not give details such as proximal extension, renal artery and iliac artery are not seen clearly.
- CTA: the single best study, especially spiral, seeing 3 dimensions, excellent for planning treatment, so much that angiograms have faded. Figure: CTA axial and 3D (skip).
  - Advantages: 1. tells size very accurately; 2. preoperative evaluation: tells anatomy, especially the neck of the AAA and the renal artery, whether there is an associated iliac aneurysm, and assesses mesenteric vessels; 3. shows a thick vessel wall with fluid or air around, suggesting inflammation of the vessel; 4. used in rAAA when the patient is still stable; 5. used for diagnosing endoleak; 6. helps rule out other causes of abdominal pain, e.g. acute appendicitis, acute pancreatitis, mesenteric ischemia.
  - Disadvantages: unstable patient is not suitable; radiation exposure and contrast nephrotoxicity; expensive; not suitable for screening or follow up.
- MRA: information generally similar to CTA; suits patients with renal insufficiency or contrast hypersensitivity; sees tissue around the AAA better than CTA; disadvantages same as other MRA studies. Figure: MRA (skip).
- Angiogram: not used as routine now besides CTA being unclear, or in cases where EVAR is done and angiogram is needed; a thrombus inside the lumen of the AAA makes angiogram size inaccurate. Figure: angiogram (skip).
- Iodinated contrast nephrotoxicity: found often in patients with prior renal dysfunction, especially diabetics, more in type 1 DM than type 2; the best prevention of contrast induced renal dysfunction is adequate hydration with 0.45% NSS, giving urine output often before sending for angiogram.

## US Screening
- Following patients over time, 100 untreated AAA: 40% die of AAA rupture, MI and CVA about 30% each; elective treatment is certainly better than treating at rupture.
- US screening program reduces death from rAAA, best figure 42% in 4 years; for screening AAA US is enough, no need for CTA or MRA.
- Criteria for selecting people to screen: 1. all men who smoke; 2. both men and women aged 65-75 years with family history of AAA.
- For the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) 2005: screen AAA as: 1. men aged 65-75 with a smoking history: screen once by US; 2. women not necessary.
- When AAA is found by screening and there is no indication to operate, schedule follow up as below (may differ slightly from the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) 2018 guideline):

| Diameter (cm) | Follow up with US |
|---|---|
| 2.5 to <3 | recheck after 10 years (some centres do not follow since it is not yet AAA) |
| 3.0-3.4 | every 3 years |
| 3.5-4.0 | every 1 year (SVS still says every 3 years) |
| 4.0-4.9 | every 12 months |
| 5-5.4 | every 3-6 months |

## Risk of AAA Rupture
- In asymptomatic AAA patients the first step is to assess rupture risk; if AAA ruptures, over 50% die.
- Risk of rupture is higher in: 1. size over 6 cm; 2. expansion or growth rate over 0.5 cm per 6 months or over 1 cm per year; 3. age over 60 years; 4. women; 5. comorbid, e.g. HT, COPD, low FEV1; 6. smoking; 7. family history of AAA; 8. saccular shape; 9. on steroid.
- Interesting: black race does not increase risk of rAAA.
- Risk of rupture related to diameter (size is the most important predictor):

| Diameter (cm) | Rupture risk (% per year) |
|---|---|
| 3-3.9 | 0.3% |
| 4-4.9 | 0.5-1.5% |
| 5-5.9 | 1-11% |
| 6-6.9 | 11-22% |
| >7 | >30% |

- Factors influencing risk of AAA rupture:

| | Low risk | Average risk | High risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diameter | <5 cm | 5-6 cm | >6 cm |
| Expansion | <0.3 cm/yr | 0.3-0.6 cm/yr | >0.6 cm/yr |
| Smoking | none | moderate | severe |
| COPD | mild | moderate | severe |
| HT | normal | controlled | poorly controlled |
| Family history | no | one relative | numerous relatives |
| Shape | fusiform | saccular | very eccentric |
- Timing of rAAA: 1. size over 5.5 cm: the average time to chance of rupture is about 18 months; 2. size over 7 cm: about 9 months.

## Management of AAA
- To choose the method, 4 considerations:
  1. Aneurysm rupture risk.
  2. Elective operative mortality: at best the operation should be done at a high volume center by a high volume surgeon, then operative mortality is about 5%.
  3. Life expectancy: the patient should live more than 2 years after treatment; those with carcinomatosis or bedridden, looking miserable, should be left alone.
  4. Patient preference: in theory, we must give both open repair and EVAR information to the patient in full detail.
- Nonoperative management: whatever method is chosen, the patient must stop smoking, exercise, reduce all risk factors, control DM, lipids, BP; nonoperative management is also suitable for those in very poor condition with short life expectancy, e.g. carcinomatosis or CVA bedridden fed by NG until death: just control BP, especially with beta blocker; although this cannot reduce size or prevent expansion in humans, it still helps lower mortality.
- Indication for open repair or EVAR (Rutherford 9th ed 2018):
  1. Asymptomatic AAA: man size over 5.5 cm; in women 5.2 cm.
  2. Asymptomatic AAA with growth rate over 0.5 cm in 6 months or over 1 cm in 1 year.
  3. Rapid growth.
  4. Symptoms from AAA, e.g. abdominal pain, back pain, or compressive symptoms, regardless of size.
  5. Complications, e.g. rAAA, fistula, distal embolization causing limb ischemia.
  6. Infected AAA (infected ones show saccular shape).
  7. Inflammatory AAA.
- Only open repair and EVAR are written here; minimal incision aortic surgery (MIAS) and laparoscopic repair are more for fellows.
- Early EVAR in AAA smaller than the indication: large RCTs from the UK and USA studied AAA diameter 4-5.4 cm and found that the early intervention group had no long-term survival benefit compared with the group that was observed and operated only when indications arose.
- Figure (ESVS 2011 non-ruptured AAA algorithm), as steps: consideration for repair -> two parallel assessments. (1) Assessment of fitness: fit for repair: optimisation -> AAA repair, open or EVAR; unfit: optimisation -> if becomes fit, repair; if still unfit for open or EVAR: manage conservatively. (2) Assessment of morphology: suitable for EVAR (standard or custom) vs not suitable for EVAR (open: transabdominal or retroperitoneal; or laparoscopic); depends on patient preference and centre preference; over 8 cm urgent: open.

## Ruptured Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (rAAA)
- rAAA is the major problem of AAA and the most common cause of death in untreated AAA.
- 50% die before reaching the ER, and when they reach the ER, a young resident fumbling through consulting the seniors step by step may lose another 50%; so survivors are very lucky.
- In patients who reach hospital, if the doctor is slow or lazy: 80% survive up to 6 hr; 50% up to 24 hr; 30% up to 6 days; 10% up to 6 weeks; most die; so whatever is done, be quick.
- Only 15-50% of rAAA patients knew they had the disease; most diagnosis depends on symptoms and examination.
- So a patient over 50 years with nontraumatic shock, abdominal pain, back pain, must always be thought of as rAAA.
- Albert Einstein had AAA for 7 years; one day in 1955 it ruptured and he died; the author mentions it as a story, not for the exam.
- Classic triad of rAAA: 1. severe back pain 70%, severe abdominal pain 50%, sudden and severe; 2. hypotension, a history of syncope 45%; 3. expansile pulsatile abdominal mass: in an obese patient or one in shock we may not palpate it.
- Posterior rupture: found 87%, since tension at the aortic wall is highest posteriorly, so most rAAA patients come with back pain more than abdominal pain.
- Anterior rupture: 12%, free rupture into the peritoneal cavity: dies for sure.
- A very small group (under 1%): rupture into the IVC or into the bowel (aortoenteric fistula).
- Some cases have flank pain thought to be a kidney stone, but actually rAAA with blood irritating the ureter. Figure: operative photo (skip).
- Investigation: if rAAA is suspected, begin with bedside US in the ER to help diagnose AAA while resuscitating the patient; the advantage of US also helps exclude diseases mimicking rAAA, e.g. ruptured HCC; the limitation: US cannot say whether the AAA has ruptured, losing to CT with IV contrast (CTA); plain abdomen does not help diagnose rAAA.
- Currently if the patient is suspected rAAA with a fairly stable state, not frantic, no shock, systolic BP still over 80: send CT with IV contrast (CTA), which diagnoses rAAA best; next best, plain (non-enhanced) CT is acceptable: if AAA ruptured, surrounding retroperitoneal hematoma is seen.
- CTA also tells the distance between the AAA and the renal artery, and serves as the way for emergency EVAR; contrast enhanced CT gives information about the size of the AAA hole and the relation of the AAA to celiac artery, renal artery, SMA, necessary for EVAR cases.
- CTA signs of rAAA: contrast extravasation, massive retroperitoneal hematoma.
- CTA signs of impending rAAA: crescent sign, irregular aortic wall, etc. Figure: CT of rAAA (skip).
- Differential diagnosis (sudden abdominal pain): 1. PU perforation; 2. acute pancreatitis; 3. gut obstruction; 4. mesenteric ischemia; 5. biliary colic; 6. ureteric stone; 7. MI; 8. aortic dissection; 9. ruptured HCC.

## Management of rAAA
- Two methods of surgery: open and EVAR; which to use depends on the patient's state, aortic morphology (suitable for EVAR or not), and the readiness of the hospital; residents should know basic open repair; EVAR in outline.
  1. Start with ABC, IV, permissive hypotension resuscitation: keep SBP 70-80; as long as the patient's consciousness remains, blood is feeding the brain enough; restrict fluid a bit, do not overload; SBP should not be pushed over 100 mmHg, for fear of more bleeding; ask the patient's relatives whether the patient has known AAA or not.
  2. Draw blood for simple labs and crossmatch blood, FFP, platelets 6-10 units; for imaging, plain film helps when abdominal or back pain resembles GI pain but is not very useful in rAAA; US is used often in the ER, seeing AAA, but the problem is that whether AAA ruptured or not is hard to tell from US; the most accurate is CT, especially with contrast, seeing extraluminal contrast clearly.
  3. Before sending to CT, we must see whether the patient is stable or not: with SBP over 80 and looking OK, send CTA aorta from neck to groin; if not stable, struggling, continue hypotensive resuscitation; at this time bring an EKG to run, to see MI or not; remember: an unstable patient must not be sent to CT; the place the patient must go is the OR, for resuscitation and preparation for surgery; if bedside US has not been done, perhaps do bedside US in the ER or OR, or gently palpate as a preliminary evaluation first.
  4. Unstable rAAA:
     - 4.1 In the past, open straight away: prep from nipple to knee, place an arterial line and Foley (see whether urine comes out, and prepare to measure bladder pressure), NG to prevent aspiration and help us palpate the aorta in the abdomen for clamping.
     - 4.2 Atmosphere: keep warm; beware hypothermia; drape the patient first, and induce anesthesia only after that; because at induction muscle relaxant is given and when we open the abdomen the tamponade is lost, so BP drops.
     - 4.3 Before induction, if there is an aortic balloon occlusion catheter, use it: inject local anesthetic at the groin, insert the aortic balloon occlusion catheter into the common femoral artery directly or by cut down, then pass the wire up to the descending aorta under fluoroscopy above the rAAA position; inject contrast into the balloon so that it inflates for proximal control; once bleeding decreases, induction can begin.
     - 4.4 If not available, or the aortic balloon occlusion is not usable: open a long midline from xiphoid to pubis straight away; the key step: proximal aortic control by cross clamping the aorta directly at the supraceliac level, or using a T bar to press; be calm; bleeding will lessen, BP rises gradually, the teacher relaxes, and when there is a reason to scold, the resident can fix it quickly.
     - 4.5 Slowly dissect the AAA off the duodenum and left renal vein, then place the clamp below the renal vessels.
     - 4.6 Then proceed to open repair of the AAA.
  5. Minor issue often asked: the technique of supraceliac control: answer in steps and with a knowing face: Figure: A-D (gastrohepatic ligament and crus):
     - 5.1 Identify the aorta and esophagus at the diaphragmatic hiatus; palpating the NG tube helps a lot.
     - 5.2 If the left lateral segment of the liver is in the way, mobilize it to the right.
     - 5.3 Open the gastrohepatic ligament to expose the crus of the diaphragm; at this time we can palpate the aortic pulse.
     - 5.4 Use fingers or the tip of a suction to gradually dissect around the aorta to make room for the clamp or T-bar.
     - 5.5 Use the left index and middle fingers as a guide, each finger on the side of the aorta.
     - 5.6 Clamp the aorta in AP direction above the celiac A; then use another vascular clamp to clamp the aorta below the renal artery; then release the supraceliac cross clamp.
     - Do not forget: practise placing fingers with a vascular clamp.
  6. Heparinization: in rAAA patients, do not give, especially in cases with massive bleeding with a chance of hypothermia, even more bleeding.
  7. Mannitol: seen being used more than heparin, since during shock or while we do supraceliac cross clamp, visceral organ hypoperfusion, especially of the kidney, often occurs; mannitol 12.5-25 gm helps promote diuresis and is a scavenger of oxygen free radicals.
  8. After opening the aneurysm sac, the surgical steps are like elective open repair; during surgery watch e.g. distal pulse, and the color of the sigmoid colon; in rAAA, watch as the same.
  9. These cases: 70% can have the abdomen closed, 30% cannot be closed and closing would surely cause abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS); the minority group should have an open abdomen, safer, temporarily covered with mesh or plastic bag; an interesting point: patients who undergo open repair often have an open abdomen so ACS is less; while EVAR does not open the abdomen; if before EVAR we loaded much fluid, beware ACS after EVAR.
  10. rAAA still stable: with time, do EKG and CTA first; if a choice exists, choose EVAR, which has mortality of only 19%, less than open, which has mortality of 36%.
- For rAAA in a centre with readiness: a vascular surgeon of expert level, a team, instruments, enough to keep BP and the anatomy of the AAA suitable for stent graft placement.
- In the past emergency EVAR had clearly lower mortality than open, but now many RCTs comparing EVAR with open found the outcomes not much different: 30-day mortality rate is similar, post op complications not much different; most reports conclude that EVAR is better in having a shorter operation time, less blood loss, and needing less blood transfusion only.
- A textbook author at a top centre (Ann Arbor Vascular Center, Michigan) says that in their institution, if possible, they choose EVAR first; but in most of Thailand, rAAA still must be open as before.
- Approach after seeing that the morphology of the aorta can use EVAR, steps:
  1. Move the patient to the OR, supine, drape first.
  2. Percutaneous common femoral artery puncture, or a cutdown under local, injecting xylocaine at both groins.
  3. Place a guide wire, assess, followed by intraaortic balloon occlusion under fluoroscopy; then blow the balloon above both renal arteries, higher than the rupture position, which usually ruptures at the posterolateral wall on the left below the renal artery about 2-4 cm.
  4. Next step, check access to see whether the AAA is of suitable morphology: 4.1 suitable: do EVAR; 4.2 unsuitable: open repair.
  5. In cases where EVAR can be done, do it on the side opposite to the leg with the aortic balloon occlusion; the new generation of surgeons place an aorto uni-iliac stent graft, but older surgeons whose bones are hard may place a bifurcated graft.
- For those who want to read about EVAR in rAAA: 1. Cameron 12th p.917: vascular centres use EVAR up to 40% (guideline easy to understand); 2. Seminars in Vascular Surgery Dec 2010.
- Figure (Guideline from Cameron), as steps: strong clinical suspicion of rAAA: stable? -> CTA -> aortic endograft candidate? No: open repair; Yes: fluoroscopy available in the operating room? Yes: endovascular repair; No: open repair. Unstable: fluoroscopy available in the OR? Yes: endovascular repair; No: open repair.
- Figure (Emergency ruptured AAA repair algorithm, from a high-volume EVAR centre): permissive hypotension = a state of consciousness with systolic BP over 90 mmHg [as printed]. ## Open Repair
- Still used even though EVAR has gotten cheaper and new; in some cases EVAR cannot be done and one must go open, e.g. unstable patient, no EVAR expert at the hospital.
- Indication for open repair: 1. ruptured AAA that is an unstable case; 2. inflammatory AAA; 3. unsuitable morphology for EVAR; 4. good risk patient who can tolerate surgery.
- Incision:
  1. Anterior abdominal approach: 1.1 midline: suits thin people; 1.2 transverse: suits obese people; sees the renal A well, but opening toward the lower side is hard.
  2. Retroperitoneal approach.
  3. Left thoracoabdominal approach: suits rAAA that is almost dead: hurry open the chest and clamp the aorta like EDT [emergency department thoracotomy], then open the abdomen and deal with the AAA.
- From RCTs: giving perioperative beta-blocker can reduce mortality and morbidity from cardiac events.
- For elective AAA, juniors need to know only midline and retroperitoneum. Figure: incisions for the two approaches: A transperitoneal, B retroperitoneal; the retroperitoneal approach can be modified for higher exposure on the visceral aorta (skip).

### Anterior Abdominal Approach
- Thin patients with a flat abdomen: midline is popular: incision from just below the xiphoid to above the pubis; but if obese with a pendulous belly, a transverse incision a little above the umbilicus.
- Advantages: 1. wide exposure: can extend into the left chest; 2. quick, suits rAAA.
- Disadvantages: 1. long wound, very painful, hard to breathe, many lung complications, long ileus; 2. long hospital stay, long ICU stay, long recovery after surgery.

### Retroperitoneal Approach
- Patient lies in right lateral position, incision on the left side about the 9th-10th ICS.
- Indications: 1. inflammatory aneurysm; 2. juxtarenal or suprarenal AAA (remember); 3. obese; 4. hostile abdomen, e.g. previous abdominal surgery or colostomy; 5. kidney in the wrong place, or renal ectopia; 6. horseshoe kidney; 7. COPD patient whose AAA morphology is not suitable for EVAR.
- Advantages: 1. fewer complications such as ileus, fluid loss, and lung problems; 2. less BP drop in rAAA cases than midline.
- Disadvantages: 1. control of the right iliac vessel is difficult (remember); 2. cannot evaluate the rectosigmoid.

### Basic Surgical Technique
- Figure A-F (aortic aneurysm repair steps): A incision line over the aneurysm; B left renal vein and right common iliac artery; C incision of sac; D clotted blood evacuated (lumbar bleeders oversewn); E aortic stump, Dacron graft, left common iliac artery; F graft sutured inside the sac (anastomosis).
- Steps of open AAA repair (elective):
  1. Long midline incision from xiphoid to pubis.
  2. General exploration for other disease (e.g. if a colon mass is found, what to do; or gallstone: cholecystectomy at once? see later section).
  3. Exposure of the aorta: read elsewhere.
  4. Clamp the IMA with a bulldog, and look again at the end to decide whether it needs reimplantation.
  5. Tell the anesthetist to prepare; clamping is near.
  6. Systemic heparinization 80-100 units per kg; wait a moment, then prepare to cross clamp.
  7. Start distal control first, to prevent distal embolization.
  8. Then place the proximal clamp in the AP direction; if the proximal neck is high, it may be necessary to cut the left renal vein near the IVC, since blood can still drain via the gonadal, lumbar and adrenal veins.
  9. Open the anterior sac of the aneurysm, toward the right, to avoid the sympathetic plexus.
  10. Evacuate the thrombus; oversew lumbar arteries; bleeders with Prolene 3-0; then place a tube or bifurcated graft of 16-20 mm.
  11. Proximal anastomosis with Prolene 3-0, end to end.
  12. Complete the upper anastomosis first before the lower one; release the proximal clamp to flush out any clot from the upper side, then do the distal anastomosis to the CIA; before the distal one is done, release the distal clamp to see back bleeding; pass a Fogarty catheter through both distal sides as well.
  13. When releasing the clamps, BP drops and reperfusion injury occurs, so release one side at a time, coordinating with the anesthetist.
  14. Suture the sac to cover the graft, then close the peritoneum over the sac again.
  15. Close the abdomen.
- Notes:
  1. Coordinate with the anesthetist throughout, from the ER to the OR.
  2. Heparinization: 2.1 systemic: give before clamping the aorta, except rAAA, which tends to bleed massively, or those with coagulogram problems; 2.2 local heparinization in elective cases: mix heparin 5,000 units with NSS about 500 cc, inject into the distal iliac A on each side about 150 cc each; rAAA with bleeding problems: do not give.
  3. Must preserve at least 1 hypogastric A, to avoid vasculogenic impotence.
  4. After anastomosis, go back to the bulldog on the IMA: if the left colon or sigmoid oozes dark, it shows the IMA must be reimplanted onto the graft.
  5. Result if the patient does not die from surgery: about 35% need help from caregivers, 5% become bedridden, 25% develop impotence.

## Endovascular Repair (EVAR)
- Place a stent graft through the groin (femoral) A into the AAA sac under fluoroscopy. Figure: EVAR steps (skip).
- EVAR is very hot now, with new devices; where EVAR is possible, centres do EVAR in 2/3 of cases, more than open.
- Advantages of EVAR: less invasive, lower morbidity and mortality, less blood, quicker postoperative rehabilitation than open, shorter hospital and ICU stay; time of 2-4 hours; while open takes 5-6 hours; follow up at 2 years: survival of EVAR is the same as open.
- Report from the Medicare national database of more than 300,000 patients, elective open vs EVAR: 30-day mortality in women who had open repair is higher than EVAR; in men, 30-day mortality is not different between the 2.
- Disadvantages of EVAR: 1. contrast nephropathy; 2. endoleak; 3. 1/5 of EVAR cases need a second EVAR in the future; 4. expensive, many instruments; 5. operator must be well trained; 6. lifelong follow up.
- Morphology criteria for EVAR (do not guess), figure with measurements:
  1. Proximal aortic neck diameter 18-32 mm: proximal landing zone fits tightly.
  2. Proximal neck length (thrombus free) 10-15 mm (Schwartz uses over 15 mm).
  3. Distal aortic diameter at the aortic bifurcation should be over 18 mm to place a bifurcated graft.
  4. Neck angulation: measure the angle between the aortic axis and the AAA axis under 60 degrees; the straighter the neck, the better the fit; should not be funnel (conical) shape.
  5. No intraluminal thrombus over 1/4 of the circumference and thicker than 2 mm: because more than this, the stent will not grip and type 1 endoleak occurs, increasing distal embolization risk.
  6. No significant heavy calcification (plaque), because the stent will not grip tightly.
  7. CIA to be used as distal attachment zone must be normal: no aneurysm or stenosis from extensive calcification, not tortuous, no thrombus, iliac angle under 90 degrees: 7.1 length of thrombus-free CIA over 20 mm: the longer the distal landing zone, the tighter; 7.2 CIA diameter 8-22 mm; 7.3 EIA and femoral artery diameter over 7 mm so the endovascular device can be passed.
  8. At least one CFA not smaller than 0.7 cm, because it is the route to place the introducer.
- Stent graft: choose a graft about 20% larger than the aorta; 2 designs: 1. single leg (aortouniiliac stent graft) for unstable or complex anatomy, often needing a fem-fem crossover bypass; 2. two legs (bifurcated aortic stent graft) for easy anatomy and hemodynamically stable.
- Introducer: inserted through the femoral A, percutaneous or cut down.
- Proximal: fixed to the aorta below the renal A. Distal: fixed to the common iliac A; must preserve internal iliac A flow in at least 1 side.
- During the procedure, do intraoperative angiogram several times, and before finishing inject dye once more to see the position of the stent and whether there is an endoleak.
- For juxtarenal and pararenal AAA, or where the infrarenal proximal aortic neck is shorter than 1 cm, EVAR can still be done with chimney, snorkel, periscope, octopus technique, or a fenestrated graft with holes that line up with renal artery and SMA. Figure: chimney, periscope, octopus, sandwich (skip).
- Follow up: 1. lifelong, because the aneurysm shrinks, which may shift the endograft; 2. use CTA: in the first year 4 times: at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 1 year, then CTA every 1 year.
- Indication for conversion from EVAR to open repair (about 10%): 1. during a hard operation, unstable rAAA; 2. persistent endoleak, treated more than 2 times and still not fixed; 3. endograft migration until iliac flow obstruction; 4. endograft infection.
- Role of EVAR in the future: 1. though large RCTs found early intervention does not add long-term survival, some suspect that AAA under 5 cm may benefit from EVAR because the long-term result looks better than doing it when over 6 cm (still to be argued); 2. newer endografts released can reduce morphology limits, e.g. grafts that can be used with aortic neck angle up to 90 degrees; also with side branches to maintain flow to visceral A.

## Endoleak
- The most common complication of EVAR: caused by blood leak at the attachment zone into the space between the inner wall of the aorta and the outer wall of the stent graft.
- Classification (figure and Table 2): Type 1: graft attachment leaks: 1A proximal end of endograft; 1B distal end of endograft; 1C iliac occluder. Type 2: retrograde blood flow arising from patient arteries: 2A inferior mesenteric artery; 2B lumbar artery. Type 3: graft defect: 3A junctional leak or modular graft separation; 3B fabric disruption. Type 4: graft wall porosity resulting in flow through fabric of graft. Type 5: endotension: sac pressurisation without evidence of an endoleak.
  - Be interested in type 2 (may be asked).
- Type 1: from the stent seal against the aortic wall not tight, from a neck too short or angulation too much, so blood still goes into the aneurysm sac, which is very dangerous; 1a leak at the proximal end; 1b leak at the distal end; 1c leak at the iliac occluder.
  - Treatment: do not leave the OR; use a balloon to press the graft tight against the aortic wall.
- Type 2: the type found most often: leakage of blood from a branch of the aorta: 2a leak from IMA; 2b leak from lumbar A.
  - Treatment: 1. if the patient is stable, wait for CTA surveillance every 6 months; 2. but if unstable or there is expansion, treat: options: clipping via laparoscope or endoscope, or superselective embolization; if cannot be controlled, open surgical repair. Figure: CT of lumbar artery type II endoleak, coil embolization (skip).
- Type 3: leakage at the stent graft joints: 3a leak at the disjunction; 3b fabric tear.
  - Treatment: do not leave the OR; place a stent graft to close the tear.
- Type 4: leak from the fabric of the graft itself; rarely found now since products are much better; the leak shows as slow bleeding oozing; this type often closes by itself; just follow up.
- Summary: 1. endoleak type 1, type 3: must treat at once because chance of rupture is high, including unstable type 2; 2. endoleak type 2 that is stable, including type 4: follow up with CTA every 6 months.
- Currently type 5 has been added: endotension: pressure in the aneurysmal sac keeps rising so the aneurysm keeps enlarging without any blood leak seen entering. Treatment: surveillance, and if necessary reintervention with endovascular treatment, putting in a new stent; if not possible, conversion to open repair.

## AAA Associated Abdominal Diseases
- GI disease found together with AAA may be found before surgery, or found during surgery, or when going in for GI disease, an AAA is found by hand.
- No guideline yet: depends mainly on clinical judgement whether to do together or later.
- Principles to consider:
  1. Should not do together.
  2. Whichever is urgent and life threatening: do first.
  3. Assess the risk of the AAA: 3.1 is there a high chance of rupture; 3.2 is the risk of infected graft high if done together; 3.3 if necessary to do together: can EVAR be done? because EVAR's advantage is that the peritoneal cavity remains virgin and clean enough to then do the GI procedure.
- Emergency condition:
  1. rAAA: do AAA first. 2. GI perforation: do GI first. 3. Both emergencies together (a rare coincidence): AAA ruptured with perforated PU too: thinking about death already; the author asks only for the AAA that is near rupture, e.g. diameter over 7 cm, and a perforated stomach: treat the stomach perforation first, may be lap or open; and the AAA done by EVAR on another day.
- Elective AAA repair together with GI pathology that can wait: treat the AAA first; GI another day.
- Explore GI pathology and then incidentally find AAA: do the GI pathology first; then call the vascular surgeon, or if it is a general hospital, then do not operate yourself: look at the patient's face, if tired, the mass may rupture after the operation; do EVAR or refer.
- AAA & concurrent colon cancer: principle: try for staged procedure; AAA, the teacher chooses EVAR:
  1. AAA large or ruptured and the cancer still small: treat the AAA first.
  2. Cancer with obstruction, perforation, bleeding, or a very large mass while the AAA can still wait: treat the cancer first; AAA another day.
  3. AAA size over 6 cm and colon cancer, about 70% of each: discuss who should take the patient to be treated first between vascular and procto.
- AAA & gallstone: generally both Rutherford and Greenfield agree that if both can be done together, it can be done (the author does not agree), but he prefers LC on another day:
  1. If doing together, it should be multiple small stones or evidence of previous chronic cholecystitis: do the AAA first; if open repair, ask for closing the retroperitoneum over the graft neatly, then do the cholecystectomy.
  2. Silent GS: do only the AAA, then follow up GS; if symptoms from GS occur in the future, then do LC another day.
  3. The author prefers 2-staged: EVAR first, then LC another day when ready.
- AAA & unexpected widespread metastatic cancer or unexpected intraabdominal infection or abscess: treat the GI problem, keep the AAA for another day; or if the patient looks tired for a long time, consult, give the prognosis, prepare the relatives; better work.
- AAA & prostatic cancer: treat the AAA first; TUR another day.
- AAA & lumbar disc: treat the AAA first; wait for the spine surgery.
- AAA & renal or ovarian malignancy: can be done together, since they believe it does not add risk of graft infection, but if done, after the AAA is finished, close the retroperitoneum neatly and the patient must be stable; do radical nephrectomy or TAH + omentectomy next; the author's opinion: if doing together, the AAA should be EVAR.

## Infected AAA
- In the past called mycotic aneurysm, which was not correct, since at that time antibiotics had not developed; found that over 90% came from bacteria, not fungi, hence the name mycotic; all have saccular shape of AAA. Figure: operative wall necrosis of the aneurysm (skip).
- Currently classified by underlying mechanism into 4 types: 1. mycotic aneurysm; 2. microbial arteritis with aneurysm; 3. infected preexisting aneurysm; 4. post traumatic infected pseudoaneurysm.
- Some add 2 more groups: aortic infection from a contiguous organ, e.g. infection in the abdominal cavity from intraabdominal or retroperitoneal organ; and HIV related AAA.
- Mycotic aneurysm: found in patients aged 30-50 years; mortality rate 25%.
  - Etiology: septic embolus from the heart in SBE, then blocks the vasa vasorum of the abdominal aorta; bacteria: number 1 staph, number 2 salmonella.
- Microbial arteritis with aneurysm: infection directly at the vessel wall; found more often than mycotic aneurysm; patients are usually elderly with many comorbidities, with atherosclerosis or on hemodialysis.
  - Etiology: hematogenous spread of bacteria onto an atherosclerotic aorta until the intima is infected and the aorta bulges; bacteria: number 1 salmonella, number 2 staph, strep. Mortality 75%.
- Infected preexisting aneurysm: found in people over 50 years who already have an AAA; etiology: bacteria in the bloodstream, or septic emboli entering the bloodstream and landing at the AAA until it becomes infected; bacteria: salmonella and staph; mortality 90%.
- Infected pseudoaneurysm: cause from trauma to a vessel, or from drug abuse; the common organism is S. aureus.
- Clinical manifestations: need a high index of suspicion in elderly patients with many diseases (DM, HT, kidney disease), or immunocompromised hosts hard to build immunity, with atherosclerosis; present with fever of unknown cause, abdominal pain, pulsatile mass, prolonged ileus; plain film sent for abdominal pain in a patient with known AAA may show erosion of the lumbar vertebra.
- Hemoculture: positive in about 50%; if positive it helps, usually staph or salmonella; but if negative it does not rule out.
- CTA: the diagnostic test of choice: haziness from retroperitoneal soft tissue edema, fluid or air around the aorta, halo enhancing wall; early on it may still look normal: serial follow-up CTA: in infected AAA size is found to increase very fast. Figure: CT with saccular aneurysm of the infrarenal aorta, periaortic soft tissue attenuation and fat stranding, infected aortic aneurysm confirmed at surgery (skip).
- WBC scan: used very little now, only when CTA is not clear.
- Treatment:
  1. Antibiotic before and after surgery, but how long is still not clearly concluded; in general at least 6 weeks IV to control infection, then oral antibiotic follows; some continue until 1 year; some give lifelong survival without operating, with a future mortality from infected AAA as high as 80%.
  2. Surgical treatment: aneurysmectomy as definitive treatment, but the debate still not over is the arterial reconstruction:
     - 2.1 If the aorta looks very inflamed, with a pus collection, or the degree of tissue around the retroperitoneum is quite severely infected, the organism more virulent, together with the patient's condition (elderly, toxic, unwell) predicting a poor course: resection of the infected tissue, copious saline irrigation, closure of the aortic stump, then extraanatomical bypass (axillobifemoral). Figure: arterial reconstruction through uninfected tissue planes (skip).
     - 2.2 In situ reconstruction: Criteria: 1. must not be grossly infected, especially pus in the retroperitoneum; 2. a low virulent organism such as staph epidermidis; 3. the repair technique must not be difficult: suprarenal AAA often has visceral artery involvement needing implant, so it should not be done in situ.
       - Disadvantage: if tissue culture is positive, chance of aortic stump blowout 50%, and if blowout happens, the patient dies.
       - Graft is of 2 kinds: 1. femoropopliteal vein of the patient, or cryopreserved allograft, to reduce infection, but the disadvantage is that aneurysm may follow later; 2. antibiotic-coated (rifampin) impregnated prosthetic graft or silver coated polyester grafts.
  3. Endovascular treatment: the role is not clear, since the prosthesis goes directly into infected tissue, so infection rate is high, and if it becomes infected, the chance of death is 70%; those who do it hope it excludes the inflammatory sac as a temporary bridge in elderly or high risk patients before going to definitive surgery.

## Inflammatory AAA
- Found 2.5%; patients are mostly male.
- Etiology unclear: may be infection, connective tissue disease; some blame autoimmune.
- It is an infrarenal type with a thick fibrotic process adhering the AAA to the 3rd and 4th parts of the duodenum and the IVC; this fibrotic process usually spares the posterior wall; 25% have ureter entrapment.
- Because the wall is thick, rupture is not common.
- Symptoms: when diagnosed, usually abdominal pain, back pain, weight loss; 1/3 palpate a pulsatile mass; no fever, abdominal pain, like infected AAA.
- ESR elevated.
- CTA: most sensitive and definitive diagnosis: thick wall, fusiform shape, contrast rim enhancement. Figure: CT labelled cuff, thrombus, inner wall (calcified rim), lumen; operative photo (skip).
- Treatment: 1. aneurysmorrhaphy with graft replacement: before surgery ask the urologist to help place ureteric stents first; 2. if EVAR can be done, it is very good.
- After surgery the incidence of graft infection is not increased, the same as general open AAA cases.

## Complications of Surgery of Abdominal Aorta
- Complications after surgery, especially for AAA: 1. systemic complications, e.g. MI, kidney failure, respiratory failure; 2. local complications, e.g. bleeding, colonic ischemia, graft failure, etc.
- Summarised sufficiently; fellows need to know much more.

### Cardiac Complication
- The most common complication, found both early and late post-op.
- Early: usually POD 2; especially MI is found about 20%.
- 50% of patients have no chest pain, but arrhythmia, CHF, then arrest.
- The most important predictors of cardiac outcome: recent MI and recent CHF; in a new MI case, it is dangerous to bring the patient in for AAA surgery.
- Prevention: 1. give fluid, give blood, control BP well; 2. during surgery, at the time of cross clamping the aorta or releasing the clamp afterwards, tell the anesthetist first and be careful.
- Remember: primary cause of death after open repair of AAA: acute early death: MI; late death: renal failure.

### Renal Complication
- Causes: 1. shock from blood loss; 2. in cases with cross clamp above the renal A, especially suprarenal AAA or operating rAAA; 3. declamping hypotension; 4. reperfusion injury: kidney from shock or myoglobinuria.
- Even with hemodialysis, mortality is still up to 50%.
- Best way to prevent renal failure is limited renal ischemia to less than 30 minutes.

### Bleeding
- Causes: 1. medical bleeding: found in patients who received much blood transfusion, or hypothermia followed by coagulopathy; 2. surgical bleeding: 2.1 suture line bleeding; 2.2 bleeding from lumbar A or IMA stump; 2.3 bleeding from a vein beside the aorta or CIA, when we control it and then release the clamp, it oozes.
- Treatment: 1. give FFP, platelet, give blood; 2. massive or continuous surgical bleeding: open and go in to suture or ligate as convenient.

### Limb Ischemia
- Usually occurs at the time of surgery, when thrombus or plaque dislodges to the distal limb; diagnosed by examination; need embolectomy.

### Declamping Hypotension
- Occurs because we release the clamp too fast so BP drops.
- Prevention: before releasing the aortic cross clamp or distal clamp, tell the anesthetist first: 1. before releasing the clamp there must be adequate hydration; 2. release the clamp one side at a time.
- Another cause to always think of: reperfusion injury: from clamping the lower side, the leg is temporarily ischemic when we finish the distal anastomosis and remove the clamp to perfuse the leg; potassium, calcium, lactic acid rush into the circulation quickly: effect on the heart: arrhythmia, BP drop; effect on the lung: ARDS; effect on the kidney: myoglobinuria followed by renal failure.

### Spinal Cord Ischemia
- Found less in AAA surgery; mostly occurs in thoracic aortic aneurysm surgery.
- For AAA it is found in EVAR more than open, because occlusion goes on a bit long, especially in rAAA, which has more than elective cases.
- Blood supply of the spinal cord comes from the artery of Adamkiewicz, between T9-12. Figure: vessels supplying the spinal cord (skip).
- Cause: believed to be lack of blood supply to the spinal cord during surgery: 1. shock during surgery; 2. cross clamping the aorta or renal A, especially rAAA with supraceliac cross clamp, especially clamping the aorta longer than 30 min; 3. unlucky, emboli going into this artery.
- Prevention: 1. do not clamp the aorta longer than 30 minutes, especially clamping above the renal A; 2. thoracic aortic surgery should have an atrial-femoral shunt; 3. somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP): monitoring; 4. CSF drainage: the purpose is to reduce pressure in the spinal canal; still no firm evidence of true benefit, but if used with a shunt it reduces the risk of neurological deficit to 2.4%; the method: put a small catheter in to drain CSF when pressure in the spinal canal rises.

### Sexual Dysfunction
- Sympathetic injury, especially along the left side of the aortic wall: if the nerve is cut, the erection is still firm, but retrograde ejaculation occurs.
- Parasympathetic injury, or interruption of internal iliac blood flow on both sides: erectile dysfunction will occur.
- Besides ANS injury, interruption of internal iliac artery blood flow also causes sexual dysfunction.
- Prevention: 1. be careful at dissection along the left side of the aorta and left CIA; 2. must preserve the IIA of at least 1 side so that blood still flows into the pelvis.

### Colonic Ischemia
- Cause: inadequate left colon perfusion.
- In open repair found about 2-3%; in EVAR also found, from IMA occlusion by the graft (1%); this condition has high mortality.
- Predisposing factors: 1. rAAA: found about 30%, with chance of death up to 73%; 2. preop chronic mesenteric ischemia or aortoiliac occlusive disease; 3. IMA ligation in a patient with SMA occlusive disease; 4. also found in elderly, or interrupted collateral from colectomy or previous pelvic radiation; 5. preop hypotension; 6. failure to maintain perfusion to at least one IIA.
- In rAAA, the most important predictor of colonic ischemia is the presence of preoperative shock.
- Colon location involved most: sigmoid colon, then rectosigmoid junction 75%; the rest splenic flexure and right colon.
- Symptoms: found usually in the early post-op period, within the first 24 hours up to 4 days: patient with bloating, watery diarrhea, LLQ pain, fever, plain film shows small bowel ileus; but if bloody diarrhea comes out and the abdomen is rigid and distended, it shows the bowel is necrotic.
- Diagnosis: a patient who has bloody diarrhea after an aneurysm repair: hurry to scope with flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy: diagnosis accuracy over 95%.
- Remember: mesenteric angiogram is not useful, since in AAA surgery we always ligate the IMA.
- A problem debated at the moment: whether to send everyone for sigmoidoscopy after surgery, or after EVAR, as routine: the answer is not necessary: mostly watch symptoms; if suspicious then do it.
- Treatment:
  1. Cases with a rigid abdomen and peritonitis already: an indication for explore laparotomy, no need to scope first; these cases are usually transmural involvement; at surgery, resect the colon with questionable viability, then bring out an ostomy; if the patient survives, take it back some days later.
  2. Cases without peritonitis yet: do flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy to look at the colon mucosa, which is often only a mucosal lesion:
     - 2.1 Mild mucosal ischemia: oozing mucosa, some may have petechial hemorrhage; treatment: NPO, IV antibiotic covering aerobes and anaerobes; avoid drugs that cause arterial spasm, e.g. vasopressors; then observe symptoms; most patients' symptoms improve in 7-10 days: if stable, no fever, no abdominal pain, bowel sounds present, begin to eat, if the patient eats well, discharge; after discharge, long term follow-up is needed, since these cases may develop stricture.
     - 2.2 Severe transmural involvement: patients usually have bloody diarrhea, rigid abdomen with peritonitis; scope shows black mucosa with slough and ulceration, or CT shows gas in the bowel wall: if so the colon usually will perforate; explore laparotomy and resect the ischemic bowel; do not anastomose; bring out an ostomy.
- Summary, indication for surgery: 1. peritonitis already; 2. CT shows gas in the bowel wall; 3. scope finds severe transmural involvement; 4. scope finds mild mucosal ischemia but while observing the patient worsens in the direction of strangulation.
- Prevention (see the technique summary in open repair of AAA): 1. do not destroy collateral circulation; do not dissect more than necessary; 2. IMA ligation: do not ligate far from the AAA wall; place the stitch right at the orifice, on the inner side of the aorta; 3. preserve the internal iliac A to maintain collateral from below in at least 1 vessel; 4. if enlarged meandering A is seen, forbidden to ligate it.
- Indication for IMA reimplantation (Carrel's patch):
  1. Orifice of the IMA looks patent, but back bleeding from the IMA is weak and very little (poor), not pulsatile.
  2. SMA stenosis or occlusion: you will see an enlarged meandering A.
  3. Bilateral internal iliac A occlusion.
  4. IMA stump pressure under 40 mmHg.
  5. Absent doppler signals on the serosal surface of the sigmoid colon.
  6. Intramucosal colonic pH under 6.86.
- No need for IMA reimplantation if: 1. the color of the sigmoid colon is not dark after releasing the bulldog clamp on the IMA put on at the start; looking at the sigmoid color again, it still looks good; 2. IMA has strong back bleeding, pulsatile spurting; 3. IMA occluded: no back bleeding at all shows that the left colon already receives enough blood supply from collaterals; 4. IMA stump pressure over 40 mmHg; 5. doppler still gets a signal at the marginal artery of the colon.
- Figure (algorithm, colon ischemia after aortic surgery), as steps: clinical suspicion + predisposing factors: patient with possible colon ischemia after aortic surgery -> peritonitis? Yes: laparotomy. No: ischemia on endoscopy? No: evaluate for alternative diagnoses. Mild, mucosal only: additional indications for surgery? Yes: laparotomy; No: medical management: improves: long-term follow-up (stricture); worsens: laparotomy. Severe, full thickness: laparotomy.

### Graft Failure
- Early failure: within 30 days; usually from technical problems, e.g. a hole at the stump, intimal flap, bleeding, embolization, kinking of the graft, or infection causing graft thrombosis.
- Intermediate failure: found 1-24 months; usually from fibrointimal hyperplasia at the distal anastomosis or at a venous valve if we use a vein graft.
- Late failure: found after 24 months; usually from recurrent progressive atherosclerosis.
- Early graft failure, especially immediate post-op: the observation is usually one limb occlusion; cause: technical problem; treatment: immediate re-exploration through the groin.
- For immediate or late failure: the patient presents with claudication, rest pain or gangrene: investigate first with duplex, CTA or MRA, then treat: angioplasty or bypass.
- Prevention: 1. proximal anastomosis high, close to the renal A; 2. distal anastomosis to a good run-off vessel; 3. the graft joined must not be tense, or too long so that it kinks; 4. beware external compression where the graft passes the inguinal ligament in aortobifemoral bypass.

### Aortoenteric Fistula
- Primary: from a graft placed that erodes into the bowel; the cause is usually infected graft from contamination of germs in that area (local infection, not bacteremia), with the local infection resulting in a pseudoaneurysm penetrating into the GI tract. Figure: duodenal defect and aortic aneurysm (skip).
- Aortoenteric fistula usually occurs about 2-6 years after surgery; early ones are usually unrecognized duodenal injury.
- Classic triad (found 30%): 1. UGI bleeding over 80% (most common); 2. abdominal pain; 3. sepsis.
- Fistula occurs at the 3rd and 4th parts of the duodenum, because that position happens to cross the graft.
- Early presentation: herald bleed: small bleeding in small amounts, bleeds briefly then stops, because peristalsis and clot temporarily block the hole; after a while the clot comes off and then massive bleeding, death.
- 5% of patients are unstable and cannot go for any investigation; must be dragged into the OR, fighting for the first moment (the patient dies).
- Those who are stable should be investigated:
  1. Upper endoscopy: the best; push down to the 4th part; will see fresh blood at the 3rd and 4th parts, mucosal ulceration, or graft exposed. Figure: endoscopic photos (skip).
  2. CT scan: air around the graft or perigraft fluid, loss of tissue plane between aortic graft and duodenum, pseudoaneurysm, leaking of oral contrast, thickened duodenal wall; MRA can be used too.
  3. Angiogram: used little now.
- What should not be done as the cornerstone is upper GI series, because besides rarely finding the fistula, the barium that remains obscures subsequent CTA or angiogram.
- Treatment: once diagnosed, must operate; otherwise it will certainly recur.
  1. Unstable: send to the OR, drag in like rAAA, hurry to control hemorrhage; in the OR use aortic balloon occlusion if available, but if not, go in for supraceliac cross clamp, distal control, excise the graft, debride nonviable aortic wall, then close the proximal and distal aorta with nonabsorbable suture; for the duodenal defect: close in the usual way, or if the fistula is large and hard to close, bring the jejunum up to join the proximal duodenum; then close the abdomen first, and assess the legs: some cases have the aorta occluded this way and the legs are not black, perhaps because the original procedure was a case of aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD) where aortobifem was done, with lots of collateral: the legs are not black, just observe; but if the legs look ischemic and the patient's condition is still OK, we should do extraanatomic bypass (axillo-bifem); but if the patient's condition is very poor, and cannot tolerate bypass, wait for amputation of the leg.
  2. Stable: luckily the bleed stops like this, continuing like a village head who got applause from the committee; this group should be done as a two-stage approach, which gives the surgeon less stress:
     - 2.1 Start with axillobifem first, about 2-3 days, to feed blood to the legs.
     - 2.2 Then take the patient to open the abdomen: aortic graft excision, with closure of the head and tail of the aorta, and close the duodenal defect.
     - 2.3 But some cases that have little retroperitoneal contamination, the bowel not very edematous, the patient still stable: in situ graft replacement gets much more cheering support, besides avoiding aortic stump blowout, it also reduces stress from multiple operations.
     - 2.4 At the end of surgery do not forget to use the omentum to wrap or separate the duodenum from the graft to prevent erosion.
- Treatment that should not be done at all: only local re-anastomosis with closing the duodenal fistula alone, which guarantees a leak, and the patient will die.
- The role of EVAR is still not OK, because there is a chance of infection and rebleeding is high.
- Postop: IV antibiotic for 6 weeks, followed by oral antibiotic for another 6 months.
- Figure (algorithm, GI bleeding in patient with suspected aortoenteric fistula), as steps: "Herald bleed", stable: upper GI endoscopy (observe third and fourth portions of duodenum): positive: consider aortography or spiral CT; negative: CT scan (MRI?) -> positive: consider aortography or spiral CT; negative: additional studies: negative: stable, no bleeding: observation; rebleed: exploratory celiotomy. If positive (aortography/spiral CT): extra-anatomic bypass, several days, graft excision. Unstable with continuing hemorrhage: emergency celiotomy: no aortoenteric fistula identified: treat other pathology; aortoenteric fistula: graft excision: unstable: viable lower extremities: close and observe, continued ischemia: amputation; nonviable lower extremities: consider in situ or extra-anatomic revascularization if minimal contamination; stable: extra-anatomic bypass.

### Aortocaval Fistula
- Etiology: 1. 90% spontaneous, e.g. pressure from AAA; 2. 10% from trauma, disc surgery.
- Symptoms: AAA patient comes with high output cardiac failure: blood from the aorta flows through the fistula into the IVC, so a lot of blood returns to the heart; some cases may have abdominal pain and frank pain.
- Examination: 1. 88% palpate a pulsatile abdominal mass; 2. fast heart rate with widened pulse pressure; 3. machinery murmur or continuous bruit audible; 4. the patient has swelling of both legs, or ischemic foot from the steal problem.
- Diagnosis: by CTA: the AAA is seen with an early venous phase from the aorta running into the IVC directly. Figure: CTA (skip).
- Treatment:
  1. Open repair is still the treatment of choice: 1.1 proximal and distal control at the aorta first to reduce venous return into the IVC; 1.2 followed by proximal and distal compression of the IVC with sponge clamps to prevent air embolism; 1.3 open the aorta, then close the fistula through the aorta with Prolene 2-0 or 3-0; 1.4 place a tube graft to treat the AAA right away.
  2. EVAR has begun strongly because mortality and complications are lower than open; but the problem with EVAR is that flow from a lumbar A may still run into the venous fistula, since case reports are still few; if answering a paper, the author still cheers open more.

### Prosthetic Graft Infection
- Found under 1% but chance of death over 50%.
- Incidence found more in: 1. rAAA over elective AAA; 2. aortobifem over aortoiliac.
- Bacteria: whichever it is, look at the time as the main thing: 1. occurs within 4 months: usually staph aureus, proteus, klebsiella, pseudomonas, usually contaminated at surgery; 2. occurs after 4 months, especially 10 years: usually low virulent organisms such as staph epidermidis.
- Symptoms: abdominal pain, back pain, prolonged ileus, unexplained fever; often symptoms appear 2-5 years after surgery.
- CTA: a sensitive tool in diagnosis: perigraft fluid or air without seeing the retroperitoneal tissue plane.
- Treatment: principle: always take out the infected graft:
  1. Virulent organism, poor patient condition: give IV antibiotic, do axillobifem, then excision of the graft.
  2. If a low virulence organism, not severe, with little contamination: in situ bypass should be chosen.

## Iliac Artery Aneurysm (IAA)
- IAA is a focal dilation over 1.5 times the normal diameter.
- Normal diameter: men 10 mm; women 9 mm.
- Isolated IAA found under 5%, and over 95% are found together with AAA. Figure: angiogram (skip).
- Indication for surgery: 1. all symptomatic, e.g. from compression; 2. size over 3 cm; 3. expansion over 0.5 cm per year; 4. complication, e.g. thrombosis, emboli, infected.
- Treatment: try placing a covered stent first; if not possible, do exclusion with bypass. Figure: embolization coils and aortouniiliac device with iliac limb over 15 mm length (skip).

## Femoral Artery Aneurysm (FAA)
- Normal size of the common femoral artery about 1 cm; FAA means focal dilation over 2 cm.
- Most are false aneurysms from needle punctures, then asymptomatic pulsatile groin mass.
- True aneurysm is found less; if found, screen for AAA and for popliteal aneurysm too.
- Location: CFA 44%, bifurcation 56%, isolated SFA 1%.
- Some may have femoral nerve compression symptoms, since the femoral canal area is narrow; the patient has pain or weakness of the rectus femoris muscle.
- Physical examination diagnoses 2/3.
- Investigation: 1. duplex US is first-line imaging; 2. CTA or MRA used much now. Figure: CTA (skip).
- Indication for surgery: 1. all symptomatic: pain, venous or neural compression; 2. size over 3.5 cm; 3. expansion over 0.5 cm per year; 4. infected pseudoaneurysm, often from IV drug abuse; 5. embolization, thrombosis, or rupture.
- Treatment: 1. in the past, all operative repair, since endovascular treatment directly at the femoral A was not good, because when the hip flexes it bends the stent, making the stent come loose or break; but now newer stents are better, so there is support to try a covered stent first; 2. but if infected pseudoaneurysm: IV antibiotic together with excision or ligation +/- revascularization with autogenous vein graft; 3. cases with no indication for surgery: follow up with serial duplex. Figure: intraoperative view of aneurysm and repair (skip).

## Popliteal Artery Aneurysm (PAA)
- The most common peripheral aneurysm: 95% come from atherosclerosis; patients are usually elderly male smokers.
- Most are diagnosed when size is over 2 cm; 30% have AAA together, femoral aneurysm, and bilateral in 2/3.
- Patients diagnosed with AAA have an associated popliteal artery aneurysm about 10%.
- Asymptomatic 37%; the rest have symptoms: limb ischemia 55%, local compression 6.5%.
- Most common complication: acute arterial thrombosis, then thrombus in the aneurysm dislodges to occlude the lower leg vessels causing limb ischemia, and amputation rate up to 25%; this usually occurs in aneurysm of size over 2 cm; chance of rupture is very small, found only 1.4%.
- A patient who palpates a mass behind the knee, suspecting popliteal aneurysm, should be sent for duplex US to see size and intraluminal thrombus.
- Imaging: duplex US is first-line imaging; MRA or CTA is preferred now; angiography is done less. Figure: CTA right popliteal artery aneurysm (skip).
- Indication for surgery (PAA): 1. symptomatic; 2. size over 2 cm; 3. complication, e.g. limb ischemia; 4. intraluminal thrombus.
- Treatment:
  1. Watchful waiting: little use: except for a mass smaller than 2 cm, no symptoms, a poor risk patient; otherwise, if not necessary do not observe in popliteal aneurysm, thinking of the high chance of problems.
  2. In cases with good inflow and good distal run-off: proximal and distal ligation with aneurysm repair using autogenous vein graft (popular); in cases with poor distal run-off, catheter directed thrombolysis (CDT) or thrombectomy before surgery helps improve outcome. Figure: A-C popliteal repair with bypass (skip).
  3. Endovascular treatment: not very good since PAA has much distortion, including placing a stent right at the knee joint often causes graft thrombosis; use only in high operative risk patients with suitable anatomy.
- Incision depends on the location of the mass: 1. posterior approach: if the mass is in the popliteal fossa; 2. medial approach: if the extension of the mass goes beyond the popliteal fossa, whether proximally or distally.

## Visceral Arterial Aneurysm
- Splenic artery aneurysm: found 60%; most have no symptoms but the serious complication is rupture. Figure: CT and MRI of calcified distal splenic artery aneurysm (skip).
  - Indication for surgery: 1. symptoms; 2. pregnant woman or planning pregnancy regardless of size; 3. pseudoaneurysm post pancreatitis; 4. size over 2 cm or growing during surveillance.
  - How to treat: read in the spleen chapter.
- Hepatic artery aneurysm: most common cause is medial degeneration. Figure: angiogram (skip).
  - Indication for treatment: 1. symptoms; 2. size over 2 cm.
  - Treatment: 1. intrahepatic aneurysm: treat with embolization; 2. extrahepatic aneurysm: try embolization first; if not possible or unsuccessful, open and do excision + grafting. Figure: hepatic artery aneurysm after coiling (skip).
- Renal artery aneurysm: Figure: operative and CT (skip).
  - Indication for surgery: 1. symptoms; 2. HT; 3. size over 1.5 cm; 4. woman planning pregnancy.
  - Treatment: 1. first choice: try covered stent first; if not possible, reconstruction with vein patch; 2. if ruptured: nephrectomy.

## Mesenteric Artery Occlusive Diseases
- Divided into 2 groups: 1. acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI); 2. chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI).

### Anatomy
- Arterial supply of the gut (figure: celiac trunk, SMA, IMA with branches; SMA lying in front of the 3rd part of duodenum and aorta, behind left renal vein):
  - Celiac A.: arises from the abdominal aorta at L1; supplies the foregut: from the stomach to the 2nd part of the duodenum at the ampulla of Vater.
  - SMA: arises from the abdominal aorta 1-2 cm below the celiac artery; supplies the midgut: from the 2nd part of the duodenum to the proximal 2/3 of the transverse colon.
  - IMA: arises from the abdominal aorta 3-4 cm above the aortic bifurcation; supplies the hindgut: from the distal 1/3 of the transverse colon to the intraperitoneal (middle) rectum.
- Collateral pathways (connected to prevent gut ischemia):
  1. Celiac with SMA: via the superior and inferior pancreaticoduodenal A: the arc of Buhler (as printed).
  2. SMA with IMA:
     - 2.1 Marginal A. of Drummond: connects between mesenteric A. along the inner border of the colonic mesentery from the SMA arcade to the IMA arcade.
     - 2.2 Arc of Riolan: connects the SMA with IMA through a branch of the left colic with the middle colic A. at the splenic flexure; normally no blood flows through here except in chronic mesenteric ischemia, when this route opens for blood to pass.
     - 2.3 Meandering mesenteric A.: the arc of Riolan enlarged so it is big; we see dilated and tortuous vessels, showing that mesenteric A. occlusion, whether SMA or IMA, has already occurred.
  3. IMA with IIA or hypogastric A.: via the middle rectal A.
- Mesenteric vasculature is controlled by the sympathetic ANS via the splanchnic N.
- Splanchnic blood flow is about 25-30% of cardiac output and contains 33% of blood volume; about 75% of blood supplies the mucosa and submucosa, while the muscle layer and serosa receive 25%. Figure: normal mesenteric CT and MRA (skip).

### Acute Mesenteric Ischemia
- Mortality up to 50-75% from late diagnosis and treatment.
- Residents should know 4 types: 1. acute embolic mesenteric ischemia (AEMI); 2. acute thrombotic mesenteric ischemia (ATMI); 3. mesenteric venous thrombosis (MVT); 4. nonocclusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI).
- Must have a high index of suspicion and always think of this group in patients with acute severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical findings, with fever, with bloody stool.
- If a case is found early, while the abdomen is not rigid yet: resuscitate then hurry the work-up with plain abdomen to exclude GI emergency such as perforation; if acute mesenteric ischemia is considered, send CTA at once, which will likely tell the cause of the abdominal pain; but if diagnosed late, the patient's symptoms and signs show abdominal sepsis, fever, drowsy and cannot talk, BP drops, leukocytosis, beware, the patient has a chance of rotten bowel or perforation, especially if the abdomen is rigid, clear peritonitis, CT shows pneumatosis intestinalis or air in the bowel wall: this pattern: open the abdomen straight away to go in and cut the rotten bowel.
- Pathophysiology: when the bowel is deprived of blood, it affects the mucosa and submucosa first: 1. within 15 minutes, villi structure begins to break; about 2 hours the villi are all destroyed; 2. within 3 hours, mucosal slough; 3. within 6 hours, transmural necrosis from mucosa to serosa; 4. severity depends on how much collateral there is.

### Diagnostic Studies of Mesenteric Ischemia
- Lab: not sensitive and specific: though the bowel is already rotten, the WBC count in the elderly may be normal; if metabolic acidosis is found, waiting for serum lactate is too slow, making the diagnosis of acute mesenteric ischemia rely on clinical suspicion.
- Plain abdomen: 25% look normal, only thick wall seen.
  1. Done to exclude causes of abdominal pain such as bowel perforation, obstruction, volvulus, since symptoms may resemble mesenteric ischemia.
  2. In the early stage of this disease only adynamic ileus is seen; thumb printing from hemorrhage and edema of the submucosa is found only 5%.
  3. Gas in the bowel wall or in the PV shows the bowel is already rotten; note: portal vein gas in the red circle differs from pneumobilia in gallstone ileus in that the air lies at the periphery of the liver tissue. Figure: films (skip).
- CT whole abdomen: used much more to exclude non-vascular emergency, making one suspect mesenteric ischemia if thick bowel wall is seen. Figure: bowel wall thickening (skip).
- CTA: has a big role: if the patient has peritonitis and a stable state, should do CTA first to confirm mesenteric thromboembolism; mesenteric angiogram, routine in the past, is selective now; CTA takes little time, results fast, sensitivity 96% specificity 94%: seen mesenteric A. and V., bowel wall: if lacking blood, the wall is thin and without contrast enhancement.
  - Advantages of CTA: 1. if embolus is clearly blocking, no need to send angiogram: can drag into the OR at once; 2. follow-up after treating MVT.
  - Disadvantage: cannot treat; while with angiogram we leave a catheter in place to treat NOMI.
- Biplanar mesenteric angiogram: done AP and lateral view, though used less since CTA is stronger and quicker, but may still be needed to confirm the diagnosis if CTA is not clear, in that case with vital signs still stable and no peritonitis yet; angiogram is better than CTA directly: it can treat: whether intraarterial vasodilator infusion, thrombolysis, or angioplasty +/- stent.
- MRA: used less than CTA since it takes a long time: should be used in chronic cases more than acute.
- Duplex US: role in chronic more; acute is not good because bowel gas obscures.
- Upper endoscopy, colonoscopy, or barium enema do not help in diagnosing mesenteric ischemia.

### Biplanar Mesenteric Angiography
- Done in cases where CTA is not clear, or cases that need intervention.
- After angiogram, do not pull out the catheter: if NOMI, give vasodilator at once; also repeated angiogram to see treatment results.
- Figure (angiographic features): SMA embolism; SMA thrombosis; non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia.
- AEMI: 1. abrupt cut off 3-5 cm below the normal proximal SMA from the aorta; the embolus blocks right at the origin of the middle colic A.; intraluminal defect seen called meniscus sign; 2. still see the first few branches that supply the proximal jejunum; 3. SMA itself still normal.
- ATMI: 1. we often see occlusion right at the origin of the SMA arising from the aorta, with no normal proximal SMA as in embolism; 2. both SMA and celiac have atherosclerosis with extensive irregular luminal obstruction together with collateral, while embolism has no collateral; 3. NOMI on CTA: diffuse segmental vasospasm while the SMA trunk still looks normal and then gradually tapers toward the distal part, or pruning of branches; 4. no embolus or thrombus; 5. string of sausage sign.
- Diagnostic laparoscopy and diagnostic peritoneal lavage: done in patients too unstable to move to CTA, with the right to die in the CT scanner; in the ER with a recommendation to inject local anesthetic, poke small holes, insert a camera to look for bowel the colour of rotten meat, collect fluid from the abdomen to examine, to help exclude other acute surgical abdomen causes (rarely done).

### Acute Embolic Mesenteric Ischemia (AEMI)
- Found most often.
- Source of emboli: 1. from the heart, e.g. AF, number 1; next MI, ventricular aneurysm, valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathy, endocarditis; 2. recent angiogram with clot dislodging to block the SMA (a rare one); 3. paradoxical emboli: venous emboli through an intracardiac shunt.
- Location: emboli block the proximal SMA just distal to the middle colic A. Figure: SMA occlusion with embolus (skip).
- Clinical: 1. sudden onset severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical findings (remember this) and rapid progression, the patient deteriorates quickly; 2. no prodromal symptom; 3. history of AF, MI, and about 20% of patients have a history of peripheral A. embolization; 4. no previous history of PAD.
- Treatment: diagnosis starts with heparinization:
  1. Open embolectomy (how: read in the operative approach).
  2. Endovascular treatment and thrombolysis are not popular because they take too long (maybe up to 12 hours) and the bowel would likely die first; except for partial occlusion only. Figure: selective SMA angiogram showing partially occluding embolus in distal vessel and after catheter-directed intra-arterial thrombolysis (skip).
  3. After surgery, must continue anticoagulation.
  4. In a patient who comes at midnight whose clinical picture fits underlying AF and CTA is not available at night: Rutherford advises not to wait for CTA in the morning; take the patient straight to open.

### Acute Thrombotic Mesenteric Ischemia (ATMI)
- Found as number 2.
- Patients have a history of PAD, and 30-50% have a history of chronic mesenteric ischemia, with postprandial pain and food fear before.
- Symptoms: severity depends on how much collateral there is; resembles AEMI (embolism) but onset appears more insidious than embolism.
- Treatment: similar to embolism, but in thrombosis, must do a surgical bypass of the SMA, a package deal.

### Mesenteric Venous Thrombosis (MVT)
- Not found often; divided into:
  1. Primary or idiopathic MVT (80-90%).
  2. Secondary MVT (10-20%): from causes: 2.1 direct injury from inflammation or infection from abdominal inflammation, or from trauma; 2.2 venous stasis, e.g. cirrhosis, CHF, HT, abdominal compartment syndrome; about 40% have a history of DVT; 2.3 thrombophilia, e.g. patient with cancer over 60 years, while causes from protein C or S deficiencies or antithrombin III deficiency are usually found in younger patients; 2.4 taking the pill.
- MVT causes venous congestion, resulting in decreased arteriolar perfusion; the bowel then becomes ischemic.
- Symptoms: 1. usually insidious onset diffuse abdominal pain followed by bloating, constipation or diarrhea, may be acute, subacute or chronic; 2. severity from very little or no symptoms at all, to some severe cases up to rotten bowel.
- Investigation:
  1. CTA: look at the portovenous phase; diagnosis 90%; CTA is currently the gold standard. Figure: CT venous phase showing complete occlusion of the SMV by a thrombus (arrow) (skip). Findings: 1.1 bowel wall and mesentery swell and thicken; 1.2 thrombus seen inside the portal system, called Bull's eye appearance; 1.3 ascites; the SMV hole is seen enlarged.
  2. Mesenteric angiography: now the purpose is for diagnosis, used very little since CT work; seeing slow arterial flow and a filling defect in the mesenteric vein; the remaining role is as a route to treat NOMI.
- Doppler or duplex does not help because the abdomen is full of gas; should be used for surveillance more.
- Treatment:
  1. Admit the patient, NPO, together with investigating the coagulogram.
  2. Mainstay treatment is IV heparin (remember). Figure: CT scan partially occluding thrombus in SMV and 4 months later complete resolution (skip).
  3. Fluid resuscitation, because MVT has quite a lot of fluid shift.
  4. Observation: if the abdomen becomes rigid with peritonitis, take to open the abdomen.
  5. These cases have high recurrence: if not dead, should get anticoagulation for life.
  6. Venous thrombectomy: found no long-term benefit, with high rethrombosis (remember).
  7. Thrombolysis: only small reports of success; looking at it, not worth using (remember).
  8. Endovascular treatment: for acute, no future; currently used in chronic mesenteric ischemia patients.

### Nonocclusive Mesenteric Ischemia (NOMI)
- Think of it in patients in shock with low flow state lying in the ICU, especially after cardiac surgery, or those on hemodialysis.
- Primary mechanism: mesenteric vasospasm (remember) to preserve blood flow to the heart and brain, with the result that the gut lacks blood.
- Risk: 1. heart disease with CHF or cardiogenic shock (low cardiac output); 2. splanchnic vasospasm from shock, or drugs or substances that constrict vessels, e.g. digitalis (remember), norepinephrine, cocaine, ergot; 3. revascularization in a patient with mesenteric ischemia then reperfusion injury; 4. advancing age.
- Remark: hepatitis is not a risk factor of NOMI.
- Diagnosis: from symptoms and the patient's condition, especially if given digitalis: remember: think of NOMI more; if classic angiographic finding with pruning of mesenteric branch vessels is seen, treat as NOMI right away. Figure: angiogram with pruning (skip).
- Treatment:
  1. Treat the cause, fix the risk, and volume resuscitation, treat shock.
  2. Hemodynamic optimization and wean or stop the drugs causing vasospasm, followed by giving dobutamine to help increase cardiac output.
  3. Selective mesenteric arterial catheterization followed by intraarterial infusion of a vasodilator such as papaverine or tolazoline at a dose about 30-60 mg/hr x 1 day; after that do repeated angiogram in 24 hr. Figure: SMA distal spasm and after papaverine, improved filling (skip).
  4. If the abdomen becomes rigid with peritonitis, showing a rotten bowel, open the abdomen; most patients after surgery are moved to admit in the ICU.

## Operative Management for Acute Mesenteric Ischemia
1. Long midline incision; cut the ligament of Treitz; the SMA is seen on the right, near the base of the transverse colon mesentery.
2. Intraoperative assessment: whether what is found is emboli or thrombosis as in the preop diagnosis, or not, because the treatments differ. Figure: usual site of thrombosis (at the SMA origin) vs usual site of embolization (distal, beyond the middle colic); operative photo of embolus of SMA with diffuse bowel ischemia and classic sparing of proximal jejunum and transverse colon (skip).
   - 2.1 Embolism: can still palpate the SMA pulse proximally, and the ischemic bowel usually starts from the proximal jejunum by sparing about 6-10 inches of jejunum; the lower part is ischemic up to the midtransverse colon.
   - 2.2 Thrombosis: cannot palpate the SMA pulse, and the ischemic bowel runs from the ligament of Treitz to the midtransverse colon. Figure: embolism vs thrombosis bowel pictures (skip).
3. If frankly necrotic bowel, black and foul smelling, resect it away.
4. The viable bowel, or the dusky bowel, seen as grey oozing alternating with dark areas: do not hurry to resect the bowel; revascularize first, but beware reperfusion injury.
5. Embolectomy: approach the SMA directly at the root of the mesentery, open the SMA transversely, do bidirectional embolectomy; then irrigate the SMA with heparinized saline and then suture closed +/- patch. Figure: embolectomy steps A-D (skip).
6. After embolectomy, assess bowel viability: where it looks clearly rotten, cut it out; if unsure but only a short segment of bowel, cut it too.
7. The unsure type: if cut it would be massive resection, which postop would surely have short bowel problem: close the abdomen first; then do a 2nd look in 24-48 hr even if clinically the patient looks stable; do the 2nd look as planned: the decision for 2nd look is done in the OR, not evaluated postop.
8. In thrombosis, besides removing the thrombus that blocks, must add revascularization, usually aortomesenteric bypass; for the conduit prefer GSV, avoiding prosthetic graft, because in emergency the chance of infection is high.
9. Arteriotomy in thrombosis: open it along the length of the vessel, not transversely as in embolectomy.
10. In elderly cases whose abdomen is opened and the small bowel and right colon are all dead, no peristalsis, must leave it all out, even if TPN is given after surgery for life, with high output duodenal fistula and defunctionalised colon, they cannot survive; Rutherford advises closing the abdomen and not going for futile treatment: end of life care.
- Intraoperative assessment of bowel viability:
  1. Gross examination: if still viable, pulse palpable, pink colour and visible peristalsis; sensitivity 78%, specificity 91%.
  2. Doppler US of mesentery: listen directly at the antimesenteric border of the bowel, if arterial blood flow is still heard it probably is viable; sensitivity 63%, specificity 88%.
  3. Wood lamp inspection after IV fluorescein: the dead bowel is seen as a non-fluorescein segment over 5 mm; sensitivity 96%, specificity 95%: the best method.
- Figure (algorithm for acute mesenteric ischemia), as steps: clinical suspicion of acute mesenteric ischemia: peritoneal sign present: surgical exploration. Peritoneal sign absent: CT +/- CT angiography or conventional angiography: SMA embolism: surgical exploration; SMA thrombosis: surgical exploration, or thrombolytic if operative risk is high; non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia: intra-arterial papaverine: clinical improvement: observe; no improvement: surgical exploration; mesenteric venous thrombosis: search for hypercoagulable state: anticoagulation with heparin: peritoneal sign: surgical exploration; observe if asymptomatic.

### Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia (CMI)
- Overall not many; hard to diagnose, need a really high index of suspicion.
- Incidence found in: 1. PAD patients with a history of claudication or coronary disease before; 2. more PAD in men, but CMI is found more in women (believable since from Rutherford); 3. history of smoking, HT.
- Vessels often have atherosclerosis; the peak age of patients has 2 ranges: 50 and 60 years.
- Symptoms: 1. postprandial abdominal pain: pain every time after eating (intestinal angina); 2. food fear with much weight loss; if weight does not decrease, do not think of CMI; 3. abdominal bruit not over 50%.
- Pain occurs 10-15 min after meals and lasts 1-4 hours.
- CMI is a multivessel disease with the SMA the dominant vessel: even if only a single SMA is involved it can produce symptoms; bowel infarction is found little since collaterals help.
- Differential diagnosis: 1. PU; 2. gut obstruction; 3. HBP disease.
- Investigation: when a woman with weight loss, thin, with intestinal angina and food fear is suspected CMI, investigate:
  1. Duplex US: the best screening (initial study), done fasting first, before eating.
  2. Imaging: when duplex is not available or the result is equivocal: 2.1 MRA: Rutherford cheers it more than CTA; 2.2 CTA: beware contrast nephrotoxicity; 2.3 biplanar mesenteric angiography: still the standard for definitive diagnosis and treatment plan, but if afraid of the contrast load, use the digital subtraction technique.
- Treatment: used in cases with abdominal pain, bloating, malnutrition, weight loss, and importantly about 40% of CMI patients will develop AMI:
  1. If severe multivessel disease: should do surgical revascularization.
  2. In single vessel disease, mostly SMA occlusion: treatment depends on perioperative risk: whether to do endovascular treatment or surgical bypass; choose percutaneous transluminal angioplasty + stent first; if it fails or cannot be done, then open and bypass.
- Endovascular treatment: whether mechanical thrombectomy or thrombolysis, or a hybrid procedure (open the abdomen first, then the endovascular approach by retrograde open mesenteric stenting); looks suitable for high operative risk patients with single vessel disease, a short-segment stenosis, little calcification; at 1 year early success 90%; but the problem is restenosis found about 50%, with recurrence more than open bypass; complications are few, mostly access site thrombosis, hematoma, infection. Figure: angiograms before/after stenting (skip).
- Surgical revascularization: currently results look better than endovascular treatment: suits multivessel disease with low or moderate perioperative risk; surgery has 5-year patency up to 90%.
- Surgical options:
  1. Transaortic endarterectomy: used in patients with an ostial lesion where the distal SMA is still good. Figure: steps A-D (skip).
  2. SMA reimplantation: not popular because it is hard.
  3. Surgical bypass: done most.
     - 3.1 Antegrade bypass: inflow draws blood from the supraceliac aorta; some call it prograde bypass. Figure: bypass to SMA and renal artery (skip).
     - 3.2 Retrograde bypass: inflow comes from the infrarenal aorta or common iliac A., whichever is usable for the SMA. Figure: CIA to SMA bypass (skip).
  - Normally choose antegrade first, because the inflow is better: the aorta above the renal A. usually has no atherosclerosis and blood flow to the SMA has less problem with turbulence, with less chance of graft kinking or compression than retrograde.
  - Disadvantage of antegrade bypass: the operation is harder, needing much dissection, exposure is difficult, and renal ischemia may occur.
  - A patient planned for antegrade (prograde) bypass should not have high cardiac risk, because supraceliac clamping for proximal control must be tolerated well.
- Conduit: for CMI, an elective case, not urgent, and with no chance of being infected by rotten bowel as in acute mesenteric ischemia: so when using PTFE the risk of graft infection is not much higher than with autogenous GSV graft; prosthetic graft has long term patency about the same; so the trend of CMI is to prefer prosthetic graft more, because it does not kink easily.

### Celiac Artery Compression Syndrome
- Some call it median arcuate ligament syndrome or Dunbar's syndrome.
- Classified in the CMI group: this disease results from extrinsic compression of the celiac A. by the median arcuate ligament at the left crus of the diaphragm. Figure: median arcuate ligament and celiac axis (skip).
- Often found in women aged 20-40 years, with CMI symptoms: intestinal angina or chronic abdominal pain, weight loss; an epigastric bruit may be heard; when investigated by GI endoscopy, looking from mouth to anus, everything looks normal.
- Whoever thinks of and diagnoses this disease must be admitted to be very smart.
- Diagnosis: from the lateral film of the angiogram, external compression of the celiac A. is seen.
- Treatment: 1. explore, release the ligament compression (transect the ligament); 2. may need bypass grafting if stricture.

## Renovascular Disease
- This chapter covers occlusive disease: renal A. stenosis (RAS) and renal fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD).
- Basics: 1. the right renal A. lies posterior to the IVC; 2. 25% have an accessory renal A.; 3. renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (figure):
  - 3.1 The kidney controls BP by regulating ECF; when renal blood volume falls, it stimulates the baroreceptor of the JGA to release renin.
  - 3.2 Renin stimulates angiotensin I, which uses converting enzyme to become angiotensin II at the lung.
  - 3.3 Angiotensin II raises BP through vasoconstriction and also stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete aldosterone, retaining Na and H2O.
- When renovascular occlusive disease occurs: from the vessel narrowing, blood flowing into the kidney decreases, and in the end renin is released.
- The important problem of renovascular occlusive disease is HT: 1. 80% of renal artery stenosis arises from atherosclerosis; 2. 5-20% arise from FMD; 3. a few from aneurysm, AVM, Takayasu, dissection.

| | Atherosclerosis | FMD |
|---|---|---|
| Sex | M > F | F > M |
| Age | >60 | 30 years |
| Site | Lt. > Rt. | Rt. > Lt. |
| Bilateral | 1/3 | - |
| Location | ostia (proximal 1/3) | distal 2/3 |
| Imaging | proximal stenosis | string of beads |
| Surgery | many | PTA + stent |
- Key: patients who should be suspected and sent for diagnostic study:
  1. HT during childhood, especially age under 30 years, or premenopausal woman.
  2. Age over 55 but with severe HT (diastolic over 115).
  3. Age over 50 with BP suddenly rising fast, or sudden accelerated HT.
  4. HT that is stubborn: 3 drugs and still cannot control.
  5. HT with deteriorating renal function, e.g. creatinine rising.
  6. HT with upper abdominal bruit or flank bruit heard.
  7. Sudden unexplained pulmonary edema with azotemia.
  8. HT with US or CT finding unequal kidney size over 1.5 cm.
- Diagnostic study: start by screening with duplex US first; if the picture suggests, duplex shows the renal artery is likely stenosed, then follow with imaging studies, e.g. MRA, CTA, DSA.
- Duplex US: a very good screening test, but the disadvantage is it is operator dependent, with bowel gas obscuring; if duplex is negative but RAS is still suspected, consider MRA to rule out branch or polar RAS.
- MRA: with gadolinium contrast, an accurate diagnostic test with low morbidity; Schwartz cheers it more than CTA; the disadvantage of MRA: the degree of stenosis can be wrongly stated, mostly overestimate more than underestimate. Figure: MRA with bilateral renal artery origins (skip).
- CTA: can still be used if the kidney is still good; but the disadvantage: arterial calcification makes the degree of stenosis inaccurate, plus contrast nephropathy; prevention: if using CTA, give saline infusion first (Rutherford).
- Conventional angiography: still gold standard, but nowadays used less; remains for cases where MRA or CTA gave no answer, or used during intervention.
- After finding true RAS, the next steps:
  1. Unilateral RAS: that kidney is called Goldblatt kidney.
  2. Or bilateral RAS.
  3. Whether the RAS found is the cause of HT or not: in the past, functional study: renal vein renin assay (RVRA) or captopril renography.
- Functional study: since MRA and CTA work so well, giving the degree of stenosis accurately, functional studies are done less now, but know a little because they may still be needed in some cases:
  1. RVRA: put a catheter through the CFV at the leg, with the tip in the renal V.; then draw venous blood from both renal veins and the IVC, then compare renin: if the renin of the side with RAS is 1.5 times the other side, the test is positive; RVRA is still used.
  2. Captopril renal scan: look at renal uptake: if over 10 minutes, uptake is delayed: the blood vessel of that kidney is narrowed and its function is poor; now this test is in the museum: forbidden to answer.
- Currently there are investigation tools that are very good: duplex can tell flow and systolic velocity of the renal artery, then compare with the aorta (pressure gradient), plus MRA which is the best; from the results, if the renal artery stenosis is over 80%, consider it significant; in a good risk patient consider revascularization right away, without a functional study.
- Management:
  1. Medical treatment: must lower BP in every case, helping prevent kidney failure and also as preop treatment; drugs used: ACE inhibitor, angiotensin receptor blocker, calcium channel blocker, beta blocker; for those whose BP is hard to control, may add diuretic.
  2. Surgical treatment for RAS: this era is the era of bypass and endovascular; for Greenfield: do PTA + stent first; results of surgery are not great.
- Indications for renal revascularization:
  1. Failure of medical treatment in controlling BP, or rapidly progressive hypertension.
  2. Degree of stenosis over 70% and with severe HT; beware: treat only if the BP is high suddenly, or normally not a surgical indication.
  3. Pressure gradient over 20 mmHg.
  4. Affected / unaffected kidney renin ratio over 1.5 : 1.
  5. Good risk or reasonable risk.
  6. RAS with rapid deterioration of renal function.
  7. Asymptomatic stenosis but bilateral renal artery stenosis, or a patient with a solitary kidney.

### Surgical Treatment (renal)
- Many options: 1. bypass: choose between aortorenal and hepatorenal bypass; 2. endarterectomy; 3. renal autotransplantation; 4. nephrectomy.
- Endovascular treatment looks more suitable in FMD patients.
- Before 1990, open surgery was the best method; but now endovascular treatment has come on strongly: outcome of endovascular treatment is rather good in the first 3 years, but in the long run, over a long time, results still lose to revascularization.
- Do not forget these patients are in a hypovolemic state: before surgery, hydrate enough.
- Control BP and lipids before surgery well.
- Renal A. bypass: still the most used treatment; inflow draws blood from the aorta or from a visceral artery in the abdomen connected to the renal artery:
  1. Aortorenal bypass: suits cases where the aorta has little atherosclerosis, so when clamping the aorta there will be no distal embolization. Figure: technique for end-to-side and end-to-end aortorenal bypass grafting (skip).
  2. Hepato- or GD- or spleno-renal bypass: suits cases with an aorta with hard atherosclerosis: we use GSV to connect to the hepatic A., a vessel often used as the inflow for the right side; the splenic A. is used as the inflow for the left side. Figure: spleno-renal and hepato-renal bypass (skip).
  - For children, the conduit used for aortorenal bypass is the hypogastric artery, an autogenous artery; but if this vessel cannot be used, may use autogenous vein; or if no vein, and the renal artery is larger than 4 mm, PTFE can be used.
- Transaortic renal A. endarterectomy: used for ostial lesions where the disease lies within 1 cm from the origin of the renal A.; this method is not suitable for cases with transmural calcification of the periaortic area. Figure: endarterectomy (skip).
- Nephrectomy: done little: should be chosen in a kidney that has atrophied already: seen from x-ray, length of kidney under 6 cm, which has poor renal function; this shrunken kidney is a source of renin production: cutting it out is better.
- Renal autotransplantation: used in cases where the occlusion is at a distal location and the need is to preserve that kidney.
- Percutaneous transluminal balloon angioplasty and stenting: now strong and used more; suits cases whose risk is too high for open bypass. Figure: balloon angioplasty and stent in the renal artery (skip).
  - Remember: PTA suits FMD and AIOD TASC II type A; for renal artery stenosis, follow up at 4 years found restenosis after PTA + stent about 10-20% (not bad).

### Management of Bilateral RAS
- Treat only if there is severe HT together with a renovascular lesion that has these features:
  1. If the stenosis on one side is severe, i.e. 80% or more, treat that side first.
  2. If both sides are severe, i.e. 80% or more, even if BP is not very high but renal function starts to deteriorate, treat both sides with endovascular.
  3. If the stenosis is moderate, 60-80%, but the HT is extremely severe, treat both sides with endovascular too.

## Fibromuscular Dysplasia (FMD)
- An angiopathy of medium sized arteries; found most in the renal artery (rank 1), next the ICA (rank 2), and rank 3 the SMA.
- Cause: still unclear; since it is common in women, some blame hormone influence, especially estrogen, but oddly FMD is not associated with the pill.
- Beware: if FMD is found in the renal artery, it is associated with intracranial aneurysm and AAA: do not forget to investigate these.
- Some cases come with HT, lethargy, easy fatigue; the eye exam finds hypertensive retinopathy; in children, some may come to the doctor with failure to thrive.
- FMD has 3 types:
  1. Medial fibroplasia: the type found most, 80-90% (remember); the angiogram looks like a string of beads. Figure: angiogram of renal FMD (skip).
  2. Intimal fibroplasia: found under 10%: angiogram shows a concentric focal band, with smooth narrowing like a string of beads (lesions of stenosis alternating with aneurysm).
  3. Adventitial fibroplasia: found under 1%: no classic angiographic finding.
- Symptoms: like RAS; some cases may hear a bruit at the abdomen or flank.
- Treatment: for FMD, endovascular treatment with PTA gives very good results, especially in patients under 30 years; but if older than 50 years the chance of cure from PTA is only 30%; and using PTA does not need a stent because FMD is not atherosclerosis.
- The problem: whether every diagnosed case must have PTA: the answer is not necessary: the initial treatment is medication to lower BP first; if it fails, then PTA; and if PTA fails, what to do: the answer is bypass surgery, but not endarterectomy.

## Aortoiliac Occlusive Disease (AIOD)
- AIOD is the blockage of the distal aorta and the iliac A.; the site of disease is above the groin (suprainguinal disease); the cause is mostly atherosclerosis; a small part comes from embolism, nonspecific aortoarteritis, or Takayasu's disease.
- Symptoms: depend on the site of occlusion and extent of the disease; patients mostly come with claudication at the hip and buttock together with femoral pulses not palpable; the ABI of the legs is decreased.
- Investigation:
  1. Segmental doppler pressure: at the upper thigh is less than brachial pressure by more than 20 mmHg.
  2. Color duplex scan: take it out to screen whether stenosis is real; if so, go on to item 2 or 3 as chosen.
  3. MRA or CTA (figure below) to see the type of AIOD and plan the treatment.
  4. Conventional angiography: currently less popular since it needs a needle puncture into the arm artery to guide the wire and catheter, which is rather invasive, and a lot of contrast is needed in the examination; the benefit: helps diagnose where MRA or CTA is inconclusive, and used in cases treated with an endovascular technique.
- Disease classification: 2 eras, the old and the current one; surgeons of the new era use TASC II 2007, which tells treatment options better; the author writes both for reading, in case an old question using the old classification comes.

### Classification, old era
- The old one divides into 3 types by the amount of arterial occlusion (disease pattern), and includes infrainguinal disease in type 3. Figure: patterns of aortoiliac occlusive disease: Type I is confined to the aortoiliac and common iliac arteries; type II is more extensive abdominal disease; type III is multilevel disease.
- Type 1 lesion: found 5-10%: think of type 1 in a patient aged 50 years who smokes, especially a woman, with occlusion at the distal abdominal aorta and proximal CIA, not yet down to the EIA; this occlusion lies only in the abdomen; in this type it is associated with hyperlipidemia more than DM and HT.
  - Symptoms of type 1 (teachers like to ask): 1. symptoms of lack of blood to the legs are still few; we do not often find rest pain and tissue loss; may hear a bruit in the lower abdomen; 2. some come with trash foot (blue toe syndrome) or microembolization running down to block the distal vascular bed; 3. some come with Leriche's syndrome, which consists of: 3.1 thigh and buttock claudication; 3.2 impotence: the penis does not harden, can urinate normally, as if it is the cause, due to ICA occlusion (vasculogenic erectile dysfunction); 3.3 femoral pulse weak or not palpable.
- Type 2 lesion: found 25%: in this type the lesion is more diffuse than type 1, the aorta is involved higher, while the lower side extends down to the EIA, but still suprainguinal disease.
- Type 3 lesion: found most, 65%: the disease progresses lower down; most are multilevel infrainguinal disease; most patients are men with a full set of risks: DM, HT, coronary, cerebrovascular disease; type 3 has the most severe symptoms, usually with ischemia of the leg needing revascularization.

### TASC II AIOD Classification
- Trans Atlantic Inter Society Consensus II was issued in 2007; different from the old one in that: 1. it separates suprainguinal from infrainguinal disease; 2. it divides into 4 types: A, B, C, D; 3. it tells treatment options: whether to operate or put a stent; 4. vascular doctors like TASC II more, because it helps reports to be of one standard and use as a guideline to choose treatment; the disadvantage of TASC is that the detail is too much, hard to remember: the juniors should hurry to pass the board before TASC III comes out and confuses, probably in mid 2020.
- TASC A lesions: unilateral or bilateral CIA stenoses; unilateral or bilateral single short (3 cm or less) EIA stenosis.
- TASC B lesions: short (3 cm or less) stenosis of the infrarenal aorta; unilateral CIA occlusion; single or multiple stenosis totaling 3 to 10 cm involving the EIA not extending into the CFA; unilateral EIA occlusion not involving the origins of the internal iliac or CFA.
- TASC C lesions: bilateral CIA occlusions; bilateral EIA stenoses 3 to 10 cm long not extending into the CFA; unilateral EIA stenosis extending into the CFA; unilateral EIA occlusion involving the origins of the internal iliac and/or CFA; heavily calcified unilateral EIA occlusion with or without involvement of the origins of the internal iliac and/or CFA.
- TASC D lesions: infrarenal aortoiliac occlusion; diffuse disease involving the aorta and both iliac arteries; diffuse multiple stenoses involving the unilateral CIA, EIA, and CFA; unilateral occlusions of both CIA and EIA; bilateral EIA occlusions; iliac stenoses in patients with AAA not amenable to endograft placement.
- Table (TASC morphologic stratification of aortoiliac occlusive disease; endovascular revascularization 3-year primary patency, secondary patency, limb-salvage rates):

| Class | Definition (summary) | 3-yr primary / secondary patency, limb salvage | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall | - | 76%, 90%, 97% | - |
| TASC A | unilateral or bilateral stenoses of CIA; unilateral or bilateral single short (3 cm or less) stenosis of EIA | >80%, >90%, >95% | endovascular revascularization |
| TASC B | short (3 cm or less) stenosis of infrarenal aorta; unilateral CIA occlusion; single or multiple stenosis totaling 3-10 cm in EIA not extending into CFA; unilateral EIA occlusion not involving origins of internal iliac or CFA | 78%, 95%, 100% | endovascular revascularization |
| TASC C | bilateral CIA occlusions; bilateral EIA stenoses 3-10 cm not extending into CFA; unilateral EIA stenosis extending into CFA; unilateral EIA occlusion involving the internal iliac and/or CFA; heavily calcified unilateral EIA occlusion | 73%, 93%, 97% | endovascular or surgical revascularization |
| TASC D | infrarenal aortic occlusion; diffuse disease of aorta and both iliacs requiring treatment; diffuse multiple stenoses of unilateral CIA, EIA, CFA; unilateral occlusion of both CIA and EIA; bilateral EIA occlusion; iliac stenoses with AAA requiring treatment and not amenable to endograft placement | 80%, 83%, 95% | surgical revascularization, except in patients with prohibitive risk |

#### Type A lesion (exam-prone)
- 1. Unilateral or bilateral stenosis of CIA; 2. unilateral or bilateral EIA with length not over 3 cm.
- Treatment: choose endovascular treatment, PTA + stent: 5 year patency rate 80%.
#### Type B lesion
- 1. Short stenosis of infrarenal aorta, 3 cm or less; 2. unilateral CIA occlusion; 3. single or multiple stenosis of EIA with total length not over 10 cm.
- Treatment: choose endovascular treatment first: 5 year patency rate 80%; but if endovascular cannot be done, then surgical bypass.
#### Type C lesion
- 1. Bilateral CIA occlusion; 2. bilateral EIA stenosis 3-10 cm long; 3. spread to ICA and CFA; 4. heavily calcified EIA on one side.
- Treatment: 1. good risk: consider endovascular first, matching the European guideline 2011, which advises endovascular too: 5 year patency rate 65%; but if cannot be done, do open surgical bypass; 2. poor risk: choose endovascular treatment.
#### Type D lesion
- More extensive and diffuse lesion: 1. bilateral EIA occlusion; 2. infrarenal aortoiliac occlusion or diffuse lesion; 3. AAA together with EIA occlusion; 4. still more, tiring to write, summarized as many, only that there is infrainguinal involvement not very long and on one side only.
- Treatment: start with reducing risk factors: stop smoking, control DM, BP, lipids, heart, take antiplatelet; then look at whether there is an indication for revascularization, e.g. severe limb ischemia, or a wound that will not heal:
  1. Surgical treatment, whether bypass or endarterectomy, is the treatment of choice.
  2. Endovascular treatment is an option: reports begin to say long term result is not bad; reports from Thailand say endovascular has a 5 year patency rate of 65%.
- Summary of AIOD treatment in the present time: in TASC-A, B, C, choose the method of dilating with a balloon then placing a bare metal stent to hold it; TASC-D usually must be open, except for those doing endovascular who are experts, and patients who are the highest risk, who may try endovascular treatment first.

### Surgical Treatment for AIOD
- Aorto-bifemoral bypass (ABF): this method has the risk from the operation, the risk of bleeding, but patency is the best: following up 10 years, patency rate 80-95%; suits good risk patients who tolerate surgery.
  - Patient lies supine; abdomen: midline incision; at the groin: vertical incision.
  1. Proximal anastomosis: join as close to the renal A. as possible, because this part of the aorta is less calcified than the lower part; use PTFE size about 18 x 6 mm; join end to end or end to side as convenient, since both give equally good results.
  2. Distal anastomosis: usually join to bifem rather than biiliac, joining to the femoral at the groin is easier, except when the groin cannot be used, e.g. surgical wound or previous RT at the groin; for the lower anastomosis, there must be flow to at least 1 internal iliac artery to prevent vasculogenic impotence and pelvic ischemia; at the femoral anastomosis, be sure the flow of blood runs into the profunda well; may need endarterectomy or profundoplasty added.
  - ABF gives very good results, long term patency 80-90% when following the patient 10 years.
- Extraanatomical bypass: 2 popular methods: 1. axillofemoro-femoral bypass (Ax Fm-Fm); 2. femoro-femoral bypass (Fm-Fm).
  - Use PTFE with ring support; place the graft with a suitable length, not tense, not slack, and not at a place that may be pressed on.
  - These methods suit high risk patients with many diseases: in Fm-Fm a spinal block can be used; and for those with a hostile abdomen not suitable for opening, e.g. much adhesion in the abdomen, previous radiation, or a colostomy.
  - In the long term the patency and blood flow of extraanatomical bypass lose to ABF. Figure: axillofemoral and femoro-femoral grafts (skip).
- Endarterectomy: open surgery to scoop out the fatty plaque in the inner layer (tunica intima) of the vessel wall, then close with a venous patch; now used little, remaining only for occlusion at the aortic bifurcation in a short segment, by removing the plaque from the intima down to the external elastic lamina.

### Endovascular Treatment for AIOD
- Suits short segment iliac lesion: best results are TASC II type A and B.
- Criteria: 1. single stenosis of CIA or EIA not longer than 3 cm, unilateral or both sides; 2. the patient's condition not suitable for surgical bypass, highest risk and short life expectancy; 3. there is a person who can do it. Figure: stent-supported angioplasty of the aortoiliac bifurcation with 8 mm balloon-expandable stents, kissing stents (skip).
- Endovascular treatment has many ways:
  1. Balloon angioplasty: both the ordinary balloon and new things: drug coating balloon (coated with drug to prevent fat accumulation), which gives better treatment results than an ordinary balloon, and cutting balloon, which can cut the fatty plaque.
  2. Endovascular stenting: self expandable and balloon expandable stent; and new things: drug eluting stent to choose from.
  3. Endovascular stent grafting: a prosthetic graft with a wire frame to hold it.
  4. Cryoplasty (cold) followed by balloon angioplasty.
  5. Laser to help cut plaque (atherectomy), or laser angioplasty +/- stent.
  6. Plaque excision.
- In TASC-C, especially TASC-D, where bypass operation is advised more than endovascular treatment because the long term patency is better; nowadays more endovascular treatment is being done, e.g. the technique called CERAB or Covered Endovascular Reconstruction of Aortic Bifurcation: place a covered balloon-expandable stent in the distal aorta first, then a 2nd covered stent into the first stent (kissing stent). Figure: kissing stents vs CERAB (skip).
- In cases with AIOD together with femoropopliteal occlusive disease, we may fix the upper side first, then assess whether the blood feeding to the lower leg gets better, or do both at the same time.
- Hybrid revascularization procedure: a hybrid operation between open and endovascular technique; often a case with both AIOD and femoropopliteal occlusive disease where we decide to do both at the same time: 1. inflow at the upper side, if opening looks too risky, use endovascular treatment, e.g. put a stent at the iliac artery first; 2. outflow at the lower side, use an open operation, e.g. endarterectomy or distal bypass.

## Blue Toe Syndrome
- Or trash foot syndrome: caused by small atherosclerotic emboli, small particles of debris or cholesterol crystals that drop down to block the microcirculation at the tip of the toes; these small emboli usually come from the abdominal aorta or from a branch of the aorta; the most common cause is aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD).
- This condition can occur by itself or follow a vascular procedure. Figure: photo of blue toes (skip).
- Symptoms: 1. the patient has pain at the toes, with the tip of the toe having bluish discoloration; 2. still palpable distal pulse (remember).
- CTA chest and abdomen: look where there is atherosclerosis or an aneurysm that is the primary source releasing the small emboli.
- Treatment: 1. treatment of choice is palliative treatment: give drugs to relieve pain, with observation, because the nature is microemboli; 2. 1/3 of patients need amputation of the black toe; 3. treat the cause, e.g. AAA or AIOD.

## Lower Limb Ischemia
- Classification: 1. Acute limb ischemia (ALI): ischemia occurring within a period of not more than 14 days; 2. Chronic limb ischemia (CLI).
- ALI emphasises embolism and thrombosis; CLI emphasises intermittent claudication and critical limb ischemia.

## Acute Limb Ischemia (ALI)
- ALI means a condition where there is a decrease of blood flow to the arm or leg until symptoms of ischemia occur, in a time not over 2 weeks; found in the lower limb 95%, upper limb 5%.
- Etiology: 1. arterial embolism; 2. arterial thrombosis (atherosclerosis); 3. trauma; 4. others, e.g. fungal (pythium) arteritis, aortic dissection, drug induced arterial spasm, phlegmasia cerulea dolens, peripheral arterial bypass graft occlusion.
- Pathophysiology:
  1. When the leg suddenly lacks blood, what follows: 1.1 thrombus propagation: blood flow stops so it clots and flows back to block along branches and collaterals; 1.2 cells swell, narrowing the vessel more; 1.3 high pressure, compartment syndrome occurs, causing even more ischemia.
  2. The ischemia has effects:
     - 2.1 Nerve: the most sensitive to ischemia: begins with paresthesia (numbness) followed by paresis (weakness) from the nerves controlling the intrinsic muscles of the foot, causing paralysis, so cannot abduct and adduct the toes (the patient cannot move toes and flex the foot); try asking the patient to use the toes to pinch paper: they cannot hold the paper; then the muscle at the next point up becomes weak too; remember: ischemia beyond 6 hours, the nerve usually dies.
     - 2.2 Skin: the second most sensitive to ischemia, in sequence: constricted vessels cause pallor of the skin and coldness (poikilothermia); if more severe, skin mottling is seen, which is blood that lacks oxygen sitting in the vessels; skin colour changes from purple to dark purple; try pressing the skin: if the colour changes to white then returns, it shows it is still reversible; but if fixed staining of skin (black purple) is seen and pressing the skin still leaves it dark as before, it shows gangrene has occurred.
     - 2.3 Muscle: can tolerate ischemia 6-12 hours depending on collateral; symptoms of muscle ischemia begin with pain and numb leg, when lactic acid accumulates, pressing the calf gives calf muscle tenderness: threatened limb; if still not corrected, the muscle becomes rigor, stiff as a board, showing the muscle is already completely dead.
- Clinical of ALI "6 Ps":
  1. Pain: the earliest symptom and the most presenting symptom.
  2. Pallor: begins with pale, followed by mottling, ending with fixed staining: try pressing the skin: if there is still branching mottling, from skin colour changing to purple then white, it shows capillary refill is still reversible; when pressing and the colour does not change over time, it shows fixed mottling or fixed staining, which is irreversible ischemia.
  3. Pulseless: beware, in some cases a pulse can still be palpated from a transmitted wave.
  4. Paresthesia: numbness, little feeling; beware it may be the earliest sign of irreversible ischemia (Rutherford 3).
  5. Poikilothermia: cold about one joint distal to the occlusion point.
  6. Paralysis.
- The location of occlusion: pallor, poikilothermia and pulselessness will be found one joint lower than the occlusion point; for example, an embolus lodged at the femoral bifurcation: the pale cold part of the leg will be below the knee, and the pulse is not palpable from the popliteal pulse downwards.
- Severity of ALI depends on: 1. the amount of collateral flow (the most important); 2. the location of occlusion: popliteal occlusion usually has more severity than anterior tibial occlusion.
- Investigation: if the patient's condition and the severity of limb ischemia are not much and there is time: start with doppler, followed by duplex, followed by imaging as a set; but most cases we meet, the patient usually has severe limb ischemia and cannot wait: must drag into the OR to do on-table intraoperative angiogram.

## Arterial Embolism
- Source of embolus:
  1. Cardiac embolism 86%: in the past AF 70%, MI 25%; now more MI; the remaining few are myxoma, vegetation from heart valves.
  2. Noncardiac source, called arterioarterial embolism, 9%: from plaque stuck at the vessel wall dislodging, e.g. from an AAA.
  3. Unknown 5%.
- AF: clot is found most often in the right atrial appendage (as printed); while MI: clot is in the left ventricle at the apex.
- Location where emboli block vessels most: 1. lower limb 80%: found most at the CFA bifurcation, next the popliteal bifurcation; 2. carotid A. 20%; 3. visceral A. 10%.
- Since most patients survive in real life and board exams, the lower limb is over 90%, so the author writes mainly for the leg.
- Upper extremity embolism: leg : arm = 10 : 1; in the arm, rank 1 is the brachial A. at the bifurcation into radial and ulnar; the cause in the arm is like the leg, AF is number 1.
  - Treatment: treat the leg the same way as the arm.
- We must separate embolism from thrombosis, because thrombosis occurs in a vessel with previous PAD, and the treatments differ: ALI from embolism: just embolectomy; thrombosis: must correct the diseased vessel as well.

## Clinical Stratification of an Ischemic Extremity
- Presented by Rutherford in 1997 to divide ALI patients by urgency and severity for treatment planning; still used now; it uses symptoms together with Doppler signals to tell the severity of ALI.

| Category | Sensory loss | Muscle weakness | Arterial Doppler | Venous Doppler | Prognosis & treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Viable (only cold toes; has pain, cold, pale, pulseless) | none | none | audible | audible | good, elective treatment |
| 2A. Marginally threatened (starts numb) numbness at toes but motor still good | none or minimal (toe) | none | often inaudible | audible | salvageable but must promptly treat |
| 2B. Immediately threatened (numbness quickly spreads) numbness beyond toes, starts motor loss | more than toes with rest pain | mild to moderate | usually inaudible | audible | salvageable but emergency treat |
| 3. Irreversible (does not come back; sleeps and does not wake; the leg does not recover) | anesthetic | paralysis, rigor | inaudible | inaudible | amputation if duration longer than 3 hr [as printed] |
- Simple summary: meeting an ALI patient, begin with doppler artery: if positive, it is Rutherford 1; but if negative, check the vein: if the vein is negative it is class 3; but if venous doppler is positive, it is class 2, then separate 2a or 2b by looking at sensory or motor deficit: only sensory deficit: class 2a; if there is motor deficit: class 2b.
- By Rutherford, those who use it need experience in listening to doppler signals, which differ by person; often new hands being trained think it is 2B or category 3, drag the patient to the OR to cut the leg, and the seniors must vouch.
- Some add capillary return to Rutherford 1997: category 1 return under 4 sec; 2A and 2B delayed; category 3 has no capillary return.
- Compare Rutherford's classification with the Thai one from the Siriraj vascular team, which feels easier to use in practice, assessing easily without Doppler, by looking at skin, nerve, muscle, MOF:

| Rutherford | Siriraj | Skin | Nerve | Muscle | MOF |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Viable | 1 Mild ischemia | pale, poikilothermia (pale and cold) | - | - | - |
| 2A | Moderate ischemia | pale, poikilothermia | paresthesia (tingling, numbness); paresis (toes start weak) | - | - |
| 2B | Severe ischemia | pale, poikilothermia, skin mottling (dark blood spots) | analgesia (no feeling, starts at foot); paralysis (cannot move leg, starts at toes: intrinsic muscle) | tenderness (pain on pressing) | - |
| 3 | Irreversible | fixed staining (completely dark) | - | rigor (swollen, tense) | dark urine, ARF, CHF, ARDS |
- When a motor deficit is found, meaning paralysis, it is at least 2B.

## Management of ALI
- When the history fits ALI with 6Ps: the additional history to ask: claudication, heart disease, risk factors, clotting problem, history of leg bypass, etc.; then examine: palpate pulses, look at skin colour, sensory, motor; use the doppler to listen and tell where the level of occlusion probably is.
- First step: hurry systemic heparinization (remember): to stabilize the clot, prevent clot propagation and preserve collateral circulation: inject UFH IV 80 U/kg followed by 18 U/kg/hr; keep the aPTT at 2-3 times.
- Followed by treating the underlying disease such as AF or MI; give oxygen, IV hydration.
- The patient will have a lot of leg pain, screaming, until relatives want to tear the doctor to pieces: it is advised to give analgesia at once, morphine in small doses, but inject IV, do not inject IM, which can bleed when heparin takes full effect.
- Then divide the severity of ALI by Rutherford 1997 and consider treatment by category:
  - Category 1, 2A: can still wiggle the toes; in this group there is time for investigation and evaluation of the patient to choose the way of treatment: embolectomy, bypass, thrombolysis, PTA; in this category revascularization should be done within 6-24 hours.
  - Category 2B: cannot wiggle the toes, cannot move them, still has much pain, venous doppler can still be heard, cheered as the category in the last stretch before the end, hurry to drag into the OR at once, doing revascularization as fast as possible, no need for imaging study before surgery: hurry emergency thromboembolectomy and intraoperative angiography.
  - Category 3, the late one: the leg has no feeling, cannot move, symptoms fade from neuro deficit, skin fixed staining, pressing the skin the colour does not fade, still green as before, muscle rigor stiff as a board, doppler of both artery and vein silent like a graveyard: this pattern must be amputated at once.
- Problems in treating ALI:
  1. Diagnosis fails because we are busy treating AF, MI, until forgetting to uncover the patient's leg to look; knowing only when the ward changes the clothes or when the nursing assistant who receives the patient says so.
  2. The patient meets the doctor late, waiting for the referral steps, by the time the hospital with a Fogarty catheter that can pull the clot arrives, it is already too late.
  3. Not separating embolism and thrombosis creates a treatment problem, since treatments differ.
  4. The doctor is busy being trendy: the patient is 2B yet sent for MRA wasting 2 hours, or sent for catheter-directed thrombolysis that needs at least 12 hours to dissolve the clot, the leg is cut in the end, or the patient becomes Rutherford 3 with the teacher busy and sleepy not looking, then still sent for revascularization, so reperfusion injury kills the patient, and the doctor dies at the conference.
  5. Heparin not given before dragging the clot.
  6. Looking at the leg but not looking at the heart, so the clot keeps dropping.
  7. Needing to hurry to amputate but still hesitating, waiting for relatives to agree (in Thailand relatives are many, the more they are, the more they fuss, it takes a long time to decide; by the time of deciding the patient is dead).
- Figure (AHA/ACC guideline management of ALI 2016), as steps: acutely cold painful leg -> suspected ALI -> clinical evaluation including symptoms, motor and sensory assessment, arterial and venous Doppler signals (Class I). Audible arterial and venous: Category I (viable limb: normal motor function, no sensory loss, intact capillary refill): revascularization (urgent) and anticoagulation, unless contraindicated (Class I). Inaudible arterial, audible venous: motor function assessment: intact: Category IIa (marginally threatened: slow-to-intact capillary refill, sensory loss limited to toes if present, no muscle weakness; salvageable if treated promptly); impaired: Category IIb (immediately threatened: slow-to-absent capillary refill, sensory loss more than toes with rest pain, mild or moderate muscle weakness; salvageable if treated emergently): both: revascularization (emergency) and anticoagulation, unless contraindicated (Class I). Inaudible arterial and venous: Category III (irreversible: complete loss of motor function, complete sensory loss, absent capillary refill): primary amputation (Class I).

### Role of Anticoagulation
- Preop thromboembolectomy: when diagnosed as ALI, hurry to give IV heparin to stabilize the clot and prevent propagation of thrombus to the distal side down to the occlusion point; besides, heparin reduces continued embolization and the venous thrombosis problem.
  - Dose: IV heparin 80 unit bolus [per kg], followed by 18 unit/kg/hr infusion; check aPTT every 6 hours, keep aPTT at the therapeutic level, about 2-2.5 times the normal.
  - Currently a new group of prostacyclin analogue (Iloprost) is given together with heparin in stage 1 and 2, found to reduce amputation rate in acute limb ischemia.
  - Then give IV drugs to relieve pain, give oxygen, then choose the way of treatment: surgical or endovascular treatment.
- In Rutherford severity 1 or 2a: send vascular imaging first; currently CTA is the imaging of choice; then choose the treatment, whether to drag to operate or endovascular, depending on the patient's condition and all the facilities.
- In Rutherford severity 2b: drag into the OR for intraoperative angiography followed by emergency thromboembolectomy; endovascular treatment (catheter-directed thrombolysis or CDT) should not be used, since it takes days to dissolve the clot, and the leg will be rotten before.
- Rutherford 3 is irreversible already: drag into the OR and cut the leg.

### Postoperative revascularization
- After surgery give an anticoagulant to continue.
- Indication for long term warfarin:
  1. If the cause is from AF or MI and the cause cannot yet be fixed, the emboli will keep being thrown down.
  2. The source of the emboli that dislodged is still not found: should give the drug first.
  3. The patient has a hypercoagulable state, e.g. protein C or S deficiency.
  4. Cases with a graft or stent placed: the trend is it will block easily.
- Role of endovascular treatment: looks suitable for elderly, high risk patients with many diseases, with Rutherford severity 1 or 2a; there are several ways:
  1. CDT (catheter-directed thrombolysis): place a catheter from the opposite side with the tip inside the clot, then continuous infusion of thrombolysis; the drawback of CDT is that it takes time, maybe days, so it is not suitable for 2B and 3, and for patients with contraindication to thrombolytic drugs.
  2. Angiojet rheolytic thrombectomy: inject strong water pressure to break the clot, then suck the clot out.
  3. Percutaneous aspiration thrombectomy: pass a catheter in to the clot and suction out.
- Open surgery is what every resident must know and be able to do; be able to present it fluently.

## Embolectomy Technique
1. After giving IV heparin.
2. Position supine, hip slightly abducted, knees slightly flexed; prep skin from the abdomen, groin, down to both toes; inject xylocaine under local.
3. Incision: the author prefers vertical at the midinguinal at the groin; even if femoral is palpable strongly but popliteal is weak, still make the incision at the groin as usual; add an incision at the popliteal if the clot at the popliteal cannot be pulled out from the upper side and clot remains stuck in the vessels below the knee, usually a case over 24 hours; the incision uses the medial approach, parallel to the border of the tibia.
4. Proximal and distal control of the artery by identifying every branch (CFA, SFA, DFA); choose a position that has no plaque, not hard; pass vessel loops around first; do not rush to clamp at the place of the clot, for fear of distal embolization.
5. Arteriotomy: above the bifurcation about 1-2 cm; avoid the hard place or where there is plaque; what type of opening depends on whether this case needs a bypass or not: if clearly emboli, the vessel has no plaque, looks no need for bypass, or the vessel is small: open transversely, close with a stitch, not narrow; but if the vessel looks hard or it has been many days, the trend is thrombosis more than embolism: will need to do a bypass, or must close the arteriotomy with a patch: open vertically; and if the incision is to be extended wider, vertical is easier; when the vessel is opened and a clot pops out, use forceps to pull the clot out, then do proximal and distal control.
6. Choose a Fogarty balloon catheter of suitable size; insert, pull the clot from the upper side first to assess inflow; then pull the clot from the lower side down to the end at the ankle; pull the embolus out completely, pull repeatedly several times; then infuse with heparin 5,000 units + NSS 500 cc; do not forget to check that the clot is all pulled out with intraoperative angiogram, seeing dye reach the tip of the foot; the author asks for it in every case, because we find residual thrombus 25-40%.
7. Pass the wire into the profunda too; do not forget: the person who inflates the balloon and the person who pulls must be the same person. Figure: Fogarty embolectomy and clots removed (skip).
8. Use heparinized NSS by mixing heparin 5,000 units with NSS 500 cc, inject into both proximal and distal to prevent new thrombus forming.
9. Everything looks fine: close.
10. Points of caution:
   - 10.1 When pulling the Fogarty and the embolus drops out, we see back bleeding spurting; but seeing back bleeding does not say we have pulled out all the clot, because the spurting blood may come from a branch lying proximal to the occlusion point.
   - 10.2 In 10-15% of patients, when the Fogarty is inserted it is hard, cannot pull the clot out; the more one tries to pull, the more trauma to the vessel: take the catheter out and try inserting again: this may be a case of thrombosis, or a delayed case with adherent thrombus; do an intraoperative angiogram to find the cause first; the treatment may be thrombolysis, or 2B and up must bypass.
   - 10.3 After the clot is pulled out and the stitching is done, a person in the OR or recovery room whose pulse is not palpable, with a pale swollen leg: the cause may be a technical problem or new embolus: hurry to re-explore, take them in to open again.
   - 10.4 Beware reperfusion injury and rhabdomyolysis at all times.
   - 10.5 In popliteal embolism, about 90% of cases can have the clot pulled out from the groin incision; but if it cannot be pulled out or not completely, open another incision at the knee to help guide the catheter from the upper side down.
- The clot pulled out: there is a white part called white clot: a clot dislodged from the heart, composed of platelet with fibrous tissue; and a dark red part called red clot, which is RBC formed after the white clot.

## Fogarty Balloon Embolectomy
- Thomas Fogarty is now 86 years old (born 1934); the author gives the story: as a medical student, he made a hollow ball stuck to a wire and pulled an embolus in a test tube; then as a fellow he turned the idea into a tool for pulling emboli; later patented it and used worldwide, with patent fees; he then built a resort and vineyard producing wine under his own name; Fogarty Resort (not for the exam; the author's remarks on this are humorous asides).
- Sizes used: from 2F to 7F, most commonly 3 and 4.
  - 2F: used with air since it is compressible, better than water, can deflate the balloon easily when used in small vessels.
  - 3F to 7F: use NSS to inflate the balloon, because if the balloon bursts there is no air embolism problem.
  - 2F: size 0.67 mm; blow gas 0.2 cc; balloon diameter 4 mm; used with pedal artery and hand.
  - 3F: size 1.0 mm; blow NSS 0.2 cc; balloon diameter 5 mm; used with radial, ulnar, tibial, profunda.
  - 4F: size 1.33 mm; blow NSS 0.75 cc; balloon diameter 9 mm; used with axilla, brachial, femoral, popliteal.
  - 5F: size 1.67 mm; blow NSS 1.5 cc; balloon diameter 11 mm; used with iliac.
  - 6F and 7F: balloon can expand up to 13-14 mm.
- Proper size (approximately): 1. iliac: use No. 5; 2. femoral: use No. 4; 3. profunda, popliteal: use No. 3; 4. tibial: use No. 2.
- Before the board exam, especially 4th year residents, go and tear the document attached with the Fogarty catheter to read.

## Arterial Thrombosis
- Treatment is more troublesome than embolism, because besides taking out the clot, one must also fix the abnormality of the blood vessel, and there are several options:
  1. Surgical bypass: suits good risk cases, Rutherford 2A, 2B with a good distal run-off and contraindication to thrombolysis.
  2. Thrombolysis: advantages: reduced endothelial trauma, complete clot lysis in branches or small vessels, gradual clot dissolution so the incidence of reperfusion injury decreases.
  3. Mechanical thrombectomy.
  4. Combination.

## Thrombolytic Therapy
- Lytic agent has 3 generations: 1st generation: streptokinase and urokinase; 2nd generation: T-PA and rt-PA; 3rd generation: TNK and r-PA (reteplase).
- Action: activation of plasminogen to plasmin on the fibrin surface, causing degradation of the fibrin clot.
- Streptokinase: half life 80 minutes; advantage: easy to get, cheap; disadvantage: allergic reaction.
- Urokinase: half life 15 minutes; disadvantage: expensive, bleeding, and viral infection contaminating from the production process, so urokinase is no longer used.
- T-PA or tissue plasminogen activator: half life 4 minutes.
- rt-PA or recombinant tissue plasminogen activator: used a lot in Thailand.
- Indication to thrombolysis:
  1. Acute thrombosis of a previously patent bypass graft or native artery with symptoms under 14 days.
  2. Acute arterial embolus not accessible to embolectomy.
  3. Acute thrombosis of a popliteal A. aneurysm causing severe ischemia where the run off vessel has all thrombosed.
  4. Acute thromboembolic occlusion in a high risk patient.
- Contraindication to thrombolysis (Schwartz p.799 gave it, for exams):
  - Absolute: 1. established cerebrovascular event, TIA or CVA, within 2 months; 2. active bleeding diathesis; 3. recent GI bleeding within 10 days; 4. neurosurgical procedure, whether brain or spine, within 3 months; 5. intracranial trauma within 3 months; 6. spinal surgery within 3 months; 7. intracranial malignancy or metastases.
  - Relative: 1. major nonvascular surgery or trauma within 10 days; 2. uncontrolled HT: systolic over 180 or diastolic over 110; 3. puncture of noncompressible vessel; 4. recent eye surgery; 5. intracranial tumor, benign or vascular malformation; 6. post MI.
  - Minor: 1. hepatic failure; 2. coagulopathy; 3. SBE; 4. pregnancy; 5. DM with hemorrhagic retinopathy.
- Points of caution in using thrombolysis: 1. more bleeding, especially in cases on heparin; 2. complications of the lytic agent; 3. delayed reperfusion, which may take up to 24 hr: so cases where the neuro exam by Rutherford is 2B, or severe ischemia of Siriraj, which need emergency treatment, should not use thrombolysis (remember, the exam asks often); 4. poor candidate patients: 4.1 acute embolism of a large artery; 4.2 acute postop bypass, since most are technical problems; 5. expensive; 6. cannot yet substitute for surgery.
- Methods of use:
  1. Intraoperative thrombolysis: supplement to embolectomy: popularly rt-PA 25 mg mixed with NSS 50 cc injected in, clamp the artery about 30 minutes, then do an intraoperative angiogram to check whether any clot remains; if still some, pull the clot again.
  2. Catheter directed thrombolysis: insert a catheter with the tip embedded in the thrombus mass, then inject, and drip rt-PA through the catheter for about 8-12 hours: so long that it is forbidden in Rutherford 2B.
     - If it works: the leg begins to warm; during this time watch for bleeding by checking Hct, coagulogram and fibrinogen; for bleeding from overdose of lytic agent, treat with FFP.
     - Before stopping: do not forget to inject dye to see whether the thrombus has cleared or not.
- After successful clot lysis, do not forget to fix the underlying problem of the blood vessel. Figure: angiogram of acute ischemia of the left leg in a 68-year-old woman with chronic renal failure: occlusion of the proximal superficial femoral artery; after tPA infusion through a multi-side-hole catheter, angioplasty and self-expanding stent, a widely patent artery (skip personal details).

## Fungal Arteritis
- Caused by the fungus Pythium insidiosum, which lives in natural water sources; the organism enters the body of the patient via a foot wound, and then touching contaminated water causes inflammation of the skin that spreads into the blood vessel (vascular pythiosis), followed by severe vascular infection. Figure: pathology of granuloma, hyphae (skip).
- Patients at risk of this fungal infection: thalassemia, aplastic anemia, those with occupations in rice fields, gardens, touching water sources that have this organism, causing infection of many systems; what to know is infection of the vessel.
- The patient has gradual ischemia of the leg from vessel inflammation, then occlusion, with fever. Figure: leg photo (skip).
- Confirmed by KOH stain of the arterial wall plus tissue culture.
- Treatment: since this fungal infection resists drugs, it is forbidden to pull the clot, because it makes the upper vessel infected too; so the treatment is amputation, taking out the infected fungal vessel.

## Paradoxical Embolus
- Starts with a DVT in the leg, the clot dislodges up to the heart, pushing into an ASD (very helpful) or a patent foramen ovale into the left atrium, down to the left ventricle, and out from the heart to block arteries elsewhere; so a paradoxical embolus is a clot from a vein that dislodges to block an artery. Figure: paradoxical embolism, narrowing of the pulmonary artery causing right-to-left pressure differential (skip).
- Emboli entering the arterial circulation cause symptoms according to the blocked site, e.g. if it runs into the carotid artery it causes stroke, and when a venous embolus dislodges again, if the 2nd clump runs elsewhere, e.g. to block the artery of the leg on the same side, symptoms of both brain and leg occurring at the same time like this is the classic finding of paradoxical embolism.
- Suspect it in a patient with DVT leg swelling and PE with a systolic ejection murmur heard over the heart.
- We can separate its symptoms from acute aortic dissection, because in this disease the pulses in the leg decrease on both sides.
- Treatment: 1. immediate anticoagulant; 2. consider embolectomy, or IVC filter in the case of unstable emboli ready to dislodge again; 3. percutaneous transcatheter closure of the patent foramen ovale.

## Acute Saddle Embolism
- Acute saddle embolus arises from a large clot lodging right at the aortic bifurcation, so both legs lack blood, with numbness and weakness of both sides, because the clot blocks the distal aorta. Figure: aortic bifurcation embolus; source is a recent myocardial infarct or atrial fibrillation (skip).
- Imaging: sharp cut-off convex filling defect at the aortic bifurcation.
- Treatment: 1. start with systemic heparinization; 2. surgery: open both groins, pass vessel loops around both common femoral arteries, pull the clot out of the aorta one side at a time; after pulling the clot out, do an intraoperative angiogram, followed by local heparinization, then suture the artery closed, then release blood down one leg at a time.

## Drug-Induced Arterial Spasm
- A drug that causes vasospasm so the leg suddenly lacks blood (ALI), usually in both legs.
- Found with drugs for migraine, the ergotamine tartrate group.

## Postoperative Complications
- Postoperative complications to know: ischemic reperfusion injury and compartment syndrome.

### Ischemic Reperfusion Injury
- The same problem as the complication of AAA repair, or muscle necrosis from electrical burn; for the leg: when revascularization is done in a Rutherford 2B patient, the toxins abundant in the cells of dead muscle, i.e. potassium, phosphate, lactic acid, thromboplastin, creatine kinase and myoglobin, rush into the systemic circulation quickly, causing reperfusion syndrome; most commonly metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, with creatinine phosphokinase leaking from muscle cells increasing.
- Symptoms: BP drop, arrhythmia, ARDS, myoglobinuria, kidney failure, DIC; if not treated well, death.
- Treatment:
  1. Treat metabolic acidosis and hyperkalemia, watch the heart tracing, and watch for good urine output.
  2. Treat myoglobinuria by giving fluids, mannitol, bicarbonate; if the kidney fails, dialysis; in a patient who has much leg pain, tense calf muscle, pain whenever passive flexion is done, pulse faint or not palpable, with a trend toward compartment syndrome: it is not necessary to measure pressure in the compartment: hurry to do immediate fasciotomy.
  3. Treated as in 1 and 2 and still cannot hold: there are 2 options: 3.1 major amputation, usually AK-amputation; 3.2 controlled limb reperfusion: insert a catheter to draw out blood with much potassium and toxin through the femoral vein, pull the dark blood out and pass it through a dialysis machine to remove the waste, then put back the filtered blood through the internal jugular vein; a laborious job, needs a lot of effort; see the report of 1 case from Siriraj, the patient survived.

## Compartment Syndrome
- Leg compartment (figure: cross-section of the leg showing anterior, lateral, superficial posterior and deep posterior compartments with the interosseous membrane and intermuscular septa).
1. Anterior compartment: the compartment involved first; its structures are the anterior tibial A. and the deep peroneal N.; when pressure in the anterior compartment rises, as taught in the basics, the nerve is the most sensitive: the patient will have numbness at the web space between the 1st and 2nd toes, followed by inability to dorsiflex the 1st and 2nd toes.
2. Lateral compartment: has the superficial peroneal N., which works for eversion and sensation of the lateral aspect of the foot.
3. Deep posterior compartment: structures: 3.1 tibial N.: works for plantar flexion of the foot; 3.2 posterior tibial A.; 3.3 peroneal A.
4. Superficial posterior compartment: has the sural nerve.
- Raised intracompartment pressure compresses the capillaries, while venous pressure rises, decreasing the arterial-venous pressure gradient.
- Clinical: must have a high index of suspicion: always think of it in a patient with ALI for more than 6 h, prolonged shock, previous DVT, or after revascularization, then these symptoms occur:
  1. Pain out of proportion to clinical signs and symptoms.
  2. Pain on passive stretch of the muscle in the compartment: the more passive flexion, the more pain; or stretching the muscle is extremely painful.
  3. Paresis (numbness) of the muscle compartment, starting at the web space of toes 1 and 2 from deep peroneal nerve compression in the anterior compartment.
  4. Tense and swollen fascial compartment.
  5. Distal pulse not palpable: but even if palpable it does not rule out.
- In a case with a trend or a high index of suspicion of compartment syndrome, should monitor compartment pressure 1-2 times per hour; a helper may be the creatine kinase (CK) in the blood: if the CK is higher than 500-1,000 unit/ml, it is likely compartment syndrome.
- In the past intracompartmental pressure was used as the criterion for diagnosis: if the measured compartment pressure is over 30 mmHg, there is a chance of muscle necrosis already: hurry to do fasciotomy; nowadays this absolute pressure is not popular any more.
- Rutherford 2018 advises using the dynamic ICP threshold: if the following values are found, do fasciotomy: 1. MAP - ICP under 40 mmHg; 2. DBP - ICP under 10 mmHg. (MAP = mean arterial pressure; ICP = intracompartmental pressure; DBP = diastolic blood pressure.)
- Incision (figure: medial and lateral incisions, with cross-section of compartments and nerves/vessels):
  1. Medial incision: to decompress the superficial and deep posterior compartments.
  2. Lateral incision: to decompress the anterior and lateral compartments.
- Open the skin, subcutis and fascia in a long line: you will see the muscle pop out along the incision; the muscle that was tense will then soften and the distal pulse can be palpated.
- Rutherford 2018 gave the absolute indications for fasciotomy:
  1. Tense compartment plus either: 1.1 pain with passive motion of the muscle traversing the same compartment, or 1.2 paresis or paresthesia referable to the same compartment.
  2. Tense compartment that has: 2.1 MAP - ICP under 40 mmHg; 2.2 DBP - ICP under 10 mmHg.
- Criteria for prophylactic fasciotomy: 1. acute thromboembolic occlusion with ischemic time over 6 hr; 2. prolonged shock; 3. combined A. and V. injury; 4. massive soft tissue injury; 5. soft tissue swelling before or at revascularization; 6. obvious failure of A. repair; 7. major venous ligation in the popliteal or femoral area; 8. phlegmasia cerulea dolens.
- In practice, one should do prophylactic fasciotomy in a patient who has revascularization at severity class 2b that has gone on longer than 4 hours, because the chance of compartment syndrome is high.

## Major Amputation
- Indication: 1. advanced extensive tissue ischemia and necrosis; 2. muscle necrosis until septic shock and MOF; 3. failure of medical treatment in myoglobinuria, because if amputation is late the kidney will fail for sure; 4. severe and life threatening sepsis; 5. when revascularization is considered inappropriate: 5.1 bedridden patient; 5.2 functionally useless limb; 5.3 technically impossible.
- If a question comes: an ALI leg that is cold, muscle hard as a board, fixed staining skin: what to do? Answer: amputation.
- A patient whose condition can still cope and has no reperfusion injury yet may have a one-stage operation; but if the patient is exhausted, with toxins running into the bloodstream, do a staged amputation: cut it off straight at the joint first, because it is quick, then leave the wound open; if the patient survives, return after some days to revise the stump.

## Chronic Limb Ischemia (CLI)
- A disease caused by blockage of the vessels of the arm and leg over a period longer than 2 weeks, mostly from atherosclerosis; the lack of blood is gradual and progressively severe, like ALI; the arm and leg compensate by dilatation of small blood vessels together with more collateral circulation; if the patient can still cope it is only intermittent claudication, but if not, critical limb ischemia occurs.
- Location of CLI residents should know: 1. aortoiliac (already separated out); 2. iliofemoral; 3. femoropopliteal; 4. tibioperoneal.
- Most emphasise CLI of the leg, found more often, called peripheral arterial occlusive disease; CLI in the arm, e.g. thoracic outlet syndrome and subclavian steal syndrome, is written for reading, enough to answer a question.
- Most common site of atherosclerotic occlusion in the leg: the distal SFA; while the most common site of peripheral A. emboli in the leg is the bifurcation of the CFA.
- The leg artery that is occluded by atherosclerosis the least: the peroneal A.
- Risk factors (same as atherosclerosis of other arteries): 1. smoking number 1; 2. DM number 2; 3. hyperlipidemia; 4. HT; 5. high homocysteine (stimulates smooth muscle to enter the vessel wall and promote fatty plaque accumulation; treat with folate, B6, B12); 6. high C-reactive protein.
- Interesting: a hypercoagulable state increases the risk of thrombosis but does not increase the risk of CLI.
- Patients are mostly men over 50 years with risk factors and coexisting disease, e.g. coronary or cerebrovascular A. occlusive disease, and have signs of chronic arterial insufficiency; CLI patients have coronary artery disease together in 40%.
- Signs of chronic arterial insufficiency (trophic change: changes of skin from CLI): figure: shiny, hairless skin, dystrophic nail changes and dependent rubor associated with peripheral arterial occlusive disease of the right foot:
  1. Skin thinner, shiny, hairless (loss of hair on the toe).
  2. Thickening of nail (brittle nail), muscle of the leg wasted.
  3. Dependent rubor.
  4. Temperature difference between thigh and foot.
  5. Pallor and slow capillary refill (normally not over 2-4 seconds).
  6. Subcutaneous tissue atrophy (thin fat layer), muscle atrophy.
- Beware of CVI symptoms such as skin induration.
- Clinical manifestations: symptoms occur when the vessel is narrowed over 50%:
  1. In the early period there may be no symptom, or pain while walking: intermittent claudication.
  2. If not treated well, or the occlusion progresses, the patient will get critical limb ischemia.
- The word claudication comes from the Roman emperor Claudius, who walked with a limp.

### Intermittent Claudication (IC)
- A symptom of lack of blood flowing to the big muscles of the leg, with lactic acid accumulation from the anaerobic pathway causing pain, occurring while the muscle works and needs more oxygen; in the early stage IC has a tight feeling like cramp, pain, tired; the symptom occurs when walking or running, but when resting the need for oxygen decreases, and the pain disappears within 10 minutes; once walking or running again, the pain returns; this pain pattern is called claudication.
- The pain occurs one level below the point of vessel occlusion.
- Pain free walking distance (PFWD): the distance the patient can walk without pain.
- Maximum walking distance (MWD): the farthest distance the patient can walk until the pain is unbearable and must stop.
- Pain symptoms: located one level below the artery occlusion site: 1. buttock claudication shows the occlusion at aortic iliac; 2. thigh claudication shows the occlusion at iliofemoral; 3. calf claudication shows the occlusion at femoropopliteal; 4. foot claudication shows the occlusion at tibioperoneal.
- Every IC patient should have pulses palpated all over the body and ABI measured; if ABI is normal but IC is still suspected, measure ABI during exercise; IC patients have ABI about 0.5-0.9.
- IC patients have an increased risk of cardiovascular events, with a chance of dying from MI about 5% per year.

### Critical Limb Ischemia
- A condition from chronic leg ischemia until finally the collateral cannot cope; the occlusion is so severe that the leg lacks blood severely.
- Clinical manifestation:
  1. Rest pain: pain all the time, very painful at midnight, nearly unable to sleep, even many morphine doses do not relieve it; the body compensates by dilating the distal blood vessels; the way patients relieve pain: they sleep with the leg hanging low, using gravity to pull blood to pool in the leg, until the foot swells and has a purple-red colour, called dependent rubor (figure: photo, skip).
     - Location of rest pain is greatest at the center of the metatarsophalangeal joint (not at the tip of the toe); the pain is worse when the leg is raised higher than heart level; looking at the leg, the leg and foot are pale, called elevation pallor (figure, skip).
     - Duration of rest pain must be longer than 14 days.
  2. Ischemic unhealed ulcer: wound at the tip of the toe, the wound edge black, the base looks wet.
  3. Digital gangrene: both dry gangrene and wet gangrene from infection, with a clear demarcation; in cases with tissue necrosis, it is probably multilevel disease.
- Even though critical limb ischemia is a clinical diagnosis, we should support it with objective tests.
- Observation: 1. IC: often only single segmental disease where collateral is still usable; 2. critical limb ischemia: often multilevel disease.
- Buerger test: may be asked by a teacher in the basics: the test has the patient lie flat, then raise both legs: the leg that lacks blood (critical limb ischemia) becomes pale; then have the patient sit with legs hanging: the leg that lacks blood severely turns dark red from reflex vasodilatation and the effect of toxic metabolites; the leg ends up cyanotic.

### Natural History of IC
- Following IC patients for 5 years: 1. 75% stabilized; 2. 25% worsened: of those, 2.1 5% need intervention; 2.2 2-5% need amputation.
- Among people of the same age, IC patients die of MI 40-60% and stroke 10-20%; on average IC patients die of MI and stroke 5-7% per year.
- From these numbers, most IC patients need only behavioral management: stop smoking, exercise, eliminate risk factors, treat comorbidities and systemic disease; control BP under 140/90, HbA1c under 6%, LDL under 70 mg/dL; the FDA-approved drugs are pentoxifylline and cilostazol; angiogram, PTA or bypass are used little: endovascular treatment or open surgery is used in IC that interferes with lifestyle, or exercise and drugs still do not improve, and in disease with a high risk of frequent problems, e.g. focal AIOD; to choose the method, use the TASC 2 classification.

### Natural History of Critical Limb Ischemia
- Following for 3 months: 9% die; 12% undergo amputation.
- After receiving treatment, taking drugs, controlling risk factors without surgery, following 1 year: 1. 25% improve slightly, pain decreases; 2. 25% die; 3. 20% pain continues; 4. 30% do not die but undergo BK amputation; 5. 45% stand up with both legs still present.
- In those who underwent BK amputation, following another 2 years: 1. 30% die; 2. 15% the BK-amputated leg becomes AK; 3. 15% the other leg gets BK amputation (a double whammy).
- When following CLI patients for 5 years, they die of comorbid disease 72%, leaving only 28% alive; survival looks worse than breast cancer; so for CLI, treat the coronary and cerebrovascular disease well, since they are the main cause of death in these patients.

### Screening for PAD
- In the general population there is no screening.
- People who should be screened for PAD, comprising history, physical examination, ABI:
  1. Age 50-69 years who smoke, or have diabetes.
  2. Age over 70 years with heart or blood vessel disease, or diabetes.
  3. Suspected intermittent claudication.
  4. Abnormal vascular examination.

### Evaluating Patients With Chronic Limb Ischemia
1. History of IC: ask about the character of the pain; do not forget to make a differential diagnosis of pain from other causes, e.g. knee pain, leg pain, back pain; do not forget to ask about coronary and cerebrovascular symptoms too.
2. Physical examination: look at trophic change, look for critical limb ischemia: ulcer or gangrene; do not forget to palpate pulses all over the body; use the back of the hand to assess the coldness of the arm and legs, comparing both sides; listen for the bruit sound of the vessels under the groin.
3. Lab: CBC, platelet count, coagulogram, DM, kidney, lipid, etc.
4. EKG.
5. Measure ankle or toe pressure and calculate ABI.
6. If critical limb ischemia, imaging must be done to see where the arterial lesion is.
7. For IC, the main treatment is risk modification together with drugs; if symptoms worsen or disturb life, revascularization must be done.
8. For critical limb ischemia, assess which treatment to give: bypass, endarterectomy, endovascular treatment or amputation, chosen to suit each patient's condition.

### Diagnostic Studies
- After taking the history and examining a patient who presents with vascular claudication, the next step is investigation, of which there are many; the author lists what is needed; here he starts with investigations that are still in the text but that in real life few do first, then the group used often:
  1. Segmental pressure gradient: measuring BP at different parts of the arm and leg: normally in the same arm or leg the pressure from the upper arm or thigh to the distal arm or distal leg should be equal; if the pressure differs between 2 points by more than 20 mmHg, there is an occlusion.
  2. Pole test: looks at flow at the foot: used for patients in whom toe pressure cannot be measured, especially those with underlying diabetes and problems of wounds at the toe: the method: ask the patient to raise the foot high, then listen to the sound through the US probe.
  3. Pulse volume recording (PVR): measures changes in volume of the leg, then plots the results as a graph: normal leg gives a high graph; vessel narrowed gives a short graph.

### Ankle Pressure
- Measures the blood pressure at the ankle: in normal people it should not be lower than 60 mmHg.

### Ankle Brachial Pressure Index
- Have the patient lie down, wrap the BP cuff at the ankle, use the Doppler probe to listen to the signal at the dorsalis pedis artery and posterior tibial artery, then record the highest systolic pressure; then put the cuff at the arm and record the highest systolic pressure of the brachial artery; if that patient has clear IC symptoms but ABI is normal, try measuring ABI after exercise (post-exercise ABI) by walking on a treadmill at an incline of about 12 degrees at 3.2 km/hr for 5 minutes.
- ABI = the higher SBP of the ankle (PT or DP) divided by the higher SBP of the arm. Figure: how to calculate the ABI; example: right arm 120, left arm 100; right ankle PT 68, DP 64; left ankle PT 136, DP 132; right ABI 68/120 = 0.57; left ABI 136/120 = 1.13; the lower number is the patient's overall ABI = 0.57.
- In a normal person ABI is above 1 always, because the SBP at the leg is equal to or more than the arm by 20%; Rutherford believes ABI over 1 can probably exclude arterial occlusive disease.
  - Normal value 1-1.2, not over 1.4; ABI 0.91-0.99 is considered borderline low.
  - Intermittent claudication: 0.5-0.9.
  - Ischemic rest pain: 0.3-0.5.
  - Limb threatening condition: under 0.3: with this ABI there is already tissue necrosis.
- In a case with clear IC symptoms but normal ABI, measure after exercise (post-exercise ABI): mild disease: post-exercise ABI 0.51-0.9; moderate disease: post-exercise ABI 0.16-0.5; severe disease: post-exercise ABI under to equal 0.15.
- ABI correlates with the severity of symptoms; also in cases with ABI under 0.9, risk of myocardial infarction increases; ABI under 0.5, or ankle pressure under 60 mmHg, must be hurried to revascularization.
- Limitations of ABI:
  1. Those with hard blood vessels with heavy calcification, e.g. DM, kidney disease, elderly: the cuff cannot compress the vessel, so ABI may be more than 1.4 or there are clear critical limb ischemia symptoms but ABI looks normal: in this case measure toe pressure instead.
  2. Not sensitive in diagnosing early cases of aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD).
  3. Cannot separate stenosis from occlusion.
- Currently in the USA ABI screening is done to detect patients with chronic limb ischemia, recommended in people aged over 70 years, or over 50 years with diabetes or who smoke.

### Toe Brachial Pressure Index (TBI)
- Used in cases where the vessels at the ankle have heavy calcification until the vessel is so hard that the cuff cannot compress the vessel at the ankle (non-compressible artery); when measuring ankle pressure, the value is higher than the truth.
- Use a small cuff wrapped around the base of the big toe, then measure pressure at the digital artery, which usually has little calcium.
- In normal people the toe systolic pressure = 90-100 mmHg; if under 40 mmHg it shows the leg really lacks blood, and if under 30 mmHg it is critical limb ischemia.
- The normal value of TBI is 0.8-0.9, slightly lower than the normal ABI.
- Claudication: TBI under 0.7.
- Critical limb ischemia: TBI under 0.2.

### Doppler Signal
- Using a hand held Doppler US probe to listen to the sound arising from the sound wave hitting the red blood cells flowing in the vessel, recorded as flow velocity of blood in the vessel.
- Artery: hear a "foo foo" sound according to the systole of the heartbeat.
- Vein: hear a sound like continuous wind blowing, a loud or soft sound according to the rhythm of breathing.
- If there is arterial stenosis the foo sound is softer, but if the wind sound of the vein is still heard, it shows the blood flowing through the vein comes from collateral; but if neither artery nor vein is heard, it shows severe lack of blood.
- Normal flow signal has 3 phases: 1. first phase: sound during cardiac contraction or systolic phase; 2. second phase: sound in early diastolic; 3. third phase: sound in late diastolic.
- A normal person without arterial occlusion hears all 3 phases, triphasic; a mildly-to-moderately ischemic leg hears 2 phases, biphasic; a severely ischemic leg hears a single phase, monophasic.

### Color Duplex Ultrasound
- Use of real time B mode US image together with pulsed Doppler by placing the probe, which sends a sound wave of frequency 5-12 MHz, perpendicular to the vessel to be examined; the sound wave hits the blood in the vessel and reflects back as sound.
- When the flow velocity is converted to a waveform, the first point is to listen to the flow signal like Doppler:
  1. Normal artery: triphasic velocity waveform with 3 sounds: the first in systole, the second in early diastole (heart relaxation first part), the third in late diastole.
  2. Mild-to-moderate ischemic leg: biphasic velocity waveform, hear only 1 to 2 sounds; from the waveform the peak height of the wave decreases while its width increases.
  3. Severely ischemic leg or occluded vessel: monophasic: hears only 1 sound, flat, wave sticking to the ground.
  - Figure (disease progression, distal flow): normal triphasic (forward flow in systolic peak, reversal of flow in early diastole, forward flow in late diastole); biphasic after mild-moderate stenosis (delayed acceleration, decreased systolic peak, increase of flow in diastole); monophasic after severe stenosis or thrombosis; absent: thrombosis.
- But what is done more than Doppler is duplex: it still shows the picture of the vessel, flow in the vessel, and the velocity of blood, displayed as a 2-dimensional (2D) image, as a beautiful colour in the rhythm of the heart contracting and relaxing, seeing the position of occlusion or narrowing, and seeing lesion morphology lying inside the lumen of the artery.
- Advantages: 1. used to screen for occlusion of the artery at the carotid bifurcation, mesenteric A, renal A; 2. rule out stenosis or false aneurysm after revascularization.
  3. Look at the deep veins for DVT or not.
  4. Look at the saphenous vein to see whether it can be used as a conduit.
  5. Surveillance of patients after open or endovascular treatment.
  6. Can be done on both sides, no need for contrast.
- Disadvantages: 1. operator dependent: the person doing it needs to be skilled and experienced, doing it often, taking long to examine; 2. it only screens, but the picture is unclear, so it often needs confirming with imaging such as CTA, MRA, DSA; 3. in obese patients, bloated abdomen, much gas, the abdominal vessels are not seen clearly, and the arm and leg vessels also not clear; 4. in the examination room, a probe that is too cold may stimulate the vessel to constrict.

## Imaging Studies
1. CTA: inject contrast into a vein, then scan in the arterial phase; noninvasive, like an angiogram; nowadays software is much better; multidetector-CTA, which rotates the CT gantry continuously, takes only about 30 seconds to finish; the aorta and branches are seen clearly, one can see whether the vessel has a mass, a wall tear or kinking, or a stretched vessel hanging or empty; sensitivity about 95-97% and specificity about 91-98%; the larger the vessel the more clearly seen; the drawbacks: contrast induced nephrotoxicity, and if the vessel wall has thick calcium attached, it may cause wrong interpretation.
2. MRA: no iodinated contrast needed, so used in patients with high creatinine; but we must know the contraindications of MRA: forbidden in patients with a pacemaker, defibrillator, spinal cord stimulator, intracranial shunt, cochlear implant, intracranial clip; for patients with a metallic stent, MRA can still be used, but the drawback is artifact with signal drop, except a nitinol stent, which causes less artifact.
   - Also MRA suits viewing blood vessels in the mediastinum, e.g. aortic dissection, in which we see the tear and the intimal flap clearly; MRA also shows the thickness of the aorta, the size of the lumen and the size of the thrombus well.
   - Point of caution: the problem of overestimated arterial stenosis: MRA images show a stenosis more than normal.
3. Angiogram: inject dye into the artery directly, then film + fluoroscopy to look; as before, angiogram is the gold standard, seeing narrowed or occluded points clearly, helping in intervention treatment; if bone covers, use software to subtract the bone, showing only the vessel: this technique is called digital subtraction angiography (DSA); the contrast used has 2 kinds: 1. iodinated contrast: be careful in patients whose kidneys are not good; 2. carbon dioxide: inject CO2 into the artery directly, but the image is not very clear, so it is often used together with iodinated contrast and then filmed; the advantage is it helps reduce the amount of iodinated contrast used.

### Femoropopliteal Occlusive Disease
- Starts from the beginning of the CFA at the inguinal ligament to the end of the popliteal artery, at the beginning of the tibial vessels.
- Figure (TASC II femoropopliteal lesions):
  - TASC A: single stenosis 10 cm or less in length; single occlusion 5 cm or less in length.
  - TASC B: multiple lesions (stenoses or occlusions), each 5 cm or less; single stenosis or occlusion 15 cm or less not involving the infrageniculate popliteal artery; heavily calcified occlusion 5 cm or less in length; single popliteal stenosis.
  - TASC C: multiple stenoses or occlusions totaling over 15 cm with or without heavy calcification; recurrent stenoses or occlusions after failing treatment.
  - TASC D: chronic total occlusions of CFA or SFA (over 20 cm, involving the popliteal artery); chronic total occlusion of the popliteal artery and proximal trifurcation vessels.
- Treatment:
  1. Lifestyle modification: control diet, control diabetes, BP, lipids, stop smoking, exercise.
  2. Medication: besides taking the drugs for the underlying disease, should take antiplatelets, which help reduce the risk to the heart.
  3. Lack of blood and brain: revascularization has 2 ways:
     - 3.1 Endovascular treatment: suits type A most, then type B; the problem lies in placing a stent at the popliteal artery below the knee, a location at a joint, where stent fracture and stent blockage occur easily.
     - 3.2 Surgical bypass: suits TASC type D, then type C; for the conduit: if the distal anastomosis is above the knee, PTFE can be used; but if below the knee, GSV should be used.
- Lower Extremity Grading System (LEGS score): to help decide which treatment to choose: open surgery, endovascular treatment, or cutting the leg off; in Cameron 12e 2017 p.993 as follows. Figure (the LEGS score table):

### Tibioperoneal Arterial Occlusive Disease
- Revascularization here is a challenge to vascular doctors, since the run-off is very poor and hard to connect.
- Figure (TASC II infrapopliteal lesions): TASC A: single focal stenosis 5 cm or less in length in the target tibial artery with occlusion or stenosis of similar or worse severity in the other tibial arteries. TASC B: multiple stenoses each 5 cm or less, or total length 10 cm or less, or single occlusion 3 cm or less in length, in the target tibial artery with occlusion or stenosis of similar or worse severity in the other tibial arteries. TASC C: multiple stenoses in the target tibial artery and/or single occlusion with total lesion length over 10 cm with occlusion or stenosis of similar or worse severity in the other tibial arteries. TASC D: multiple occlusions involving the target tibial artery with total lesion length over 10 cm or dense lesion calcification or non-visualization of collaterals; the other tibial arteries occluded or dense calcification.
- Author's shortened version (as printed in the text): Type A: single lesion less than 1 cm long, no trifurcation involvement. Type B: single lesion less than 1 cm long but involving the trifurcation; multiple lesions with total length under 1 cm. Type C: extensive trifurcation involvement; occlusion 1-2 cm; stenosis 1-4 cm. Type D: occlusion longer than 2 cm.
- Treatment: for a lesion that can be connected: revascularization is mostly open distal bypass using autogenous vein, i.e. the GSV from the other side of the bypass, as the conduit, with the inflow mostly the common femoral artery, and the outflow choosing between the anterior or posterior tibial first; if not usable, choose the peroneal artery; a low bypass below the knee is hard to do, leave it to the vascular fellow; endovascular treatment here has little role, except in an acceptable situation: the highest-risk patient with estimated life expectancy under 2 years, or no good vein to use as a conduit.

## Management of Chronic Limb Ischemia
- Two main treatments: no operation and operation; those who operate use open or endovascular treatment.
- Every CLI patient, even one who must be operated on, needs advice and treatment to correct the risk factors of atherosclerosis, and choosing an exercise suited to each person's physical condition, together with drugs; for treatment guidelines often quoted, the ACC/AHA 2005 by Hirsch AT et al. in Sabiston 2017 p.1767. Figure: ACC/AHA guideline for the management of PAD: steps toward the diagnosis of PAD (individuals at risk: age 50-69 with smoking or diabetes history; age 70 or more; abnormal lower extremity pulse examination; known atherosclerotic coronary, carotid or renal arterial disease -> obtain history of walking impairment and/or limb ischemic symptoms -> no leg pain / atypical leg pain / classic claudication / chronic critical limb ischemia / acute limb ischemia -> perform a resting ABI -> diagnosis and treatment of each category) (skip detail).
### Nonoperative management
1. Risk factor modification: follow the ACC/AHA 2006 guideline: control diet, exercise, stop smoking, control BP, DM, lipids (see table).
2. Drug management of IC: the one to know most is cilostazol; pentoxifylline and beraprost only need a rough mention.
- Do not forget to advise foot care (read more in DM foot).
- LDL lowering drug (statin): give to keep LDL under 100, but if at risk of MI, keep LDL under 70 mg/dl.
- BP drugs: beta-blocker and ACE inhibitors, control BP not over 140/90 in nondiabetic patients and 130/80 in diabetic patients or kidney disease.
- Drugs with no indication for use: prostaglandin, vitamin E, chelating agent, warfarin.
- Thrombolysis: does not work much; keep it for acute limb ischemia.
- IV heparin may help reduce ischemic rest pain in theory, but morphine and sleeping with the leg lowered work better.
- In conservative cases that improve, follow up every 3-6 months together with a noninvasive vascular lab test, e.g. ABI, or Doppler US: one of the two, or both, no one cares.
- The time to tell whether things are better is 4-6 months before the result is seen.
- Following the patient for 1-2 years, if the patient is stable, follow up once a year.
- Do not just look at the vascular system and palpate pulses: also examine risk factors, e.g. DM, BP, lipids, heart, kidney, brain.

### Exercise Program in IC Patients
- Exercise increases muscle performance and adaptive muscle enzyme, by increasing the % oxygen extraction by muscle; the result is increased walking distance, walking longer, and the pain decreases.
- Steps: 1. look at age and underlying disease, beware heart disease that needs care during exercise; 2. warm up, stretch muscles 5-10 minutes; 3. walk a suitably long route until claudication pain occurs; stop walking, wait until the pain disappears, then walk again; 4. walk at least 30 minutes a day, gradually adding 5 minutes a day until reaching 50 minutes a day; walk 3-5 times a week; continue for more than 6 months; 5. cool down at the end; 6. besides walking, may exercise with a stationary bicycle (not an electric one) or walk on a treadmill.

### Drug Management of Chronic Limb Ischemia
- Antiplatelet: to inhibit clot formation; there are 2 main drugs to choose from: aspirin 75-325 mg, found that low dose ASA 75-150 mg/day is about as effective as 325 mg, so give low dose, with the other being clopidogrel (Plavix) 75 mg once daily.
  - The issue still debated: between low dose ASA and clopidogrel, which drug to use: though clopidogrel is the newer drug, studies comparing head to head show it slightly better than ASA but much more expensive; most still advise low dose ASA first.
  - How to use: choose only one of the two, not both, except in patients treated with vessel dilatation then a stent placed, in whom a phase 3 study found that giving both together, with clopidogrel 75 mg for 3-6 months together with ASA 75 mg, with ASA continued lifelong, gives a better patency rate than ASA alone; this issue still awaits further studies.
  - These 2 antiplatelets prevent (primary prophylaxis) disease occurrence and reduce mortality from MI, stroke and peripheral artery occlusive disease by about 25%, with clopidogrel reducing vascular death rate better than low dose ASA slightly; there are reports that both drugs do not reduce pain while walking (intermittent claudication).
  - The new antiplatelet drug vorapaxar is not yet recommended because of bleeding risk.
- Anticoagulants, whether warfarin or enoxaparin, do not reduce vascular death from stroke and MI, and have the drawback of bleeding, so are not yet popular; currently awaiting results of a new anticoagulant, rivaroxaban.
- Currently no drug has been proven to add walking distance significantly, better than walking; the FDA has accepted only cilostazol and pentoxifylline.
- Cilostazol (Pletaal): a phosphodiesterase inhibitor with an effect to inhibit platelet aggregation, inhibit smooth muscle proliferation and increase vasodilatation (and so helps open the vessels); evidence base 1A, i.e. everyone accepts it clearly benefits.
  - Dose 100-200 mg bid per day, taken continuously; this drug helps reduce PAD pain and increases maximum walking distance by 50% (not double or 100%); anyway, being able to walk longer and farther; so it is advised to use cilostazol in every case; it works more effectively than pentoxifylline clearly (remember); after taking the drug, symptoms improve in about 3 months.
  - Disadvantages of Pletaal: 1. headache from vasodilation, diarrhea, not constipation; 2. contraindicated in CHF (remember) because the drug makes the heart beat fast and not rhythmic; for DM it can be used; 3. increased risk of bleeding, and when the drug is stopped, IC often returns.
  - Since it is expensive, use it when IC is severe enough to affect daily life; but once it is critical limb ischemia, cilostazol does not work.
- Pentoxifylline (Trental): a methylxanthine drug that helps increase the flexibility of RBCs and also reduces blood viscosity (not a vasodilator); evidence base 2A: results lose to cilostazol; some studies even say it does not increase maximum walking distance; conclusion: Trental is no better than placebo.
  - If one must give, dose 400 mg tid per day; disadvantages: 1. sore throat; 2. dyspepsia, nausea, diarrhea; 3. increased risk of bleeding.
- Beraprost (Donner): a prostaglandin group drug acting directly on the blood vessel to dilate it and reduce platelet aggregation; but studies still have no clear supporting data; from the guideline give 40 microgram TID with evidence base 3A, so it is used rather little; may give or not give, little different from taking vitamin; recently it was used in Buerger's disease patients (better than not taking it).
  - Disadvantages: 1. headache; 2. flushing; 3. GI distress.

## Operative Management
- The purpose is to increase the blood flow to the distal part of the leg (revascularization) to reduce pain, reduce amputation and increase quality of life.
- Consists of: 1. surgical bypass; 2. endarterectomy (written under AIOD, this operation is used little for lesions below the knee); 3. endovascular treatment; 4. angioplasty; 5. amputation.
- Indication for operative treatment: 1. critical limb ischemia; 2. disabling claudication, meaning claudication that affects quality of life and work after lifestyle modification, control of underlying disease, stopping smoking, and exercising to the fullest: IC alone is not an indication; 3. vasculogenic impotence.
- Capacitating claudication: leg pain while walking in an elderly person, but it does not disturb daily life.
- Incapacitating or disabling claudication: leg pain that disturbs daily life.
- Contraindication for operative treatment: 1. elderly, high risk with multiple level disease in whom claudication would not improve; 2. life threatening condition such as MI, CHF; 3. bedridden, looks like a vegetable; 4. terminal cancer, near going into the grave.

## Surgical Bypass for Infrainguinal Diseases
- For lesions below the inguinal ligament, there is femoropopliteal disease and infrapopliteal disease.
- The principles of surgery are similar, 3 items: 1. there must be good inflow; 2. there must be good outflow or run-off vessel; 3. choose a suitable conduit.
- If the patient has problems with both inflow or suprainguinal disease and outflow or infrainguinal disease, consider doing a staged procedure:
  1. Start with inflow first, e.g. aortobifem or iliac endarterectomy, then see whether the leg ischemia symptoms improve or not; in some cases we do only the inflow procedure and as blood flows in more, symptoms improve and the wound heals.
  2. If not better, with the foot wound still an ischemic ulcer, then do an outflow procedure next.
- Inflow vessel: choose a good vessel without plaque; CTA helps choose a suitable vessel.
- Outflow run-off vessel: need a good CTA to see which vessel is likely the best run-off; but if seen unclearly it may be because the vessels are very small or have much calcification, so conventional angiogram or DSA may be needed to see more clearly; Doppler listening to the arterial signal helps in looking for target vessels; in a case with good run-off with several vessels seen, choose the least diseased vessel.
- Conduit:
  1. If connecting above the knee, choose autogenous vein as the 1st choice since long term patency is slightly better than PTFE; but if necessary, with no good saphenous vein, or having to keep the vein for coronary bypass, bypass above the knee with PTFE is acceptable, 5-year patency rate about 50%.
  2. If connecting below the knee, the best conduit is still autogenous vein as before; but when no good GSV, because it is diseased or already used for grafts, there are still options:
     - 2.1 Use another alternative vein: lesser saphenous vein is the first alternative, cephalic vein is the second option, or if the vein obtained is not long enough: try a composite graft, i.e. sewing lesser saphenous vein to PTFE; may solve it.
     - 2.2 Use a prosthetic graft: the last option for a conduit below the knee, in a case where no vein of good quality is found and it cannot be used; since patency is rather low, one should add an adjunctive procedure, e.g. Miller cuff or a distal AV fistula; besides, PO ASA for long term.
- There are reports of using cadaveric saphenous vein in cases with no vein to give; studies found that besides the higher cost, 2-year patency rate is only 24%, so it is advised to use cadaveric vein in cases needing a temporary conduit, at a site with infection, to bring blood to help the wound heal, then later replace it with a conduit with longer patency.
- Patency of the bypass graft in tibioperoneal disease depends on the patent pedal arch.
- Autogenous vein conduit: 1. reverse GSV bypass; 2. in situ bypass.
- Disadvantage of reverse GSV: leg swelling in the first 4-5 postoperative days from lymphatic disruption.
- In situ bypass: 5-year patency 75-80%, equal to using reverse GSV.
  - Advantages: 1. minimized trauma and still maintains the vasa vasorum of the vein, helping prevent ischemia of the endothelial layer; 2. the blood vessels joined are of similar size, so even if the saphenous vein has a smaller diameter, it can still be used.
  - Disadvantages: 1. must cut the inner venous valve, which can cause endothelial injury, and if the valve is not cut completely, early thrombosis occurs postop; 2. must ligate all venous tributaries; if not ligated completely, perfusion to the distal part may be insufficient.
- Proximal bypass: the distal anastomosis lies above the popliteal bifurcation.
- Distal bypass: the distal anastomosis lies below the popliteal bifurcation to the distal bifurcation.

### Follow up post-treatment and surveillance
- After infrainguinal bypass, to see patency there are many ways: ABI, duplex, CTA, MRA, angiogram are all very reliable, while palpating pulses as routinely done at OPD is the least reliable, depending on whether the examiner's palpation is correct or not.
- In practice, evaluation has 3 ways:
  1. Clinical response: symptoms decrease, wound heals, severity by Rutherford category decreases at least 1 grade, ABI measured increases more than before surgery by at least 0.15.
  2. Technical success: duplex or CTA helps see good blood flow, no dissection, no contrast extravasation, residual stenosis under 30% from before surgery.
  3. Hemodynamic success: look at ABI increasing from before treatment by 0.15, seeing waveform become triphasic or biphasic.

## Infrapopliteal Endovascular Treatment
- The role of endovascular treatment in treating still loses to bypass, cannot be done, but in the future there is much belief it will be strong.
- The role of endovascular treatment in acute limb ischemia: used in Rutherford category 1 and 2A:
  1. Catheter directed thrombolysis: juniors must know the indications and contraindications well.
  2. Percutaneous thrombectomy: we use it as a supplement to treat acute embolism patients in whom the vessel has stenosis lying beforehand, to help clean the residual clot by pulling or suctioning the clot out; most still need thrombolysis afterward.
- The role of endovascular in chronic limb ischemia: gives good results in proximal large caliber arteries, e.g. iliac artery with short stenosis and good run-off; next, the superficial femoral artery; men get better results than women, to have a longer patency rate; after dilatation and stent placement the 2-year patency rate is 50-75% but restenosis occurs, most from neointimal hyperplasia, a minority from stent fracture.
- For cases treated endovascularly with poor results: we often find a lesion at the distal artery below the knee: those with long stenosis, those with poor run-off, and female sex.
- From the TASC II morphologic characters of the lesion: treatment by endovascular suits lesion type A and type B, not suitable for type D and type C (summarized above).

### Endovascular Technique
1. PTA: using a balloon to help dilate the vessel and may place a stent to hold it open; there are 2 kinds of stent used:
   - 1.1 Bare stent: a stent without vessel wall covering, 2 kinds: self expandable (expands by itself) and balloon expandable stent, which expands using a balloon to help dilate it.
   - 1.2 Covered stent: has an artificial vessel wall of polyester, polyurethane or PTFE covering the stent.
2. Cutting balloon angioplasty: use a balloon of the kind with small blades on the outside, acting as atherotomes, helping cut out the plaque.
3. Endoscopic atherectomy: a treatment supplementing PTA by inserting a plaque-scraping tool or laser into the vessel to scrape out the plaque.
4. Others, e.g. cryoplasty, subintimal angioplasty (the author notes he has not been given permission to write further, since it is unlikely to be asked).
- Angioplasty at the superficial femoral artery is an alternative done more, especially in cases with short stenosis (5 cm); compared with stent placement, the rate of restenosis is found not different, 3-year patency rate about 80%.

### Amputation
- Used for patients who cannot be reconstructed at all, e.g. no good run-off to use, or a patient with a severely infected wound, sepsis, who cannot control the infection; amputation may be above or below the knee, and one must consider morbidity, mortality and the rehabilitation outcome.
  1. Above-knee amputation (AKA): used for patients aged over 70 years, dementia, end-stage renal disease, advanced coronary disease, limited preoperative ambulatory ability: when compared with BKA, AKA has higher mortality while stump healing of AKA is easier.
  2. Below-knee amputation (BKA): for patients who do not meet the criteria for AKA.
- The problem to think about is stump healing: there is no objective single test that can tell exactly whether the wound will heal: measuring ankle pressure can be used in patients who are not diabetic; found that pressure over 60 mmHg gives stump healing 50-90%; but if diabetic, with hard vessels that cannot be compressed, use toe pressure; and look at CTA to see whether blood reaches the leg enough.
- Measurement of transcutaneous oxygen tension (TcPO2) or transcutaneous oxygen pressure measurement (TCOM): measures the oxygen level at the skin, and can be used in cases where the leg is not very swollen, by placing a sensor directly on the skin at the position where the leg will be cut: if TcPO2 is over 40 mmHg the wound usually heals well; but if under 20 mmHg the wound usually will not heal. Figure (TcPO2/TCOM): measures the local oxygen tension in the skin deriving from local capillary (nutritive) blood perfusion; predicts wound healing potential; helps define degree of small vessel disease; accurately determines amputation level; monitors efficacy of patient's ongoing therapy; establishes candidacy for HBO treatment.
- In the future, treatment with stem cells, which are the cells of origin of blood vessels, taken from mononuclear cells of bone marrow or from peripheral blood, which differentiate into vascular endothelial cells and stimulate new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis); this method is waiting to see in the long run whether it can reduce the amputation rate; stem cell therapy is therefore a hope for cases where nothing can be done.

## Immune Arteritis
- Inflammation of blood vessels, divided into groups of diseases according to the size of the vessel:
  - Large artery: 1. temporal arteritis; 2. Takayasu arteritis.
  - Medium artery: 1. polyarteritis nodusa; 2. Kawasaki disease.
  - Small artery: 1. hypersensitivity arteritis; 2. there are more but not written (the author says to know much more is pointless, because the patient goes to the physician).
- This group of diseases is treated with steroid with good results, except hypersensitivity arteritis, which is treated with a calcium channel blocker together with pentoxifylline (no questions).
- (The author's aside: if asked how to treat, answer steroid, nothing more: steroid gets it right more than 90%.)

## Temporal Arteritis
- Some call it giant cell arteritis: a systemic inflammatory vascular disease of large sized arteries and parts of medium sized arteries similar to Takayasu; this disease likes to involve the aorta and extracranial branches, especially the superficial temporal artery.
- Cause: unknown; some believe genetic role.
- Found in middle aged women, age over 50 years; while Takayasu is age 30 years, quite young.
- Symptoms: 1. fever, headache, blurred vision, weakness, muscle aches and pains; 2. jaw claudication and temporal artery tenderness found very often, not rare; 3. ischemic optic neuritis may cause blurred vision or blindness.
- Complications: 1. acute aortic dissection; 2. blindness.
- Diagnosis: 1. temporal artery biopsy: cut out about 2 cm and send to pathology; they will read: multinucleated giant cells with perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration (figure: giant cells within a granuloma of granulomatous inflammation, skip); 2. duplex scan: may see a halo around the superficial temporal A.
- Treatment: 1. best is steroid: guess it right away: responds quickly but recurs often; 2. bypass may be needed in some cases; with an artery inflamed like this, whoever answers endarterectomy gets scolded: remember endarterectomy suits short segment atherosclerosis, not panarteritis; 3. key: a middle-aged woman with blurred vision, headache, painful scalp, in the morning answers steroid.

## Takayasu's Arteritis
- A chronic inflammatory arteritis involving large vessels, especially the aorta (the arch is found often) and its branches, so the pulmonary and coronary arteries are also affected; the character of the inflammation is panarteritis, so the aorta becomes hard and thick, followed by occlusion. Figure: CT of aorta (skip).
- Found in Asian women aged 25-30 years.
- Cause: still unknown; many believe in autoimmune.
- Symptoms:
  1. Early stage: fever, pain over the body, joint pain, fatigue, anorexia.
  2. Followed by vascular symptoms from inflammation of aortic branches: 2.1 cerebrovascular symptoms such as CVA, blurred vision; 2.2 aortic valve regurgitation, CHF; 2.3 pulmonary HT; 2.4 claudication of the arm and leg.
  2.5 HT from renal artery stenosis. 2.6 mesenteric ischemia.
- May not palpate the pulse of that arm, or BP of the 2 arms differ.
- Should do differential diagnosis with giant cell arteritis, radiation arteritis and syphilitic aortitis.
- Diagnosis: 1. ESR and C-reactive protein elevated; 2. CTA or MRA shows the aorta narrowed or occluded.
- Treatment: 1. by formula respond to steroid; may add methotrexate (some cases); 2. if medical treatment fails, or advanced disease with stenosis, consider bypass after inflammation subsides; forbidden to do percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) or endarterectomy, because the vessel wall is hard and cannot be scooped out.

## Non-atherosclerotic Vascular Disease
- Many, but to know for exams, only 2 diseases: 1. Buerger's disease; 2. popliteal entrapment syndrome.

## Buerger's Disease
- Or thromboangiitis obliterans: a chronic inflammation of small sized arteries, with panangiitis, multiple segment occlusion with skip areas; besides this it involves veins and nerves too; 40% of patients have small superficial thrombophlebitis, sometimes DVT.
- Found more often in the leg than the arm; some cases involve arm and leg together; some are found on both sides.
- Cause: actually unknown; some suspect autoimmune.
- Classic: found in men aged 25-40 years, heavy smokers; found in women 20%, meaning women can be found but less than men.
- Symptoms:
  1. Starts with claudication; then the disease progresses to ischemic rest pain and ulcers at the fingertip and toe tip, extremely painful.
  2. Recurrent superficial migratory thrombophlebitis.
  3. An issue often asked: BP: even though this disease narrows the vessels, BP is usually normal.
- Diagnostic criteria: use Shionoya criteria; some like the Olin criteria; if all are positive, it is probably this disease:

| Shionoya criteria | Olin criteria |
|---|---|
| Onset before age 50 | Onset before age 45 |
| Smoking history | Current (or recent past) tobacco use |
| Infrapopliteal arterial occlusions; upper limb involvement or phlebitis migrans | Distal extremity ischemia (infrapopliteal and/or infrabrachial), such as claudication, rest pain, ischemic ulcers, and gangrene documented with noninvasive testing |
| Absence of atherosclerotic risk factors other than smoking | Laboratory tests to exclude autoimmune or connective tissue diseases and diabetes mellitus; exclude a proximal source of emboli with echocardiography and arteriography; demonstrate consistent arteriographic findings in the involved and clinically noninvolved limbs |
- Classic angiogram: 1. large vessels and medium sized ones look normal; we see distal occlusion below the popliteal or brachial A.; 2. the occlusion is segmental with skip lesions, looking like cork screws or tree root appearance; 3. poor collateral.
- Classic histology: 1. segmental panarteritis with microabscess and multiple giant cells; 2. fibrous obliteration of the arterial lumen.
- Differential diagnosis: 1. atherosclerosis; 2. autoimmune vasculitis; 3. hypercoagulable state; 4. DM; 5. embolism.
- Buerger's disease differs from atherosclerosis in that Buerger involves all 3 layers of the vessel.
- Treatment:
  1. The most effective is to make the patient stop smoking, including being a secondary smoker, meaning someone near others who smoke, then walking by and inhaling their smoke: symptoms improve and the risk of amputation falls; if the patient continues smoking, 80-90% get a leg cut off for sure.
  2. No drug works well for this disease: many trials, whether calcium channel blocker, cilostazol, fish oil, anticoagulant: results are only slightly better than doing nothing.
  3. Operation: bypass has no role, because the run-off is very poor; the effective procedure is amputation.
  4. New things such as bosentan (endothelin antagonist), hyperbaric oxygen therapy, gene therapy by VEGF gene intramuscular transfer, stem cell therapy still await results.
- Sympathectomy helps a bit by increasing collateral at the skin and subcutaneous layer, but the problem is that the muscles still lack blood as before.
- Wherever one reads, sympathectomy is mentioned, so a bit of explanation:

## Sympathectomy
- Cervical sympathectomy: 1. most effective in treating palmar hyperhidrosis; 2. also used to treat complex regional pain syndrome; 3. common complication: Horner syndrome, comprising ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis; we can prevent it by preserving the upper half of the stellate ganglion.
- Lumbar sympathectomy: 1. most effective is treating complex regional pain syndrome, i.e. 1.1 reflex sympathetic dystrophy or causalgia; 1.2 pain from frostbite; 2. next effective: used to treat Raynaud syndrome that fails medical treatment; 3. no results in: 3.1 DM, because there is often already auto-sympathectomy; 3.2 non-healing ischemic ulcer; 3.3 Buerger disease.

## Popliteal Artery Entrapment Syndrome
- A rare disease but often found in exams: a case of a young man playing sports, e.g. football, then calf pain suddenly occurs; once he stops running, the pain disappears; no history of heavy smoking as in Buerger disease. Figure: popliteal artery, femoral epicondyle, medial and lateral heads of gastrocnemius (skip).
- This disease still palpates a normal femoral pulse, but the popliteal pulse is rather weak, and when asked to dorsiflex the foot or extend the knee, the gastrocnemius muscle contracts, and the result is that the distal pulse cannot be palpated.
- Cause of popliteal A. compression: 1. over 80% from the medial head of the gastrocnemius pressing on it; 2. congenital anomalies in the development of the popliteal A. and popliteus muscle, including an abnormal fibrous band beside the popliteal A. pressing on the vessel.
- Unilateral 75%; bilateral 25%.
- Diagnosis is difficult because the pulse is often normal.
- Investigation:
  1. ABI: while the patient is in the extended-knee position and the foot is in 3 positions: neutral, plantar flexion, dorsiflexion: ABI decreases by 50% or more.
  2. CTA or MRA: shows the popliteal A. deviating medially more than normal; if only one can be chosen, the author prefers MRA. Figure: MRA showing entrapment (arrow); resonance with right popliteal artery occlusion; arteriogram confirming right popliteal artery occlusion (skip).
- Treatment:
  1. Popular is the posterior approach: go in, divide the entrapping muscle +/- femorodistal bypass with reverse GSV; in a case found to have popliteal stenosis or aneurysm already, do the bypass right away.
  2. The medial approach: used in cases where the disease extends to the popliteal trifurcation.
  3. Endovascular treatment alone does not work, because it does not fix the muscle that is the cause of the popliteal A. compression.

## Upper Extremity Arterial Diseases
- Found less than in the leg; residents should know roughly 3 diseases: 1. subclavian steal syndrome; 2. Raynaud syndrome (RS); 3. thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS).

## Subclavian Steal Syndrome
- Arises from stenosis or occlusion of the proximal subclavian A.; symptoms are few or only arm fatigue.
- Found in the left arm (82%) more than the right; when the difference of systolic BP between the two arms exceeds 50 mmHg, symptoms will appear, which often occur while using that arm or exercise (arm pain on exertion); the blood that comes to feed the arm is pulled from the vertebral A. on the same side flowing down to the subclavian artery below the occlusion point; hence the result is symptoms of vertebrobasilar insufficiency, e.g. dizziness, diplopia, ataxia, vertigo, tinnitus. Figure: CT and diagram of reversed vertebral flow (skip).
- BP of the arm on the affected side is lower than the other side.
- Treatment: bypass with PTFE drawing blood from the common carotid to the subclavian artery:
  1. Carotid to subclavian bypass with PTFE is used most, better than subclavian to subclavian. Figure: carotid-subclavian and axillary bypass (skip).
  2. Endovascular treatment with PTA + stent is a new line of treatment: if it can be done, choose it first, success up to 70%; the problem is the long term result still loses to option 1.
  - Remark: carotid endarterectomy has no benefit.

## Raynaud's Syndrome
- The most common vasospastic disorder, found more in the hand than the foot.
- Cause: unknown; but there are precipitating factors that make the vessel constrict: cold temperature, smoking, emotional stress.
- Found in women more than men; young age: 90% aged under 40 years.
- Primary (cause not found): called Raynaud disease; usually found in healthy strong women; the primary group has the classic reactive hyperemia after the vessel constricts on exposure to cold.
- Secondary: when a cause can be pinned down, e.g. scleroderma (found most, 90%), Buerger, TOS, SLE (10-40%), rheumatoid arthritis (10-20%), etc.: the secondary group is called Raynaud syndrome; they often have trophic change, ulcer, and often much pain.
- Symptoms: classic in teenage women who meet cold or are stressed (from a lover leaving): hands pale white (pallor), followed by cyanosis (blue hands), taking 15-45 minutes, then returning to red (rubor) as before; the classic triad of color change; it starts at a single finger first, then several fingers, and finally both hands. Figure: Raynaud's phenomenon: fingers become white due to lack of blood flow, then blue as vessels dilate to keep blood in tissues, finally red as blood flow returns (skip).
- Investigation: 1. cold recovery time: the most accurate, very (how it is done is left to the technicians); 2. immunologic study to rule out causes from connective tissue disease or autoimmune; 3. CTA to rule out TOS.
- Treatment: the disease cannot be cured whatever treatment is used, so the aim of treatment is palliative:
  1. Lifestyle modification or avoiding precipitating factors, e.g. wearing gloves to keep warm, avoid vibrating tools, stop smoking, avoid stress, avoid tea and coffee, drugs to forbid: beta-blocker, decongestant, cocaine: doing only this improves 90%.
  2. Give a calcium channel blocker group drug such as nifedipine or diltiazem: improves 30-60%; if still no result, give a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor such as fluoxetine, which reduces the frequency and duration of the vasospastic episode; other drugs that can be used are topical nitrates, angiotensin receptor blocker.
  3. If after taking the drugs in item 2 it still cannot be controlled: try IV prostaglandin: may give results in cases that do not respond to every drug or with severe symptoms.
  4. Surgery is the last way:
     - 4.1 If the cause is thoracic outlet syndrome, fix it.
     - 4.2 Black or rotten finger: about 15% of patients undergo finger amputation.
     - 4.3 Cervicothoracic sympathectomy: gives good results with vasospasm of the toes and feet more than the fingers, helps symptoms improve 60%; but in the long term there is often recurrence, so this operation is not a good option; at present digital sympathectomy is advised: whichever finger, operate only that finger, using a microsurgical technique going in to strip the adventitia of the digital artery, which has sympathetic fibers, gives better results than the old operation.
  5. Injection of needles or spinal cord stimulation still has no data supporting real benefit.

## Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS)
- A group of symptoms arising from compression of nerve, vein and artery at the point passing out of the thoracic outlet.
- Normal anatomy (figure: scalenus anterior, subclavian a. and v., first rib, brachial plexus, pectoralis minor, etc.):
  1. Brachial plexus and subclavian A. pass over the 1st rib behind the anterior scalene muscle, but in front of the middle scalene muscle.
  2. Subclavian V. passes over the 1st rib in front of the anterior scalene muscle.
- When muscle, bone grows or has an abnormality, it compresses the structures that pass through the outlet.
- Cause: 1. scalene anterior or subclavius muscle hypertrophy; 2. bone abnormality, e.g. cervical rib pressing; 3. fibrosis or scar at the spot compressing. Figure: cervical rib (arrow) in a patient with subclavian artery aneurysm caused by thoracic outlet compression.
- Neurogenic TOS: the most commonly found, 95%, especially C8 and T1 or the ulnar nerve, causing weakness of the triceps and intrinsic muscles with numbness, next the radial nerve; diagnosis depends mainly on history and physical examination; if sympathetic compression occurs, Raynaud syndrome can occur.
- Tests for neurogenic TOS:
  1. Adson's test: have the patient inhale deeply, extend the neck, then turn the face to the opposite side: if the radial pulse decreases, count it as a positive test; this test is less reliable, because normal people are positive in about 70%.
  2. Tinel's test: by tapping and the patient will have pain along the ulnar nerve distribution.
- Venous TOS: found about 4%; the patient has a swollen arm; if subclavian vein thrombosis occurs, we call it Paget-von Schroetter disease or effort thrombosis.
- Arterial TOS: found 1%; the cause is usually a complete cervical rib pressing on the subclavian A., causing claudication until the vessel occludes from thrombosis, or an aneurysm may arise; the symptom to be suspicious of is unilateral ischemic symptoms, e.g. exertional cramping at the arm, and when asked to do arm abduction with full external rotation, which is the position where the thoracic outlet compresses the subclavian artery, we cannot palpate the radial pulse.
- A classic case to think of TOS: a patient aged 25-30 years with pain and numbness of the arm and hand, which worsen when the arm is raised above the head: when seeing such a picture, remember TOS.
- Investigation:
  1. Nerve: send MRI or CT to see the outlet, and nerve conduction velocity or EMG.
  2. Vein: send duplex and venogram.
  3. Artery: send DSA, CTA or MRA.
  4. Do not forget the chest film and C-spine film: may see a cervical rib that should not be there, which needs to be operated out.
- Treatment:
  1. Initial treatment: physical therapy and changing posture, looking for a position where nothing compresses nerve, vein and artery.
  2. 10-30% of patients need operation because symptoms do not improve, depending on what causes the compression: 2.1 cervical rib resection +/- 1st rib resection; 2.2 scalenectomy, both anterior and middle group; 2.3 combined procedure (2.1 + 2.2): beware injury to the phrenic nerve and subclavian artery during surgery.
  3. For venous thrombosis: heparinization +/- catheter directed thrombolysis.
  4. For arterial TOS: may need bypass.

## Chronic Leg Ulcer
- Chronic ulcer means a wound lasting longer than 2 weeks; causes of the wound are many: the location of the wound can roughly tell the cause (remember).

### Venous Ulcer
- Location: found at the gaiter area at the medial malleolus (remember): not found under the ankle joint or at the foot.
- Found in the late stage of CVI, may have severe varicose veins and lipodermatosclerosis.
- The wound is not very painful, and when the leg is raised the pain decreases, unlike an arterial ulcer, which gets worse on raising the leg, so the patient cries out.
- The wound looks good, with healing pink and moist granulation tissue.
### Arterial Ulcer
- Location: the wound is at the tip of the toe.
- An ischemic ulcer with a history of claudication or rest pain; usually very painful, especially at night.
- Pain decreases on lying with the leg hanging down and increases on raising the leg.
- The wound looks dry, without much granulation tissue, a deep punched out ulcer, with a grey-purple edge.
### Diabetic Ulcer
- Location: the wound lies at a pressure area, i.e. a weight bearing point, such as the 1st metatarsal head and the heel. Figure: foot ulcer photos (skip).
- The wound has overhanging callus, some are infected; deep wound.
- These patients have neuropathy, so not much pain.
### Hematological Ulcer
- Found in patients with thalassemia, sickle cell, TTP, leukemia, polycythemia vera.
- Mechanism: unclear, maybe from venous or capillary thrombosis.
- Location: often at the lower 1/3 of the leg.
- No specific character of the wound but often multiple painful ulcers.
### Malignant Ulcer
- Found following chronic ulcers, e.g. burn wound, venous ulcer, pressure sore; a wound with chronic pus drainage such as fistula in ano, chronic osteomyelitis for a long time; the wound has a rolled-out edge, grows a mass, smells foul, not very painful.
- Seeing a picture like this, the teacher wants us to biopsy.

## Diabetic Foot
- A serious problem of the diabetic patient: foot infection and ulcer, where infection will progress fast; if not careful, lose the leg or lose life: DM patients have about 15 times higher chance of being amputated than villagers.
- Cause:
  1. Neuropathy 62%: loss of motor, sensory and ANS: the skin cracks and dries, numbness, with intrinsic muscle paralysis, muscle in the foot atrophies, so the foot becomes deformed, the weight bearing points change until ulcers occur.
  2. Ischemic ulcer 38%: DM patients usually have atherosclerosis of small arteries below the knee, i.e. anterior and posterior tibial A. and peroneal A.; often the patient has no claudication, because the legs are numb and feel nothing.
  3. Combined: mixed of both.
- In cases that come with a DM foot ulcer:
  1. Cause from neuropathy: high sugar makes nerves swell and inflame, sensory neuropathy makes the foot numb, while motor neuropathy makes the intrinsic muscles of the foot weak and atrophied, and the structure of the foot changes, with flexion contracture of the PIP joints so the toes curl more than normal, called claw toe or hammer toe.
     - 1.1 From the thinned muscle, the foot skin bears more pressure until the skin thickens and callus forms; so DM wounds are usually at points that bear weight, e.g. the metatarsal head or the heel; the wound is rather round, the edge with a thick border of dead skin that we call callus. Figure: ulcer photo (skip).
     - 1.2 Destruction of bone and joints from pressure on the foot together with inflammation: the bone collapses until the middle of the sole bulges out, called Charcot joint. Figure: stages of ulcer development: callus formation; subcutaneous hemorrhage; breakdown of skin; deep foot infection with osteomyelitis.
  2. Cause from ischemia: the wound is usually at the tip of the toe, the base looks dry and does not bleed.

| Characteristics | Neuropathic foot | Ischaemic (neuroischemic foot) |
|---|---|---|
| Skin temperature | warm | cold |
| Pain | painless | painful |
| Skin colour | not altered | dependent rubor |
| Callus | thick at pressure point | usually not present |
| Ulcer | usually on tips of toes and plantar surfaces under metatarsal heads | often on margins of foot, tips of toes, heels |
| Peripheral pulses | bounding | feeble / absent |
| ABI | more than 0.9 | less than 0.9 |
| Complication | Charcot joints | critical ischaemia |
- The problem often met and asked is DM foot infection: the infection may be just ordinary cellulitis, or severe infection until necrotizing fasciitis, deep abscess or osteomyelitis.
- From history and examination, one should be able to separate DM foot infection into non-limb threatening infection or limb threatening infection.
- Bacteria (previously asked in exams):
  1. Non-limb threatening infection: usually a single organism such as staph aureus.
  2. Limb threatening infection: usually mixed organisms, both aerobes and anaerobes.
- Osteomyelitis: when osteomyelitis is found in an infected diabetic wound, it counts as limb threatening that needs hurried treatment: suspect it in a case where the wound is deep to the bone: when a metal probe is inserted into the wound, the probe hits bone, called probe to bone test; including foot x-ray may find gas around the bone, the bone, and the bone tissue change seen on plain film may take about 10-20 days.
  - MRI diagnoses osteomyelitis faster than plain film but the drawback is the high price; MRI should be used when truly suspicious and the plain film shows no abnormality.
  - Bone biopsy is the definitive diagnosis (theory).
- So in a patient with persistent DM foot infection, there may be devitalized tissue hiding in a closed space that has not been debrided; in this case we should repeat the film at intervals, or may need an MRI to help diagnose.
- In this era, no classification would be out of trend: there are many DM ulcer classifications, the popular one is Wagner's, dividing into 6 groups. Figure (Wagner classification): Grade 0: no ulceration in a high-risk foot; Grade 1: superficial ulceration; Grade 2: deep ulceration that penetrates to the tendon, bone or joint; Grade 3: osteomyelitis or a deep abscess; Grade 4: localized gangrene; Grade 5: extensive gangrene requiring a major amputation.
- The author thinks it is easy to use only 3 groups: mild, moderate and severe.
- Principles of DM wound care consist of: control the sugar level, assess the blood supply of the foot, do local wound care correctly and control infection; do not forget to advise foot care.

### Non-limb threatening infection (mild)
1. Cellulitis under 2 cm2: the inflammation is only in the skin and subcutaneous tissue, redness and swelling limited, not spreading.
2. If it is an ulcer, do the probe to bone test: the probe tip will not hit bone.
3. Not much ischemia: no gangrene or necrosis.
4. No systemic toxicity.
- Treatment:
  1. Can treat as an OPD case: local wound care is the most important.
  2. If there is a wound, debride, dress, keeping the wound moist, do not let it dry; avoid washing the wound with very irritating solutions such as povidone iodine or H2O2.
  3. Give oral antibiotic.
  4. Wear suitable shoes: reduce pressure at the sole; follow up foot examination every 6-12 months.
  5. Check the wound in the first 1-2 days and appoint every 3-7 days until the wound heals; if the wound looks worse, hurry to admit, then treat as limb threatening infection.

### Limb threatening infection (items 1, 2, 3 are moderate severity; item 4 is the severe group)
1. Cellulitis over 2 cm2: the redness and swelling spread out, with red streaks of inflammation extending to the middle of the foot; lymphangitis; or the inflammation deeper to fascia, muscle, bone.
2. Probe to bone test: if the wound is deep over 3 mm, with osteomyelitis, or the probe hits bone, it shows severity.
3. Much ischemia until black necrotic tissue.
4. Necrotizing fasciitis, sepsis.
- Treatment:
  1. Admit the patient, give IV antibiotic: the bacteria are usually mixed organisms, gm positive, gm negative and anaerobe.
  2. Culture.
  3. Sharp debridement: remove the devitalized tissue including foreign bodies, cutting down to good tissue that bleeds oozing; look at the wound at intervals, and if osteomyelitis, the bone infected should be scraped away, because diabetics have poor blood supply and wounds heal poorly; antibiotic alone seldom works: leave the wound open and wash it often.
     - Maggot therapy was presented by alternative doctors, using certain kinds of fly larvae that eat only dead tissue, not good tissue, put in a net bag placed on the wound to help eat the dead tissue around the wound (the author says he does not understand).
  4. If infection can be controlled, assess the healing potential of the diabetic wound: can it heal or not:
     - 4.1 If the tissue looks red, enough: guess the circulation of the wound is still OK: continue to let the wound go on, then find a way to close the wound: may suture it, or use VAC to help.
     - 4.2 If there is no granulation at all, the wound looks pale, with tissue necrosis at the edges: assess arterial status to see whether revascularization is needed, with toe-brachial index, Doppler, including CTA or DSA.
- If it cannot be saved, with clear dead tissue, may need amputation of the toe or the foot.
- But if saved and the foot can be kept, come to reduce the pressure at the sole with suitable shoes, for diabetic patients, instead of ordinary shoes; some cases may need an operation to correct the deformed foot by tendon lengthening or transmetatarsal osteotomy.
- Amputation in diabetic patients: the problem often met is stump healing: we guess the wound should close in cases where:
  1. Amputation of only a toe or the foot: when we measure the toe pressure and get over 40 mmHg: the wound should heal.
  2. If doing BKA or AKA: measure transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2) at the site to be cut, in mmHg, with a special instrument, not a pulse oximeter: 2.1 under 20: stump will surely not heal; 2.2 20-30: hesitant; 2.3 over 30: acceptable, a little happier; over 40: if not diabetic, stump will surely heal.
  3. But the best predictor to predict the healing of BKA is a positive popliteal pulse.
- Revascularization: DM patients often have multiple infrapopliteal stenoses, long segment, and poor run-off, so endovascular treatment does not work well: must bypass without exception.
  - Principles of revascularization: 1. inflow must be good: CFA, SFA or popliteal A. can be used; 2. outflow must be good, because distal bypass is made to a small vessel such as the peroneal or even at the dorsalis pedis; 3. use GSV as the conduit, reversed or in situ as one pleases.

## Vascular Access for Hemodialysis
- Surgery to create a vessel, or placing a catheter with 2 lumens (double lumen), to draw blood out of the patient through a filter of waste (semipermeable membrane) and then return it into the patient's body again.
- Different from central venous access, which is used as a route for IV or as a port device to give chemotherapy.
- The ways done today: 1. AVF or arteriovenous fistula; 2. AVBG or arteriovenous prosthetic bridging graft fistula; 3. indwelling double lumen central venous catheter (because it is so long, the author calls it the "2-lumen line").

### Double Lumen Catheter
- One line has 2 tubes: one is the arterial channel, which has the duty of drawing blood to the filter, and the other is the venous channel, used as the route returning blood into the body; the advantage is that it can be used for dialysis at once after insertion. Figure: catheter photos, catheter in the SVC/right atrium with blood to and from the dialysis machine (skip).
- Insert through the skin at the neck; even though it can be done at the bedside, the author advises doing it in the OR, since some patients do not cooperate well, bleed a lot; watch the coagulogram.
- The vein to choose: puncture the right internal jugular vein first, with the catheter tip lying in the SVC; puncture here can be used for about 3-4 weeks and the line is blocked often; but if using a cuffed tunneled catheter, which has less chance of infection or thrombosis, it can be used for many months; for the femoral vein it is fine, but often works only 1-2 weeks.
- Avoid using both subclavian veins and the left internal jugular vein, since later they may develop central vein occlusion or narrowing, so that they cannot be used for AVF or AVBG.

## Vascular Access
- Rule of 6 (handwritten note): creating an anastomosis between artery and vein so that the wall of the vein thickens and grows large enough to be used for dialysis; maturation often takes 6 weeks; the vein used must be at least 6 mm in diameter, with flow through the AVF more than 600 cc/min, and the depth of the vein from the skin not more than 6 mm; at the usable size of 6 mm, we puncture with a needle into the vein that has swelled, drawing blood to dialyse; the artery and vein taken must be good quality vessels, not PAD, not narrowed and not blocked.
- If possible, choose AVF, which is a native vein, first, because it can be used longer and is less likely to be infected, except when there is no suitable vein; but in practice a PTFE graft is more convenient, waiting about 3 weeks it is ready to use.
- Currently in the USA they try to push the use of native veins; so the theoretical answer is AVF every time.
- Considerations in making an AV access:
  1. Choose the arm that has a normal artery and vein first.
  2. Choose the non-dominant arm first; choose the side that has never had a C-line first.
  3. Use the arm before the leg.
  4. At the arm, choose the forearm before the upper arm; use the cephalic vein before the basilic vein.
  5. Choose AVF before AVBG.
  6. If doing an AVBG, a loop configuration has better patency than straight. Figure: anatomy of upper extremity vessels (skip).
- Choice of AV access:
  - 1st choice: AVF at the wrist, in the non-dominant hand, i.e. autologous radial-cephalic (radiocephalic), or called by some the Brescia-Cimino fistula.
  - 2nd choice: autologous radial-basilic fistula.
  - 3rd choice: autogenous ulnar-basilic or brachial-cephalic fistula.
  - 4th choice: PTFE 6 mm used as a bridge graft at the forearm.
  - 5th choice: AV graft at the leg, e.g. femorofemoral or femorosaphenous, or at the arm, e.g. axilloaxillary AV graft, used in cases where no native vein, a good one, can be found, only bad ones, because they have been punctured, injected with drugs until hardened, or the AVF has all been exhausted; may be a loop graft connecting the axillary A. and axillary V., or at the leg between the femoral A. and GSV. Figure: snuff-box AVF (end-to-side anastomosis, radial artery and forearm cephalic vein) and forearm loop AV graft (skip).
- Assessment before making an AV access:
  1. History: whether heart disease or CHF that is difficult to treat; avoid using vessels on the side that previously had RT at the chest wall, or previous subclavian vein puncture, because if the central vein is narrowed it will not work; easy to see: if superficial veins dilated at the shoulder and chest wall, or a swollen arm, it shows the subclavian vein is already narrowed; avoid an arm with muscle weakness, because exercising that arm by clenching a rubber ball to squeeze it will not be possible, and the vein will not enlarge in size, so will be narrowed easily.
  2. Examine the veins: start with the nondominant wrist: put a BP cuff at the midforearm; we see superficial veins at the forearm swell; assess diameter, compressibility and continuity with the upper arm vein; the arm to be operated on must not have had a previous subclavian line, so the central vein is narrowed not over 50%.
     - Rutherford recommends duplex US in every case; the vessel chosen should have these qualities: 2.1 for AVF the vein should have diameter over 2.5 mm; 2.2 for AVBG the vein should have diameter over 4.0 mm; 2.3 no stenosis and the vein course should be straight; 2.4 good continuity with the deep or central system; 2.5 check both the cephalic vein and the ulnar vein.
     - Duplex should be done; venogram only as indicated: see item 4.2.
  3. Examine the arteries: palpate radial, ulnar, brachial A. pulses: must be of strength like normal; vessels not hard; do an Allen test to check the palmar arch of the hand, and the radial artery diameter should be over 2 mm; measure BP of both arms: should not differ by more than 20 mmHg. Figure: the Allen test (digital occlusion of radial and ulnar arteries; open and close hand; ulnar release and reperfusion assessed to confirm a complete palmar arch; handheld Doppler may be used).
  4. Investigation:
     - 4.1 Duplex US: what must be done before surgery every time: the advantage is that it evaluates both artery and vein, especially the vein very well: diameter, wall thickness, and positions that should be used for central vein, e.g. subclavian vein, or a vein that has often been punctured and injected, seeing is difficult.
     - 4.2 Ascending venography: inject dye at the back of the hand, then look at the veins from the wrist up to the central vein. Indication: choose in cases where forearm autogenous access failed, or in cases where the central vein may be narrowed: 1. arm swollen, or unequal size; 2. too many superficial dilated veins; 3. history of trauma to the arm, neck, chest on that side; 4. previous double lumen catheter in the subclavian V.; 5. previous intravenous pacemaker; 6. previous AVF.
     - 4.3 DSA or MRA. Indication: 1. BP of both arms differs by more than 20 mmHg; 2. pulse faint; 3. positive Allen test; 4. previous AVF or AVBG at many positions.

### Thrombosis
- Look at whether it is early or late thrombosis, and whether double lumen or AV access thrombosis.

#### Double Lumen Catheter Thrombosis
- A complication often found with the 2-lumen line; the longer the tube is left, the higher the chance of occlusion.
- Suspect when doing HD and the venous pressure is high; there may be a painful swollen arm together.
- Fixing it, 2 methods: 1. thrombolysis with rTPA: success 90% but the drug is expensive; 2. most often pull the line out and insert a new 2-lumen line: this method works better.

#### AV Access Thrombosis
- Early symptoms are few because there are many collaterals; suspect when, after the needle is inserted, blood does not gush well; doing HD finds venous pressure rising; there is a painful swollen arm; superficial dilated veins seen; palpate a thrill and cannot hear a bruit.
- When measuring BP, find that BP drops during dialysis.
- Diagnosis: from symptoms, color duplex or venogram seeing thrombus in the vein.
- Early thrombosis: found within 3 months, arising from: 1. technical error, e.g. narrowing at the anastomosis, or the vein kinks; 2. stasis in the fistula, e.g. the size of A and V not proper; 3. hematoma compressing.
- Fixing early thrombosis, 2 methods:
  1. Open surgery: open the fistula or graft then clear the clot or do thrombectomy, then fix the cause that made thrombus form, e.g. revise the anastomosis narrowing, or if the graft is too long and kinks, cut off the extra and reconnect; may add thrombolysis too.
  2. In a case with AVF thrombosis but not over 3 weeks, give endovascular treatment, e.g. CDT or catheter directed thrombolysis with lytic agent tPA (tissue plasminogen activator) first; if the fistula has stenosis, PTA + stent is added; but if the occlusion from thrombosis has gone over 3 weeks, thrombolysis seldom gives results.
- Late thrombosis: found after 3 months, usually at the venous site: the cause is often neointimal hyperplasia of the vein hit by turbulent flow often, until the intima thickens, resulting in narrowing; when suspicious, inject dye to do a fistulogram.
- Fixing late thrombosis:
  1. Change the position by jump graft over the area of the old narrowing.
  2. Open the graft and do a graftotomy at the venous site, then do thrombectomy; at closing the graft, may use a patch to help widen it.
  3. In AVF cases, look at the vein first: there are often narrowings lying above the anastomosis 1-2 cm; in this picture an easy way is ordinary revision, e.g. cut and end-to-end, patch, or a new interposition.

### Infection
- Kidney failure patients have low immunity and often have diabetes too, so infection occurs often; in vascular access, infection of the line or the graft often comes from contaminating organisms, most often staph. aureus 70-90%.
- Infection may be found from just after surgery, or it may occur long after use.
- Symptoms: pain, swelling, redness, heat at the skin area, may have pus drainage, with pseudoaneurysm.
- US or CT sees perigraft fluid collection or aneurysm.
- The radiolabeled leukocyte scan, with sensitivity and specificity over 90%, is found especially in textbooks, never seen in real life (the author's remark).

#### Infected Double Lumen Catheter
- Treatment:
  1. If only local symptoms (tender, redness): try wound care and give antibiotic first; if it does not improve, take out the double lumen, then change the catheter and puncture a new site.
  2. If systemic sepsis (fever): do not delay: hurry to take the catheter out together with IV antibiotic for about 3 weeks; hurry to change the catheter, puncturing a new site.

#### Infected AVF
- Figure: infection exposing the AVF (skip).
- Treatment: 1. if only cellulitis: antibiotic with local wound care is enough; 2. infected pseudoaneurysm, palpable as a pulsatile mass: beware rupture and septic emboli dislodging: such a case must be operated: 2.1 ligate the fistula and leave it; or 2.2 exclusion proximal and distal followed by extraanatomical bypass past the infected area.

#### Infected AVBG
- Found more often, arising from skin flora at the time the graft is placed or when needles are inserted to draw blood; symptoms are more severe than infected AVF.
- Treatment: 1. hurry to give IV antibiotic; 2. if the PTFE is not yet attached to the surrounding tissue, or the graft is seen floating out, the infection will spread fast: hurry to take the graft out, then find a place to do a new one.

### Venous Hypertension
- Found in AV access, whether AVF or AVBG.
- Key symptom: swollen hand and swollen arm, purplish discoloration, swelling that increases continuously until the patient feels pain and tightness; in cases that develop slowly we may see superficial dilated veins at the chest wall. Figure: swollen hand with AV access (skip).
- Causes:
  1. From making an AVF by side to side anastomosis where the anastomosis opening is too large, so the blood flow runs into the vein a lot, especially the distal end connecting to the fistula; besides the swollen hand, a suspicious symptom is that when dialysing, blood is drawn out through the vein a lot, so pressure in the vein decreases: the patient says swelling, pain, tightness, redness whenever blood is drawn out.
     - Treatment: by ligating the fistula distal to the venous anastomosis so blood cannot flow on to the end of the hand.
  2. From venous outflow obstruction, found less than item 1: the cause is central vein obstruction, e.g. previous double lumen via the subclavian, then AVF made: suspect when the patient's arm swells; bleeding at the puncture position is abnormal, and when finished and the needle pulled out, it is hard to stop and takes a long time; there are many collateral veins at the upper arm, shoulder, and chest. Figure: central vein stenosis; central venogram: intrastent stenosis in the right subclavian vein; waist on the balloon used for angioplasty; post-angioplasty complete resolution of the stenosis.
     - Treatment: before treating do a venous duplex or venogram to see where the obstruction in the subclavian vein is, to plan how to treat: 1. if it can be bypassed or PTA'd, do so, because if it can be done the blood flows back well and venous pressure falls, making the arm go down; 2. ligate the fistula: blood no longer enters the vein; then find a place to do a new AV access. Figure: venous outflow stenosis (93-year-old with left upper arm straight AVG: 8 cm stenosis from the graft into the axillary vein, successfully angioplastied using an 8 mm balloon) (skip personal details).

### Steal Syndrome
- A symptom arising from lack of blood at the distal part, where the arterial blood passing through the AVF or AVBG does not run distally but runs up proximally back to the heart; often occurs in AVBG more than AVF, and with access at the arm (brachial artery) more than the forearm. Figure: hand photo with ischemic fingers (skip).
- Prominent symptoms are pain from distal ischemia: pain, numbness, cold hand, poor capillary refill, atrophied muscle; if severe may occur as ischemic rest pain or a wound; we can still palpate a thrill at the fistula, but cannot palpate the distal pulse.
- Hand pain with numbness increases when doing dialysis, because blood is drawn out to be filtered, which makes the hand lack even more blood; different from venous HT where swelling is the prominent symptom, and during dialysis symptoms get better.
- Diagnosis is easy: use our finger to compress the patient's fistula, and when pressed the symptoms disappear and we can palpate the distal pulse: when meeting this think of steal syndrome.
- Investigation: use Doppler or inject dye to do a fistulogram.
- Treatment: the purpose is to reduce the flow passing the shunt:
  1. Mild case: found slightly: these have only a cold hand, swollen hand, capillary refill still normal, motor strength and 2 point discrimination still normal, but the radial pulse cannot be palpated, motor still normal: treatment: let the patient raise the arm high, keep warm together with exercise to increase collateral at the hand, and follow up.
  2. Severe case: with severe pain all the time, or tissue necrosis: the way of treatment is chosen by whether the fistula flow is high or low:
     - 2.1 Distal revascularization interval ligation, abbreviated DRIL: used with low flow, meaning cases whose AVBG has a flow rate under 1,000 cc/min: the way of treatment is to increase blood going to the hand by ligating the artery just distal to the anastomosis, then doing a proximal revascularization above the anastomosis so blood flows down to the part distal to the position where we ligated the vessel. Figure: DRIL procedure (cephalic vein, brachial artery, reverse saphenous vein graft) (skip).
     - 2.2 Banding fistula: tie, clip, or wrap as convenient: used with high flow: the purpose is to narrow it, hoping the blood will flow back slowly, by reducing flow through the shunt; this method does not work much because thrombosis occurs.
     - 2.3 Ligation fistula: then find a place to do a new AV access.

### Aneurysm
- Must separate whether it is a true aneurysm that bulges by itself, or a false aneurysm at the anastomosis or at the position punctured with needles.
- Must rule out the cause from proximal venous obstruction too. Figure: arm aneurysm photo (skip).
- Treatment: 1. true aneurysm: no need to treat, even though it looks ugly, unattractive: the craftsman who made it it ugly [remark]: advise the patient to beware so it does not rupture from trauma, enough; 2. false aneurysm: must treat by opening to suture and close the leak, or cut and reconnect anew.

### Ischemic Monomelic Neuropathy (IMN)
- A devastating neurological morbidity with motor and sensory dysfunction of the arm on the side that has the AVF; found rather rarely, but whoever meets it helps greatly from permanent disability; the cause is believed to be a microvascular steal syndrome of the vasa nervorum of the peripheral nerves, making the nerves lack blood, while the skin and muscle still have blood supply.
- Symptoms: may occur immediately after surgery: weak forearm muscle together with severe paresthesia and hand pain; if treatment is late, disability results.
- Diagnosis: from symptoms and the pulse can still be palpated; use it to separate from steal syndrome; no need for duplex, no need for MRI.
- Comparison of Dialysis-Associated Steal Syndrome (DASS) and IMN:

| | DASS | IMN |
|---|---|---|
| Onset of symptoms | variable, hand pain usually not excruciating | immediate post-op, hand pain excruciating |
| Predilection for DM | +++ | ++++ |
| AV access location | brachial or radial artery (radial artery uncommon) | brachial artery |
| Target tissue affected | all soft tissues of hand | all 3 major nerves of the hand |
| Degree of ischemia | severe, diffuse | minimal or none; hand is warm, capillary refill is preserved, palpable radial pulse or audible Doppler signal |
| Digital blood pressures (DBP) | decreased | normal or mildly decreased |
- Treatment: whoever suspects IMN should immediately sacrifice it: close the AVF straight away.
