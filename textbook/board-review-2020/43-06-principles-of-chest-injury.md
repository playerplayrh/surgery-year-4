# Principles of Chest Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 227-277 (PDF pages 2361-2411) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated ๒๒ มกราคม ๒๕๕๙)

## Introduction
- Chest injury is a major topic every surgery resident must know.
- Only ~10% need open thoracotomy; 90% are managed with analgesics, oxygen or ICD alone.
- Mechanism: penetrating and blunt trauma.
- Penetrating wound medial to the nipple line in front, or medial to the scapula behind → may involve great vessels, pulmonary hilum or heart.
- Remember (จำไว้นะว่า): **no local wound exploration in penetrating injury**.
- Practice for a stable penetrating injury with normal exam, equal breath sounds both sides, and normal upright chest film without pneumothorax: do not discharge yet — observe ~24 hours. In the first 6 hours repeat upright chest films, both inspiration and expiration; if still normal (the "**6 hour rule**"), little is likely to follow, but to be safe repeat the film at 24 hours; if still normal, discharge.
- General principle per ATLS: deal with life-threatening problems first — airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, flail chest, massive hemothorax and cardiac tamponade (summarised in this chapter). Trachea, bronchus, lung, heart and esophagus are covered in separate chapters.

## Neck Veins
- Neck veins help tell the cause of shock:
  1. Prominent (distended) neck veins → think of:
     - 1.1 Tension pneumothorax
     - 1.2 Cardiac tamponade
  2. Collapsed neck veins → hypovolemic shock

## Investigation in Chest Injury
- Covers 3 main systems in the chest:
  1. Cardiovascular system (heart, great vessels) — usually FAST and CTA
  2. Respiratory system (trachea, bronchus) — usually bronchoscopy
  3. Digestive system (esophagus) — usually water soluble contrast study and esophagoscopy

## Thoracic Incision for Chest Trauma
- Basic idea only; helps when reading the intrathoracic vascular injury chapter and choosing the right incision.

## Median Sternotomy
- Incise from manubrium to xiphoid, then divide the sternum with a sternal saw (now electric).
- Can be extended up or down.
- Suits heart, ascending and arch of aorta, and pulmonary vein & artery.
- **Right neck extension** — innominate, right common carotid and right subclavian artery.
- **Left neck extension** — left common carotid artery.
- **Left clavicular extension** — aortic arch and great vessels on the left.
- During median sternotomy beware injury to the innominate vein, which lies more anterior.

## Anterolateral Thoracotomy
- Left 4th or 5th intercostal space — suits EDT; can be extended across to the other side to deal with right atrium, SVC or IVC wounds.
- Left 3rd intercostal space plus left supraclavicular incision — proximal left subclavian artery injury.

## Posterolateral Thoracotomy
- Patient must lie on the side → suits elective, not emergency, cases.
- **Left side** used for:
  1. Traumatic rupture of the aorta
  2. Posterior aspect of trachea
  3. Left main pulmonary vein
  4. Diaphragm
  5. Lower esophagus
- **Right side** used for:
  1. SVC
  2. IVC
  3. Right trachea or bronchus
  4. Upper and middle third of thoracic esophagus
- **Disadvantages** (ข้อเสีย)
  1. Unsuitable for emergencies
  2. Bleeding in the bronchus → do not use this incision; in the lateral position blood runs down into the other lung

## Trapdoor or Book Incision
- Components:
  1. Median sternotomy of upper sternum
  2. Left supraclavicular incision and resection of median half of the clavicle
  3. Anterolateral thoracotomy at the 3rd or 4th space
- Used for thoracic outlet vascular injury, especially proximal left subclavian artery.
- The author, as chief resident, helped อาจารย์เกียรติ กังวาลไกล use it once for proximal left subclavian artery injury; in his view trapdoor does not work well — exposure is not great, opening and closing take long, and it is very painful for the patient.

## VATS for Thoracic Injuries
- Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery is used for:
  1. Clotted or retained hemothorax
  2. Empyema
  3. Persistent pneumothorax
  4. Diaphragmatic injury in stab wound at left lower chest
- Disadvantage: needs one lung ventilation.

## Emergency Department Thoracotomy (EDT)
- Also called Emergency Room Thoracotomy (ERT); ATLS calls it Emergency Resuscitative Thoracotomy.
- **Aims of EDT**
  1. Release cardiac tamponade
  2. Control bleeding from the heart or intrathoracic vessels
  3. Treat air embolism
  4. Open cardiac massage
  5. Descending thoracic aorta cross clamp
- **Indications for EDT**
  1. Strong indication = penetrating chest injury (ต้องจำ):
     - 1.1 Cardiac arrest in the ER
     - 1.2 Cardiac arrest before the ER < 15 นาที with continuous prehospital CPR; for blunt only 10 นาที with signs of life still present; for penetrating neck or extremity injury, arrest must be < 5 นาที of prehospital CPR
     - 1.3 Persistent severe hypotension, SBP < 60 mmHg throughout, not responding to resuscitation, from:
       - 1.3.1 Cardiac tamponade
       - 1.3.2 Bleeding, in chest or abdomen
       - 1.3.3 Air embolism
       - 1.3.4 Note: in penetrating chest injury, if BP > 60 mmHg after resuscitation, open the chest in the OR, not the ER
  2. Blunt chest injury — EDT is not favoured; if done, it should be for witnessed cardiopulmonary arrest (cardiac activity present, then arrest in the ER).
- **Survival**
  1. Penetrating injury ~7%
  2. Blunt injury ~0.03%
- **Signs of life — PERM**: Pupil response, EKG activity, Respiratory effort, Motor activity.
- **Contraindications for EDT**
  1. Penetrating trauma with CPR > 15 min
  2. Blunt trauma with CPR > 10 min
- **Summary of changes from Schwartz 9th to 10th ed. (3 points)**
  1. EDT renamed Resuscitative thoracotomy
  2. Blunt trauma time extended from < 5 min to < 10 min
  3. Algorithm background changed from light blue to pale yellow; the algorithm that follows is from the 9th ed., adjusted by the author himself.
- Figure: EDT algorithm (patient in extremis undergoing CPR), as steps —
  1. Blunt trauma → ECG shows any rhythm? No → dead. Yes → CPR < 5 min? No → dead; Yes → EDT.
  2. Penetrating trauma, torso → CPR < 15 min? No → dead; Yes → EDT.
  3. Penetrating trauma, nontorso → CPR < 5 min? No → dead; Yes → EDT.
  4. At EDT: cardiac rhythm? No → tamponade? No → dead; Yes → repair heart. Cardiac rhythm present → treat cause: tamponade → repair heart; thoracic hemorrhage → control; air emboli → hilar X-clamp; extrathoracic hemorrhage → aortic X-clamp.
  5. Then SBP > 70 mmHg? Yes → OR. No → dead.
- Worth following the EAST guidelines 2015, whose conclusions are always cited in standard textbooks; note the signs of life.

**EAST 2015 ED Thoracotomy Guideline**

| Mechanism | Signs of life | Proceed with EDT? |
|---|---|---|
| Penetrating thoracic | Yes | Yes |
| Penetrating thoracic | No | Maybe |
| Penetrating extra-thoracic | Yes | Maybe |
| Penetrating extra-thoracic | No | Maybe |
| Blunt | Yes | Maybe |
| Blunt | No | No |

- Yes = "strongly recommend"; Maybe = "conditionally recommend"; No = "conditionally recommend against".
- Signs of life (EAST): pupillary response, spontaneous ventilation, presence of carotid pulse, measurable or palpable BP, extremity movement, cardiac electrical activity.

## EDT Technique
1. **Operator**: a well-trained surgeon with good judgment, confident, neither too fast nor too slow — these cases need full, complete, well-organised effort.
2. Initial assessment and resuscitation: ET tube, rapid IV.
3. (As printed, a prayer that the patient you are helping has no HIV, HBV, HCV.)
4. **Position**: supine with elevated left arm.
5. **Incision**: anterolateral thoracotomy at about the 5th space — in men below the nipple; in women lift the breast up and cut at the inframammary fold.
6. **Length**: from the sternal edge at the sternocostal junction to the latissimus dorsi, or about the lower edge of the bed.
7. Sharp transection of intercostal muscle (printed "muscal") — no bleeding, since BP is zero.
8. Open pleura.
9. Finochietto retractor to spread the ribs, with the handle/crank placed toward the bottom, because the incision may need extending to the other side as a clamshell. Divide the sternum with a Lebsche knife and hammer — about 10 blows cuts through.
10. Remember: if the patient survives, ligate the internal mammary artery both proximally and distally before closing the chest — otherwise when BP returns some bleed enough to need re-opening.
11. Even for a not-very-skilled surgeon, entering the chest for EDT should take ≤ 30 วินาที.
12. Open the pericardium longitudinally, about 1-2 cm anterior to the phrenic nerve; scoop out blood; plug the cardiac hole with a Foley; internal cardiac massage may be needed at this point.
    - Internal paddles for defibrillation are positioned on the anterior and posterior aspects of the heart.
13. Suspected air embolism: lift and push the lung medially; if needed, clamp the hilum or twist the hilum.
14. Locate the descending aorta beneath the esophagus (feel the NG tube if one is in place) and cross clamp the aorta to direct blood to brain and heart.
15. If BP returns, treat the cause (brief summary):
    - 15.1 **Cardiac injury**: suture the hole, or close with staples; just enough to stop bleeding. Two holes → close both, then move to the OR for definitive treatment.
    - 15.2 **Bleeding from pulmonary hilum or lung**: cross clamp the hilum, or twist the lung 180° to stop bleeding (divide the inferior pulmonary ligament first, as illustrated). Lung parenchymal bleeding → linear stapler, or clamp the lung temporarily.
      - A Satinsky clamp is used to clamp the pulmonary hilum for hemorrhage control or to prevent further bronchovenous air embolism.
    - 15.3 Bleeding outside the chest causing arrest → aortic cross clamp only, then take to the OR for the source, usually in the abdomen.
    - 15.4 Air embolism — covered further below (ทีปี 3 ต้องรู้).

## Air Embolism
- 2 types:
  1. Systemic or arterial air embolism
  2. Venous air embolism

## Arterial Air Embolism
- An often-overlooked complication of pulmonary injury: air leaks from bronchial injury through a bronchovenous fistula into the pulmonary vein, then to the left heart; ventricular contraction ejects the air, disastrous if the emboli enter a coronary artery or a carotid artery to the brain.
- **Suspect when:**
  1. Penetrating chest injury with hemopneumothorax, air bubbling from the ICD, and the event typically follows ET intubation on a ventilator or bagging — positive pressure forces more air from the bronchus through the fistula; a few squeezes of the bag and the patient arrests.
  2. Focal or localizing neurological signs without head injury.
  3. Theoretically, air bubbles in retinal vessels on exam — the author thinks nobody has time for fundoscopy in an arresting patient; it does not work.
- Only 1 cc of air blocking a coronary artery can cause arrest.
- During EDT after cross clamping the pulmonary hilum, follow these steps (จำ step ให้ดี):
  1. Immediately Trendelenburg's position, tilted right side down, so air floats to the apex of the left ventricle
  2. Pulmonary hilar cross clamp
  3. 18 G needle on a 50 cc syringe to aspirate air from the left ventricle and aortic root
     - Sequential aspiration sites in bronchovenous air embolism: left ventricle (1), aortic root (2), and the right coronary artery.
  4. Air in a coronary artery: gently massage to push the air out first, or aspirate with a needle on a tuberculin syringe — easy to say, very hard to do
  5. If the patient survives, repair the pulmonary vein and bronchus, or resect the lung
- Some advise: if arterial air embolism is suspected at intubation, pass the tube tip beyond the bronchial tear — the author considers this impractical (no time for flexible bronchoscopy in a dying patient).
- In real ER life it is mostly chaos; by the time you think of tipping the head down, the patient is already gone (as printed).

## Venous Air Embolism
- Air enters a peripheral vein (internal jugular, subclavian or innominate vein) into the systemic veins.
- Air volume that blocks right ventricular outflow must be large — said to be about 100-200 cc; venous blood cannot reach the lungs → shock and death.
- Once diagnosed: quickly put the patient head down, legs up, left side down = Trendelenburg's position with left side down or right side up; air then collects at the apex of the right ventricle and right atrium; if a cut-down line is in place, aspirate the air through it.
- Figure: aspiration of air from the right atrium via a catheter from the SVC, tip about 3 cm into the right atrium where air collects.

## Mediastinal Traversing Injury
- Penetrating injury entering one side of the neck or chest with a weapon or bullet track heading to the opposite side of the chest; mortality is high.
- Previously every chest was opened, with 40-60% negative exploration → in hemodynamically stable patients a **selective approach** is recommended.
- Unstable, clearly dying → quick bilateral ICD, then EDT (left), and clamshell to the other side if needed.
- Not quite needing EDT → supine, prep from neck to knees, left or right anterolateral thoracotomy depending on which side you suspect is hit or more serious.
- Hemodynamically stable → selective management; investigation of choice = **CTA with esophagogram**:
  1. Physical exam and film chest PA and lateral view
  2. CT chest first, then CTA, water soluble esophagography; if contrast swallow shows no leak → esophagoscopy, and finish with flexible bronchoscopy
  3. Chance of significant injury:
     - 3.1 Occult cardiac injury 5-10%
     - 3.2 Occult great vessel injury 20%
     - 3.3 Esophageal injury 15-30%

## Tube Thoracostomy
- Better known as ICD (intercostal drainage); technique is assumed known at this level.
- Figure: Chest tube placement — 2-3 cm transverse incision at the 5th intercostal space anterior to the mid-axillary line; local anesthetic infiltration of skin and tissues; proximal end of tube clamped and advanced through the incision into the pleural space with a Kelly clamp; tube sutured and taped in place.
- **Practical aspect**
  1. If unsure (when in doubt), insert at the 5th or 6th space between anterior and midaxillary lines — thinnest chest wall muscle here; dissect over the top of the rib to avoid the vessels under the rib above.
  2. Chest drain size need not be as large as before; do not insert lower than the 6th space or you may hit the diaphragm.
  3. Before inserting the tube, sweep a finger in the pleural space: lung felt, adhesions, blood or air; sweeping low may feel diaphragm or stomach.
  4. Never insert ICD through the open chest wound to avoid a new incision — whoever does this will be scolded.
  5. Small pneumothorax or hemothorax but needing anesthesia or positive pressure → ICD first; also before long-distance referral, especially by air.
  6. After thoracotomy, place the ICD above the incision if possible.
  7. Connect ICD to the bottle.
- Figure: bottle systems — single bottle: tube from patient under 2 cm of water, a second tube open to atmosphere vents air; two bottles: fluid drainage bottle then water seal (2 cm fluid); three bottles: drainage collection → water seal (2 cm) → suction control (10-14 cm).
  - **1 bottle**: drains air leak alone, or residual post-operative blood, only if volume and rate are small.
  - **2 bottles**: drains air leak together with blood or fluid.
  - **3 bottles**: when suction pressure must be controlled (passive suction) at a constant level.
- **When to remove ICD**
  1. Hemothorax — remove when:
     - 1.1 Lung fully expanded
     - 1.2 Drainage < 50 cc/day; the author waits about 24 hours after bleeding stops
  2. Pneumothorax:
     - 2.1 Lung fully expanded
     - 2.2 No air leak; in practice the author waits for good adhesion between parietal and visceral pleura — usually removes ICD about 48 hours after air bubbling stops

## Indications for Operative Treatment of Chest Injuries
- **Emergency department thoracotomy**
  1. Cardiac arrest after blunt trauma with loss of signs of life in the trauma bay
  2. Cardiac arrest after penetrating trauma with loss of signs of life in the field or in the trauma bay
- **Emergency (in OR)**
  1. Massive hemothorax 1,500 ml in blunt injury or > 1,000 ml in penetrating injury
  2. Pericardial effusion after penetrating chest injury
  3. Acute deterioration after penetrating chest injury
  4. Major tracheobronchial injury
  5. Chest wall disruption
- **Urgent (in OR)**
  1. Ongoing thoracic bleeding 200 mL/hr × 3 hr
  2. Ongoing massive parenchymal air leak
  3. Radiographic evidence of vascular, tracheal or esophageal injury after mediastinal traverse
  4. Cardiac herniation
- **Non-acute**
  1. Evacuation of retained hemothorax despite placement of 2 chest tubes
  2. Decortication / drainage of empyema
  3. Repair chronic diaphragmatic hernia
  4. Repair fistulous aerodigestive tract connection
- Ongoing/continuous hemorrhage: some textbooks use 200 cc/hr × 3 hr, others 250 cc/hr × 3 hr; for small-built Thai patients the author holds 200 cc/hr × 3 hr is enough to go to the OR.
- Evacuation of retained clot is now more often by VATS than open.

## Practical Plain Film Chest Interpretation
- These days we are dazzled by expensive equipment more than clinical signs and plain films, which tell a lot (the author grumbles).
- **Pitfalls in interpretation** (ข้อควรระวังการแปลผลผิด)
  1. Tracheal shift may just be from the patient's head being turned.
  2. Supine film shows hemothorax poorly — sit the patient up 45-60° to see it better.
  3. Lung opacity may be atelectasis as well as lung contusion.
- **Approach to reading the film**
  1. Bird's eye view — look for blood or air; compare lung markings both sides; trachea position and diaphragm level.
  2. Bilateral one intercostal space — compare each ICS side by side; look for fractures of rib, sternum, clavicle, scapula.
  3. Lung marking from central to periphery — pneumothorax shows a hair line density.
- Plain film findings that should raise suspicion of specific injuries:

| Findings | Diagnoses to consider |
|---|---|
| Any rib fracture | Pneumothorax, pulmonary contusion |
| Fracture of first three ribs or sternoclavicular fracture-dislocation | Airway or great-vessel injury |
| Fracture of lower ribs 9 to 12 | Abdominal injury (liver, spleen, kidney) |
| Two or more rib fractures in two or more places | Flail chest, pulmonary contusion |
| Scapular fracture | Great-vessel injury, pulmonary contusion, brachial plexus injury |
| Sternal fracture | Cardiac contusion |
| Mediastinal widening | Great-vessel injury, sternal fracture, thoracic spine injury |
| Persistent large pneumothorax or air leak after chest tube insertion | Bronchial tear |
| Mediastinal air | Esophageal disruption, tracheal injury, pneumoperitoneum |
| GI gas pattern in the chest (loculated air) | Diaphragmatic rupture |
| NG tube in the chest | Diaphragmatic rupture or ruptured esophagus |
| Air fluid level in the chest | Hemopneumothorax or diaphragmatic rupture |
| Disrupted diaphragm | Abdominal visceral injury |
| Free air under the diaphragm | Ruptured hollow abdominal viscus |

## Tension Pneumothorax
- Pneumothorax with a **one way check valve mechanism**: on inspiration the valve opens and air leaks into the pleural cavity; on expiration it closes, trapping air. Air accumulates until pleural pressure turns from negative to positive; above atmospheric pressure → tension collapses the lung, the mediastinum shifts to the opposite side, SVC compression reduces venous return → cardiac output falls → tachycardia → finally shock.
- **One way check valve arises from:**
  1. Oblique penetrating chest wound
  2. Oblique laceration of lung parenchyma
- **Symptoms/signs**
  1. Dyspnea, cyanosis, restlessness; after intubation the bag feels stiff to squeeze, or on a ventilator airway pressure keeps rising with constant alarms
  2. Distended neck veins
  3. Fast, weak pulse; BP starts to fall
  4. Trachea shifted from midline to the opposite side
  5. Hyperresonant percussion; absent breath sounds on that side
  6. Oxygen saturation gradually falls
- **Diagnosis** — clinical; no x-ray confirmation needed. Tension is extremely urgent; sending for a film risks arrest in the x-ray room. Always think of it in trauma patients during the primary survey: after intubation and a few bag squeezes, instead of improving the patient turns blue and arrests.
- **Treatment**
  1. Immediate needle thoracocentesis — dramatic improvement
  2. Then ICD size 28-32F
  3. Oxygen
- **Technique of needle decompression**
  1. Needle length depends on chest wall thickness; usually medicut size 14 or a needle longer than 5 cm. ATLS 9th ed.: a needle > 5 cm reaches the pleural space in > 50%; > 8 cm reaches it in > 90%.
  2. Insert at the midclavicular line, 2nd ICS, just over the upper border of the lower rib, perpendicular to the chest wall.
     - Figure labels: directly over the top of the third rib; directly under each rib is an artery, vein and nerve (neurovascular bundle).
  3. Entering the pleural cavity gives a hiss of air, or the plunger of an attached syringe pops out.
  4. Needle puncture converts tension pneumothorax into a small open pneumothorax; then insert ICD.
  5. If ICD is not yet available: attach IV tubing to the needle with the other end under water, or tie a glove finger over the needle hub and cut its tip to let air out.

## Open Pneumothorax
- Open chest wall wound larger than the narrowest part of the upper airway, or > 2/3 of the tracheal diameter → outside air is sucked into the pleural cavity on inspiration → compresses the lung, mediastinal shift, narrowing of SVC and IVC → reduced venous return.
- **Diagnosis** — easy: gaping chest wall wound; air heard or bubbles seen moving in and out with breathing = **sucking chest wound**.
- **Treatment**
  1. Start with a 3 sides occlusive dressing, leaving the 4th side open as an outlet
  2. (items 2 and 3 blank as printed)
  4. Air can escape but not enter — converts open into closed
  5. Pneumothorax — the patient usually improves immediately
  6. No gauze available → cover the wound with your palm, lifting it intermittently
  7. In the OR insert ICD — never through the open wound, which is usually dirty and carries infection into the chest
  8. Close the wound

## Flail Chest
- Fracture of ≥ 2 ribs, each broken in ≥ 2 places → unstable chest wall from a free float segment.
- Classic sign: **paradoxical movement**:
  1. Inspiration: pleural negative pressure rises, chest wall expands but the float segment sinks in
  2. Expiration: chest wall falls but the float segment bulges out
- Posterior fractures, covered by thick muscle and the scapula and with the patient lying on them, cause fewer problems than anterior rib fractures.
- **Respiratory failure — 4 Ps**
  1. **Pulmonary contusion** — found with flail chest in up to 90%; the most important cause
  2. **Paradoxical (pendulous) movement** — reduces gas exchange while work of breathing rises
  3. **Pain** — hypoventilation, then hypoxia and hypercarbia
  4. **Pneumothorax** — may coexist
- **Treatment** — principle: correct hypoventilation:
  1. Adequate analgesia; some need intercostal or epidural block
  2. IV enough to correct shock, but avoid overhydration
  3. Chest physiotherapy, percussion, suction of secretions
  4. Monitor in ICU: examination, blood gas and serial chest films
  5. About 20-40% develop respiratory failure — RR > 35/min, alar nasi flaring, severe shock, underlying COPD, PaO2 ≤ 60 or PaCO2 ≥ 50 mmHg → intubate and ventilate
- **What NOT to do in flail chest now** (สิ่งที่ไม่ควรทำ):
  1. Prophylactic endotracheal intubation
  2. Chest wall stabilization by pressing with a hand, lying on the flail segment, sticky plaster or pulling with a towel clip
  3. Over hydration
- **Internal stabilization** — still has a place:
  1. Already doing thoracotomy → fix the fracture with miniplate
  2. A minority (~10%) without pulmonary contusion who need intubation: ORIF with miniplate shortens time on the endotracheal tube; the author personally thinks tracheostomy with ventilator is easier than operating to fix the ribs (ความเห็นส่วนตัว).

## Massive Hemothorax
- Basics:
  1. **Mild** hemothorax: ≤ 350 cc
  2. **Moderate** hemothorax: 350-1500 cc
  3. **Massive/severe** hemothorax: > 1500 cc; besides blood loss, the lung is also compressed
- Estimating blood volume from an upright chest film (often the patient cannot stand):
  1. Blunted costophrenic angle ≈ 300-350 cc
  2. Fluid level 5 cm above the diaphragm ≈ 1000 cc
  3. Opacity outlining the whole lung curve, > 1 cm from the chest wall ≈ 1500 cc
- **Symptoms and signs**
  1. Tracheal deviation
  2. Flat neck veins — but distended if tension coexists
  3. Dull percussion, absent breath sounds
- In massive hemothorax the most common bleeding vessel is the **intercostal artery**.
- Retained blood or clot → infection; clot on the lung surface promotes fibrosis; if thick → restrictive lung.
- **Treatment**
  1. IV resuscitation, treat shock
  2. ICD size 34 or 36; much blood remaining → a second ICD to drain better
  3. Two ICDs still not enough → no third tube; finish with surgery, open or scope as the situation allows (usually open)

## Chest Wall Injury
- Skin, subcutaneous and muscle wounds are skipped; only fracture rib and fracture sternum are covered.

## Fracture Ribs
- Each rib fracture site loses about 50 cc of blood (1 rib broken in 1 place → 50 cc).
- Problems: lung contusion and pulmonary parenchymal abnormality raise the risk of pulmonary morbidity; associated injury from fracture ends piercing inner organs; pain causes shallow breathing and retained sputum → pneumonia.
- **Observations** (ข้อสังเกต)
  1. Ribs 1 and 2 lie deep; fracture means very severe injury → beware associated thoracic vessel injury at the thoracic outlet.
  2. Ribs 8-12 fractured → beware liver and spleen rupture.
- **Symptoms** — pain, worse with deep breathing or hard coughing; the patient keeps still, breathes shallowly, cannot clear sputum.
- **Film chest** — about 54% of patients with rib fracture have a normal chest film. If the first film shows no fracture but suspicion remains, repeat the film in 2 weeks; a true fracture shows callus at the fracture ends. Fractures at cartilage or the costochondral junction may not be seen.
- **Treatment**
  1. Adequate analgesia; pain is worse in the first week then gradually eases, improving within 2-3 weeks; most fractures unite within 1 month.
  2. Oral plus injected analgesics not enough → intercostal nerve block: inject marcaine at the fractured rib and also the rib above and below.
  3. Epidural anesthesia gives the best pain control, especially in elderly with rib fractures; reduces incidence of nosocomial pneumonia better than IV morphine, but ICU and hospital length of stay are no different.
  4. Breathing exercise for adequate clearance of pulmonary secretion.
- Patients without significant comorbidity (e.g. COPD) can be discharged; with COPD, old age, frailty or inability to care for themselves → admit with intercostal nerve block.
- Figure: sites of regional analgesia for rib fracture — intrapleural, intercostal, paravertebral, epidural.
- Since 2008 the concept of rib stabilization has returned: a report in World Journal of Surgery 2009 by Trunkey DD, using MIS rib fixation, reported shorter ventilator time and hospital stay, less pain, and best of all less chest wall deformity.

## Fracture Sternum
- Usually blunt trauma — chest striking the steering wheel.
- Usually a transverse fracture near the sternal angle; mostly incomplete undisplaced fracture; often with rib fractures.
- Previously high mortality from associated injury, especially heart and great vessels; now lower with seat belt use.
- **Examination** — tenderness or palpable crepitus; displacement shows a step/depression.
- Film chest: look at the lateral view.
- **Treatment**
  1. Conservative as for rib fracture; sternal fractures unite in about 6-12 weeks.
  2. **Indications for surgery**
     - 2.1 Non-union
     - 2.2 Ugly depressed deformity
     - 2.3 Respiratory failure — pain too severe to breathe
     - 2.4 Complete fracture with displacement
- Surgery: open fixation with plate or wire.

## Simple Pneumothorax
- Differs from tension: a small tear, and pleural pressure equal to or below atmospheric; even with much air in the chest, symptoms are milder than tension, where the one way valve makes pleural pressure much higher.
- **Treatment**
  1. Observation — asymptomatic and pneumothorax < 25%: on film the lung apex is pushed down ≤ 4 cm, or the lateral lung edge is ≤ 1 cm from the chest wall. Air is absorbed at 1.25% per day.
  2. **Indications for ICD**
     - 2.1 Symptomatic
     - 2.2 Pneumothorax > 25%
     - 2.3 Needs anesthesia or ventilator
     - 2.4 Referral by air
- Caution: an observed case may progress to tension; in practice the author usually just inserts an ICD (as printed, "too lazy to worry").

## Mild to Moderate Hemothorax
- Insert ICD in every case from the start, to prevent clotted hemothorax, which if poorly treated becomes fibrothorax or empyema.
- Incomplete evacuation after one ICD → add another ICD.
- Antibiotic not necessary — does not reduce pleural infection.
- A minority recommend observing mild hemothorax for fear of introducing infection; the author does not follow this approach.

## Retained Clot Hemothorax
- Retained clot hemothorax is the **single most important risk factor for development of empyema**; it also causes fibrothorax, trapping the lung so it cannot fully expand.
- Clot also leads to fibrothorax with lung entrapment.
- Film chest: lung not expanded. CT chest: clot collection seen.
- **Treatment**
  1. VATS instead of open thoracotomy. Previously one waited ~2 weeks; now only 2-4 days — if much clot remains while still soft and easy to remove, VATS scoops it out.
  2. Thrombolysis via ICD — limited by drug allergy and systemic absorption raising bleeding risk; gives some results in children but in adults usually does not work (as printed, "จิ บอกให้").
  3. A second ICD or applied negative pressure suction usually does not work either, like thrombolysis.

## Esophageal Injury
- Mostly from penetrating injury, e.g. GSW entering one side of the chest and exiting the other. Stable patients get CT chest, usually showing posterior mediastinal hematoma with a small amount of air ("smaal amout" as printed); treat as esophageal perforation.
- The trauma covered here is the blunt group: a blow to the lower sternum or upper abdomen while the patient vomits or retches violently → lower esophagus tears as in Boerhaave.
- **Film chest** shows:
  1. Mediastinal air or pneumomediastinum — most classic
  2. Pneumothorax or hemothorax
- Confirm with oral water soluble contrast study.
- **Treatment** — upper 2/3: right thoracotomy; lower 1/3: left thoracotomy; then close the hole as a reinforced repair with wide drainage.
- Figure: reinforcement options — intercostal muscle flap or parietal pleura patch over the esophageal repair.
