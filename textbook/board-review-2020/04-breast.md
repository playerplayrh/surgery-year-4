# Surgery of the Breast 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 193-316 (PDF pages 197-320)

## Introduction (book p.193)
- The author's chapter is the 7/2020 revision. It is revised often because breast surgery has fast-changing data, trials and guidelines, and many debated topics where consensus is unclear.
- Sources added in 2017-2019: AJCC TNM 8th ed 2017, Cameron ed.12 2017, NCCN 2019, St. Gallen 2019, plus teachers' input.
- It is a summary only; consult textbooks or experts for more depth.
- Breast is important to mothers, infants and women who want a fast-growing, shapely breast; men may be affected too.
- A good breast shape depends on the Cooper suspensory ligaments, which tether the breast to the chest wall. When cancer invades Cooper ligament it pulls on it, producing skin dimpling/retraction.

## Anatomy (p.194-195)
- Breast = modified apocrine sweat gland with 2 parts: glandular tissue (lobules + ducts, hormone-responsive) and stromal tissue (fat + fibrous tissue filling the space between lobules).
- Figure (labels): nipple-areolar complex, lactiferous sinus, breast duct, fat, Cooper's ligament, lobules; layers from superficial: epidermis, lymphatics, dermis, Cooper's ligament, lobules/ducts, retromammary space, deep fascia, pectoralis major.
- Extent: from rib 2 or 3 down to rib 6 or 7; medially to the edge of the sternum, laterally to the edge of latissimus dorsi (about midaxillary line). The tail of Spence extends through the axillary fold.
- 5 quadrants: upper outer, upper inner, lower outer, lower inner, central (beneath nipple/areola).
- Most breast tissue lies in the upper outer quadrant, so breast disease is most often found there.
- Parenchyma: 15-20 lobes, each made of many lobules; ends in acini (alveoli) -> small ductules -> collecting (lactiferous) duct 2-4 mm, 15-20 ducts opening at the nipple.
- Nipple-areola complex: nipple has the duct openings (infant sucks); areola contains sebaceous and sweat glands; the glands raise the skin into bumps called Montgomery tubercles.
- Blood supply: (1) internal mammary artery - supplies the inner quadrants; (2) lateral thoracic artery; (3) thoracoacromial artery; (4) 2nd, 3rd, 4th posterior intercostal arteries - supply the outer part (via the 2nd-4th interspaces). Figure labels also include anterior intercostal perforating arteries and axillary artery.
- Lymphatic drainage is covered in the cancer section; nerve supply under operative complications.

## Hormones and pregnancy (p.195)
- Estrogen: initiates ductal development (duct growth).
- Progesterone: differentiation of duct epithelium and lobular development (gland growth).
- Prolactin: stimulates milk production in late pregnancy and postpartum.
- In pregnancy the breast enlarges from hormonal stimulation, with accumulation of lymphocytes, plasma cells and eosinophils in the tissue.
- After delivery, milk secretion begins; in the first 2-3 days the milk is low in fat but rich in antibodies = colostrum.

## Development of the breast (p.195-196)
- Develops from about week 6 of gestation from thickened ectoderm, along the milk line (axilla to groin).
- By week 8 buds appear along the milk line, then regress except one pair on the chest, which becomes the breast. By week 16 duct formation starts (still primitive ducts); by week 24 the ducts become rounded and distal ducts join the acini.
- Until childhood only the major lactiferous ducts exist; the gland is not yet developed.
- At about 9-11 years, estrogen and progesterone are released and ducts elongate into terminal ductules; fat increases, forming the breast bud, and the shape changes from flat to a projecting one.
- A breast bud (age 9-11) can be mistaken for a tumor/cyst and wrongly excised; it is a normal pubertal change (do not remove it, which would permanently flatten the breast).

Tanner staging of breast development (figure with its own captions):

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Prepubertal: elevation of the papilla only (flat areola) |
| 2 | Breast bud forms below the areola; papilla and areola rise as a small mound; areolar diameter increases |
| 3 | Further enlargement and elevation of breast and areola; no separation of breast contours |
| 4 | Areola and papilla form a secondary mound projecting above the contour of the breast |
| 5 | Mature: areolar mound recedes into the general breast contour; papilla continues to project |

## Congenital abnormalities (p.197-198)
- Ectopic breast: abnormal location along the milk line, from axilla to the inguinal ligament/groin; often bilateral.
  - Extra complete breast = polymastia; extra nipple only = polythelia.
  - Treatment: if no symptoms, leave it; keep it if it does not interfere with life.
  - Hereditary ectopic breast may coexist with duplication of renal pelvis or ureter, and with renal cancer.
  - Indications for surgery: cosmetic; suspicion of cancer.
- Congenital absence:
  1. Amastia: no breast at all (some have only a bud or hypoplasia). Associated with Poland's syndrome.
  2. Poland's syndrome (named after Alfred Poland): defect in development of the proximal subclavian artery -> reduced blood flow to pectoral region and distal limb -> tissue loss there. Components: unilateral amastia or hypoplasia of the breast; absent pectoralis major and minor; hypoplasia of the shoulder; some have short, fused fingers (brachysyndactyly). Treatment: it is NOT linked with cancer risk; for the flat chest, reconstruct with an implant if the patient wants.
  3. Amazia: nipple only, no breast tissue.
  4. Athelia: breast tissue present but no nipple.
- Accessory/supranumerary breast (polymastia): more than 2 breasts, along the milk line; common in the axilla, looks like a fatty lump; it enlarges, becomes painful and tense before menses, in pregnancy, and after delivery may even give milk if a nipple is present.
  - Management: usually reassurance, normal follow-up (accessory tissue can develop cancer like normal breast).
  - Indications for excision: symptoms (severe pain, a large mass pulling the shoulder/arm), interferes with activity, cosmetic problem.

## Diffuse hypertrophy (p.199)
- Seen in healthy pubertal girls: breasts become so large they cause chest weight, back pain, shoulder grooving/postural problems.
- Believed to be due to over-sensitivity of the breast to estrogen. Treatment: reduction mammoplasty.

## Pendulous breast (p.199)
- Normally the nipple lies above the inframammary line; sagging below it is ptosis, which can hurt self-confidence.
- Figure: degrees of ptosis = normal; grade 1 mild sagging; grade 2 moderate; grade 3 significant; pseudoptosis (lower breast sagging, nipple still above the fold); parenchymal maldistribution (unusual shape).
- Treatment if bothersome: plastic surgery (mastopexy).

## Infection and inflammatory disorders (p.200-)
- Divided into lactational mastitis and non-lactational mastitis.
- Lactational mastitis: during breast feeding, usually bacterial (fungal rare). Non-lactational: e.g. skin inflammation (hair follicle/sebaceous gland inflammation) spreading to the breast.

### Acute mastitis (p.200)
- Means the breast is inflamed but there is no abscess yet.
- Mastitis neonatorum: not the mother; it is the newborn's breast, which is inflamed after birth from maternal hormones; organism usually staph.
  - Treatment: (1) antibiotic first; (2) if it becomes an abscess, drain with an incision away from the nipple so that ducts and the breast bud are not traumatized.
- Lactational mastitis:
  - Typically 2-6 weeks after delivery, during breast feeding, when milk is stagnant.
  - Diagnosis is clinical: pain, swelling, redness, heat in the breast; no investigation or CT is needed.
  - Most common organisms: Staph. aureus and Streptococcus; TB can occur. If strep: generalized mastitis without clear borders. If staph: tends to spread to the other side and form an abscess.
  - Initial treatment:
    1. IV or oral antibiotic for 10-14 days (Schwartz prefers IV); choose cloxacillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate because staph is the usual cause. Plain penicillin is often ineffective; if allergic to cloxacillin use erythromycin or clarithromycin. Drugs to avoid in breastfeeding (excreted in milk, harm the infant): tetracycline, chloramphenicol, aminoglycoside, sulfonamide, metronidazole, ciprofloxacin.
    2. Breast support, warm compress before feeding to stimulate the let-down (milk ejection) reflex; after feeding, cold compress to reduce swelling. If the baby cannot suck the inflamed breast, it is still possible to feed from it, but the milk may carry organisms; in practice "play safe": pump and discard the milk, stop feeding from the affected side.
    3. Do not stop breast feeding completely: continue, and pump out milk to reduce congestion and promote drainage.
  - Antibiotics are theoretically not needed in every case; criteria used to decide to give antibiotic: (1) signs of infection (fever, raised WBC); (2) nipple fissure; (3) severe symptoms from the start; (4) no improvement within 12-24 hours.

### Breast abscess (p.201)
- Mostly after delivery like acute mastitis; no more than 25% occur in non-lactating women.
- Cause: usually a wound/skin break or just a fissure, together with milk stasis. Organism usually staph; TB very rare.
- Suspect when a mastitis case treated does not improve: high fever, a tender focus, swelling, shiny tight red skin, fluctuation.
- US is increasingly used to separate mastitis from abscess: abscess = macrolobulated cyst with internal debris, peripheral hypervascularity, with or without loculation.
- Treatment trend has moved from routine incision and drainage to needle aspiration with irrigation of pus (repeat every 2-3 days until pus is cleared). Open drainage is used when the skin is thin or about to rupture, or when there is necrosis.
  1. Urgent, adequate drainage.
     1.1 US-guided aspiration is likely to fail (and needs I&D) when the abscess is multiloculated, has thick walls, is larger than 5 cm, or skin necrosis is present; make an incision at the fluctuant/most tender point.
     1.2 If the lesion is smaller than 5 cm and the site is deep: initial treatment is aspiration every 2-3 days, then consider combined percutaneous catheter drainage under US guide; if the abscess is larger than 5 cm, drain it or do I&D.
     1.3 Do I&D for multiloculated abscess, overlying skin necrosis, and cases needing open drainage; also biopsy the abscess wall to rule out underlying breast cancer. Do this in cases where antibiotic + drainage the usual way has failed.
  2. Give antibiotic for a long time (10-14 days for mastitis) - reduces septicemia and helps the drain wound heal faster.
  3. Pump the milk out, especially from the affected side if there is an abscess; the baby may still feed from the other side.
  4. After clinical resolution, schedule a repeat US about 6 weeks later.
- Chronic recurrent abscess, cellulitis with a tender mass that does not resolve, or a wound that does not heal after drainage: suspect breast cancer (it may be inflammatory cancer that was drained). Do a core needle biopsy.
- Summary: a breast abscess must be drained; acute mastitis alone should get antibiotic.
- Non-lactational breast abscess usually arises from ductal ectasia or an infected cyst.

## Mammary duct ectasia (p.202)
- Dilated, obstructed milk ducts from stasis; commonly subareolar, cause unknown; age 30-60 years.
- Problem: it often has chronic infection too. Presentations (figure): nipple discharge, periductal mastitis, abscess, fistula, nipple retraction, mass.
- Some present as a subareolar breast abscess = periductal mastitis; some abscesses burst and leave a tract opening on the skin = mammary duct fistula.
- Chronic inflammation causes nipple retraction or abnormal nipple discharge.
- Duct ectasia itself carries no cancer risk.
- Treatment: treat the consequences of inflammation, i.e. periductal mastitis and mammary duct fistula.

## Mammillary fistula (p.202-203)
- Same entity as mammary duct fistula: infection of the central part of the breast (periareolar infection). Often attributed to Zuska (Zuska's disease): a patient with recurrent periareolar or subcutaneous abscess, then a fistula opening at the nipple or at the areolar edge.
- Occurs in both sexes; not related to lactation.
- Some call it recurrent periductal mastitis. Smoking is the main risk factor (about 90%); vitamin A deficiency (squamous metaplasia) is a secondary factor.
- Findings: painful erythematous subareolar masses that become abscesses and drain through a fistula, healing and recurring. Culture for organisms before treatment (also consider fungus, TB).
- Treatment: antibiotics (Augmentin), incision and drainage.
  - Fistula surgery: pass a fine lacrimal probe through the external opening along the tract; then lay open the tract as for fistulotomy, leave the wound to heal by itself (fistulotomy). In some cases where inflammation is tolerable, excise the fistula (fistulectomy) with primary closure.
- Total duct excision: use in older age >50 years, or when diffuse infection involves >50% of the areolar circumference, or nipple inversion. Before surgery, if age >35 years do a mammogram first; a biopsy may be needed to exclude comedo necrosis in DCIS and inflammatory breast cancer (IBC).

## Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis / lobulitis (p.204)
- Rare, benign inflammatory disease; histology: noncaseating granulomas centered on lobules; sometimes with fistula or abscess. Cause unknown; not related to smoking (unlike mammary fistula). Seen in women aged 30-40 years who are pregnant or recently so; some believe underlying autoimmune disease.
- Symptoms: mild pain, usually no fever.
- Some cases are hard to distinguish from inflammatory breast cancer; imaging may show an abscess-like lesion resembling a complex cyst, so core needle biopsy (CNB) is needed.
- Treatment:
  1. Steroids work well; for severe pain give NSAIDs; resolution takes 2-3 months.
  2. Treat the underlying autoimmune cause, if any.
  3. Surgery is rarely used; except for an abscess that must be drained; avoid I&D because a persistent draining sinus tract results.
- Figure (algorithm): suspicious presentation of GM -> trial of antibiotics -> radiographic evaluation -> core biopsy with cultures. If no GM -> treat underlying cause. If GM: localized findings/mild symptoms -> excision, observation or steroids; generalized findings/severe symptoms -> steroids; failure -> mastectomy; improvement -> observation.

## Mondor's disease (p.205)
- Disease of a vein, usually in the lateral part of the breast.
- Pathophysiology: thrombophlebitis of the thoracoepigastric, lateral thoracic or superficial epigastric vein at the chest wall; cause unknown but associated with trauma.
- Usually unilateral, involves only one vein; no cancer risk.
- Patient has breast pain and a tender cord along the side of the breast.
- Diagnosis is clinical; US, mammogram or biopsy are not required, but if done and a mass is found near the tender cord, evaluate it.
- Treatment: (1) NSAID for pain; (2) local warm compression and wearing a brassiere for support.
- Usually resolves within 4-6 weeks (self-limiting; counsel and observe).
- Surgery only in those who fail NSAID: if short segment, excise the vein; if long segment, ligate the proximal and distal parts only.

## Benign breast diseases (p.205-206)
- Non-cancer breast conditions; present with mass, cyst, mastalgia, nipple discharge, pruritus.
- Figure (pie, incidence of breast disease): fibrocystic changes 40%; no disease 30%; miscellaneous benign 13%; cancer 10%; fibroadenoma 7%.
- Classical grouping by disease: fibrocystic disease (FCD), fibroadenoma, gynecomastia.
- Newer grouping: ANDI concept = Aberration of Normal Development and Involution (Hughes 1987; Dixon 1997). Based on the idea that the breast changes with age; 3 periods:
  1. Early reproductive (15-25 years): development of lobules and ducts to complete units.
  2. Mature reproductive / cyclic change (25-45 years): the functioning period.
  3. Involution (35-55 years): lobular and duct epithelium start to shrink and fibrous tissue replaces it.
- ANDI: conditions that begin as normal development or an aberration and may later become disease under hormone influence. Between normal and disease there is a range of changes called a benign disorder or aberration. ANDI = an aberration that exceeds normal but is not disease.

### Dupont classification (pathology and cancer risk)
1. Nonproliferative lesion: no malignancy risk. Includes cyst, ductal ectasia, mild ductal epithelial hyperplasia, fibroadenoma.
2. Proliferative lesions without atypia:
   - 2.1 No malignancy risk: sclerosing adenosis, radial and complex sclerosing lesion, intraductal papilloma.
   - 2.2 Malignancy risk about 1.5-2 times: florid or moderate ductal hyperplasia.
3. Proliferative lesions with atypia: malignancy risk about 4-5 times: atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH) and atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH). ALH has a slightly higher chance of becoming cancer than ADH.

### Incidence of breast mass
- Overall, fibrocystic disease/change (FCC) is found in 34%; next is fibroadenoma 30%.
- By age: 9-12 years = breast bud; 15-30 years = fibroadenoma 50%; 30-50 years = FCC 45%; >50 years = think of cancer.

## Breast mass in children (p.208)
- Groups: (1) physiologic: breast bud, small lumps under the nipple at age 9-11; (2) inflammatory; (3) tumors: mostly benign, i.e. fibroadenoma.
- Initial assessment: US; if tissue diagnosis is needed use US with FNA. Avoid core biopsy in children because it may deform the breast.
- Do not do excisional biopsy (the developing breast is damaged; girls' breasts grow unevenly).

## Triple assessment (p.208-209)
- The gold standard evaluation of palpable breast lesions; do it in every patient with a palpable lump before planning treatment.
- Components: (1) clinical examination; (2) imaging, mainly mammogram and US; (3) pathological examination (CNB or FNA). Figure: clinical (age, examination), imaging (US, mammography), pathology (FNA cytology, core-cut biopsy) -> confident diagnosis in 95% of cases.
- Triple assessment is now accepted as almost equal to excisional biopsy in accuracy; advantage: avoids unnecessary excision in women <35 years.
- Clinical breast examination (CBE): an examining surgeon's judgment of cancer or not is correct about 70% of the time, mainly from history taking and examination.
- History: how long the mass has been present; fast or slow growth; whether it varies in size; painful or not; relation to menstruation; age at menarche, menopause, children and age at first child; history of breast disease, family history of breast cancer (esp. in a first-degree relative, bilateral, or 2 relatives); previous irradiation to the chest; hormone/OCP use.
- Examination timing: 7-10 days after menses ends, when hormone stimulation is lowest; if menopausal examine any time. Inspect and palpate, with nipple and nodes, sitting and lying, both sides, comparing; record the quadrant, distance from the nipple (cm), size, margin, mobility vs fixed, consistency (hard, rubbery, cystic), surface (smooth, nodular, lobular), tenderness.
- Cancer features: hard, irregular, fixed to adjacent tissue, skin dimpling or retraction, bloody nipple discharge or scaling.
- Mnemonic BREAST: Bumps, Retraction, Edema, Axilla, Skin change, Tender or thickening.
- Imaging: in women over 35 years, diagnostic mammogram with US considered; in women under 35 US is more useful than mammogram.
- Pathology: FNA = cytopathology; core needle biopsy = histopathology. Do not do FNA or core before mammogram, because hematoma or tissue inflammation can distort the film and cause misreading.
- Triple test sensitivity 99.6%, specificity 93%.
- Approach: (1) all 3 tests positive -> cancer probability 99.4-100%; (2) all 3 negative -> cancer probability <1%: observe and follow up (open biopsy if the surgeon remains uneasy); (3) never do mastectomy on cytology positive alone: all 3 must be positive; (4) if any test is negative do excisional biopsy next.

## Benign breast diseases without cancer risk (p.209-210)
1. fibroadenoma; 2. sclerosing adenosis; 3. intraductal papilloma; 4. Mondor's disease; 5. fibrocystic change (FCC); 6. gynecomastia; 7. duct ectasia, periductal mastitis; 8. squamous metaplasia.
- If clinically diagnosed as benign disease in this group, screening is as for normal-risk women. Beware: "papillomatosis" in the USA means ordinary or mild hyperplasia, which has no cancer risk.
- But moderate or florid hyperplasia, or papilloma with fibrovascular core, carries about 1.5 times cancer risk.

### Benign conditions hard to distinguish from cancer on examination
1. Sclerosing adenosis: an aberration at age 30-50, the benign disease most difficult to exclude from cancer.
2. fat necrosis; 3. radial scar; 4. fibromatosis; 5. granular cell tumor.

## Nipple discharge (p.210-211)
- Unilateral: usually a breast cause. Bilateral: usually physiologic (e.g. pregnancy, galactorrhea).
- Colour green, yellow, or brown suggests benign cyst most often; reassure.
- Bilateral milky discharge: think lactation in pregnancy or prolactinoma; check for pregnancy first; if not pregnant and milk flows >2 months, send serum prolactin.
- Unilateral bloody discharge: intraductal papilloma is a more common cause than breast cancer.
- Pathological discharge (suspicious): from one breast only, spontaneous (without squeezing), persistent (>2 times per week for consecutive weeks), from a single duct, bloody or multicolored from many pores/serosanguineous, associated with a mass, age >40. Figure: discharge suggestive of malignancy if spontaneous, unilateral, single duct, bloody, associated with mass, age >40.
- Causes of pathological discharge: benign intraductal papilloma 50%; duct ectasia 15% (dilatation of major duct from chronic inflammation with inflammatory cells and fibrosis around the duct); cancer 10%.
- Approach:
  1. Ask whether it fits pathological discharge.
  2. Examine: press lightly around the areola, from outside toward the nipple, to see which duct it comes from.
  3. Palpate; if a mass is found do triple assessment.
  4. If no mass, do breast imaging: age >35 US + mammogram; age <35 US alone unless cancer is strongly suspected (then US + mammogram).
  5. If imaging is normal, test the discharge for occult blood; fluid tumor markers are not yet useful.
- Discharge cytology: smear on a slide fixed at once in 95% alcohol; sensitivity 46%, specificity 99.5%.
- Ductoscopy: difficult, low sensitivity, not widely used (figure shows normal ducts, debris, papillary lesion, intratumoral). Ductal lavage: not useful because cells cannot be traced to a particular duct.
- Ductogram: inject 0.1-0.2 cc contrast through the duct opening, then mammogram without compressing the nipple; not routine; used when multiple duct abnormalities are suspected with no inflammation and persistent discharge; difficult and results not definitive. If the facility cannot do it, go straight to duct excision.
- Contraindications to ductogram: (1) mastitis or breast abscess; (2) bloody discharge in late pregnancy (usually from ductal hyperplasia or vascular engorgement; observe, it usually resolves).
- Summary: for pathological discharge, even clear discharge, if from one breast, a single duct, spontaneous and persistent, examine the breast and do mammogram; if no cause is found, ductogram then directed duct excision; some advise skipping ductogram and doing microdochectomy straight away (diagnosis and treatment together).
- Multiduct non-bloody green-black discharge: no need for surgery.

## Intraductal papilloma (p.212-213)
- Benign disease; the commonest cause of unilateral blood-stained nipple discharge.
- A single lesion = solitary; many lesions in many ducts = multiple papillomas / papillomatosis.
- Not a true papilloma: abnormal proliferation of ductal epithelium (epithelial hyperplasia) around a central stalk (fibrovascular core with fibrous tissue and blood vessels) that bleeds easily. Common in young women; associated with FCC.
- Classic patient: <40 years, bloody nipple discharge, mass usually not palpable (usually 2-3 mm; palpable in only 30%). If palpable, do FNA or core biopsy; if not, US or mammogram to look for suspicious features.
- Solitary intraductal papilloma has no cancer risk; multiple intraductal papillomas in young people, rarely with discharge, carry cancer risk.
- If bloody discharge and no palpable mass: ductography; the papilloma is usually in a subareolar major duct within 5 cm of the nipple. Or go straight to microdochectomy. Rule out cancer before ductogram.
- Treatment: microdochectomy (duct excision) = excise only the offending duct.
  1. Before surgery, tell the patient to press the breast for about 3 days; in OR press at the nipple and mark the position that produces discharge.
  2. Local anesthetic (or GA); incision around 1/3 of the nipple circumference.
  3. Theory: insert a small lacrimal probe into the duct and inject methylene blue, then excise just that duct.
  4. In practice the probe is hard to insert and methylene blue can stain everything; the author opens by a circumareolar incision, raises the flap, dissects under the nipple and looks for the duct. A duct with papilloma contains brown fluid or old blood, and the duct is dilated.
  5. Excise the duct from the nipple opening downward until the dilated duct beyond (about 1 cm); mark orientation for pathology (nipple end versus periphery).
- Central duct excision (nipple cut into a quadrant) loses normal ducts too; avoid it; the one best operation for intraductal papilloma is microdochectomy.

## Breast cyst (p.214-215)
- Change of terminal duct lobular unit; by ANDI an aberration.
- Patients over 40 up to menopause; often a rapidly appearing mass, may be multiple/bilateral, may recur; many have no pain.
- Management: FNA with a 21 G needle, aspirate all fluid; US may be needed.
- US simple cyst: thin-walled cyst without debris or septation.
- Look at the fluid colour first:
  1.1 red or bloody -> send cytology.
  1.2 black fluid -> occult blood or microscopy; if blood is found, send cytology.
  1.3 yellow, green, grey, brown and the mass disappears after aspiration: in a patient under 35 the cancer risk is very low; no cytology needed; discard the fluid, reassure and re-check in 6-8 weeks; if it does not recur resume normal screening.
- If aspiration gives blood, or a mass is still palpable, or the lump remains = complex cyst: needs tissue diagnosis (core needle biopsy), not just fluid cytology.
- Complex cyst = cyst with internal septation or mural nodule on US. Aspirate fully first, then US-guided CNB at the mural nodule or thick septum, and place a clip in the complex cyst to allow excisional biopsy later if needed.
- Indications for further diagnostic or surgical evaluation: (1) bloody fluid or occult blood positive; (2) recurrence after 2-3 aspirations; (3) palpable mass after aspiration -> core biopsy; (4) complex cyst; (5) inconclusive cytology.
- Figure (algorithm): cyst -> FNA -> non-blood-stained aspirate: no residual mass and no recurrence -> no routine follow-up; residual mass or recurrence (x3) -> surgical biopsy. Blood-stained aspirate -> FNAC / surgical biopsy.

## Fibrocystic disease (p.215-216)
- Now regarded as fibrocystic change (FCC) rather than disease; by ANDI FCC is a disorder of cyclic change. Believed to arise from cyst rupture: secretions enter adjacent stroma -> chronic inflammation and fibrosis -> breast becomes firm/nodular (figure: cyst rupture -> secretory material -> adjacent stroma -> chronic inflammation, fibrosis -> palpable firmness).
- Histology variants:
  1. simple cyst: ductal cells with wall 1-2 layers;
  2. wall 3-4 layers = mild hyperplasia;
  3. wall >4 layers with papillary projection = papillary hyperplasia;
  4. many acini or ductules around the cyst = adenosis;
  5. hyperplastic epithelium with cellular atypia = atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH): risk of becoming cancer.
- No cancer risk except as above. Pain alone without a mass is usually benign.
- 96% have no palpable mass; with cyst in 4% -> aspiration and approach the fluid as for breast cyst.
- Patients usually >30 years; if over 40 years, send mammogram in every case.
- Presentation: often pain; (1) cyclic mastalgia 50-90%; (2) palpable nodularity of the breast, which may be several sites or both sides.
- Other painful breast diseases: sclerosing adenosis, fibroadenoma, phyllodes; be careful: 7% of breast cancer starts with breast pain only.
- Breast pain has 3 types (figure: true breast pain, costochondral pain, lateral chest wall pain; musculoskeletal pain; cyclical vs non-cyclical mastalgia across the menstrual cycle):
  1. Cyclic pain: from hormone changes in the cycle; pain before menses, related to hormone stimulation; usually both breasts; eases after menses ends.
  2. Non-cyclic pain: no relation to menses; often one side, short-lived; about 50% resolve spontaneously; patients are usually older than those with cyclic pain.
  3. Extramammary pain: from muscle, bone, cartilage (costochondritis) or pressure on the breast.
- Tietze syndrome: pain at the lateral anterior chest wall near the sternum from costochondritis; in young to middle-aged women, cause unknown; treat with NSAIDs.
- Key principle: reassure the patient that breast pain is usually not cancer; need history, examination, mammogram, with US added if needed.

### Treatment of FCC / mastalgia (p.217)
1. If imaging is normal: reassurance and symptomatic treatment. Importantly, the aim is a single answer: tell the patient it is not cancer.
2. If no mass is palpable and imaging shows no cyst, no need for complete triple assessment or FNA.
3. If a mass is palpable and imaging shows a cyst, do FNA.
4. For painful cases, first-line drug: evening primrose oil 3 g/day, or vitamin E, for about 4 months (no adverse effects; reduces pain in 30-40%); can be combined with analgesics, NSAIDs. For severe pain: danazol 100-400 mg/day reduces pain up to 90%, but side effects: weight gain, acne, voice deepening, hirsutism, irregular/absent menses. Bromocriptine, tamoxifen, the pill are drugs that may work in some patients. In danazol non-responders, Cameron states the drug of choice is tamoxifen.
5. Reduce methylxanthines (coffee, tea, chocolate, cola).
6. Low-fat diet, low-tyramine diet (wine, beer, cheese, nuts, bananas) and stop drugs that cause breast pain, e.g. cimetidine, beta-blockers, theophylline.
7. Well-fitting brassiere support.
8. Regular, non-strenuous exercise.
9. Subcutaneous mastectomy only as the last option.
10. Drugs found not to help: diuretic, progesterone, vitamins A, B2, B6, D.
- Figure (management guideline for mastalgia): Breast pain -> Focal: clinical exam + diagnostic imaging (mammography, targeted sono): normal -> mild/moderate: reassure; severe: analgesics; abnormal -> intervention or biopsy. Diffuse and bilateral: noncyclic -> lab tests (prolactin, beta-hCG): hyperprolactinemia -> brain MRI -> bromocriptine; pregnancy -> reassure; normal -> mild/moderate reassure, severe analgesics. Cyclic -> mild/moderate reassure; severe analgesics and/or danazol or tamoxifen.

## Sclerosing adenosis (p.218)
- A form of FCC: a rather hard, nodular mass from fibrosis with ill-defined margin, fairly fixed, so hard to distinguish from cancer.
- Diagnosis by CNB.
- Treatment: if pathology shows no cellular atypia, reassure and follow up (no risk of becoming cancer, so no excision) unless the patient is truly anxious.

## Fibroadenoma (p.218-220)
- Benign tumor arising from the lobular system; common at age 15-30, rare over 35.
- By ANDI: a disorder of development. Size usually <2 cm; >5 cm = giant fibroadenoma (4%); by ANDI, giant is a disease.
- More than 5 lumps in the same breast is also considered a disease regardless of size.
- The most common tumor in adolescence/early reproductive life. No cancer risk. Grows slowly, is estrogen-sensitive: may enlarge with OCP use or in pregnancy.
- Typical: oval, firm, smooth or lobular surface, well-encapsulated, freely mobile with rubbery consistency ("breast mouse"); multiple/bilateral in 15%; about 30% regress; some have pain, usually just before menses, and feel it enlarge.
- Theoretically do triple assessment:
  1. CBE: round, firm, lobulated or smooth, mobile mass. In the involuting phase fibrosis can make it hard to separate from cancer.
  2. Imaging: age <35 -> US; age >35 -> mammogram + US.
  3. FNA or core biopsy (Schwartz and most prefer core cut): fibroadenoma has no mitosis, unlike phyllodes.
- If examination is typical and US shows classic findings (hypoechoic mass, well-defined margin, homogeneous; maybe coarse calcification inside) in a patient <=25 years with a classic mass, cancer chance is very low: tend to observe with follow-up every 6 months without CNB; this is an option Schwartz 11th ed accepts. For patients who follow guidelines strictly, do CNB in all (play safe).
- Beware rapid growth, especially size >3 cm: may be phyllodes; needs CNB.
- Figure: fibroadenoma is a well-defined ovoid mass on US and mammogram; definitive diagnosis by US-guided needle biopsy.
- Indications for surgery (excision): (1) age >35; (2) rapid growth >20% or >0.5 cm in 6 months; (3) size >2 cm (Cameron uses 3 cm); (4) symptoms such as pain or breast distortion; (5) CNB shows increased cellularity; (6) very anxious patient fearing cancer.
- Multiple lesions: 2 ways: (1) excisional biopsy of a single lesion and follow the rest; (2) core biopsy of every lesion; any suspected phyllodes -> excision (see phyllodes).
- Cryoablation (ablative treatment) is approved as an alternative, safe and effective treatment for fibroadenoma <= 3 cm: lump shrinks or disappears; a lump >3 cm should be excised. (For cancer, cold or RFA heat are being tried in the NSABP B-21 trial.)
- Steps of cryoablation (figure): cryoablation needle placed into the center of the fibroadenoma under US guidance; freezing creates an "iceball" on US; the tumor dies and shrinks over time.

## Giant fibroadenoma (p.220-221)
- Benign lesion in women aged 15-20; rapid growth in 3-6 weeks; usually >5 cm or >500 g, or a mass occupying more than 4/5 of the breast.
- Key: hard to distinguish from phyllodes; breast enlarges, heavy, painful, tense.
- Differential diagnosis: juvenile hypertrophy (usually both sides); hematoma (history of trauma); cyst (age usually >30).
- Treatment: (1) local excision through an inframammary incision; (2) observation seems unsuitable because of very fast growth with possible tumor infarction or skin necrosis; phyllodes must also be excluded.

## Phyllodes tumor (p.221)
- Fibroepithelial tumor; in women about 35 (35-55) years; arises from mesenchymal tissue; mass >3 cm, fast-growing, may be painful.
- Examination: round, firm, well-defined border, mobile; overlying skin often bluish/dark with dilated veins at the apex; usually NO skin invasion or nipple retraction unless the mass pushes the overlying skin until it ulcerates.
- Histology: mixed epithelial and stromal proliferation like fibroadenoma; to differentiate need mitosis and stromal invasion; classic = leaf-like architecture (epithelium covering stroma in papillary projections into the stroma).
- NCCN 2019: US, and if age >=30 add mammogram: US shows heterogeneous echo, internal round cystic spaces (fluid-filled), and clefts. Imaging cannot reliably separate it from fibroadenoma; MRI shows it more clearly but is only an option.
- FNA is often not diagnostic (pathologist reads non-diagnostic: cannot assess stromal cell number), so core biopsy is better. CNB positive predictive value 83%.
- About 10% are malignant phyllodes, but nodal spread is very rare.
- Malignant criteria: (1) mitotic count >4/10 HPF; (2) infiltrative foci at margin; (3) evidence of hematogenous metastasis.
- WHO classification of phyllodes (by pathology):
  1. Benign: mitotic count not more than 3/10 HPF, well-defined border, no stromal overgrowth or atypia, low Ki-67; recurrence post-treatment 8%; no metastasis.
  2. Low grade / borderline malignant: mitosis 4-9/10 HPF, mild-moderate stromal overgrowth or atypia, focal small stromal invasion, margin still fairly clear, Ki-67 low; recurrence 29%; metastasis up to 5%.
  3. High grade malignant: mitosis 10 or more /10 HPF, marked stromal overgrowth or invasion, unclear border, Ki-67 high; recurrence 36%; metastasis 25%.
- Even though metastasis happens, death from metastasis is rare.

| Feature | Fibroadenoma | Phyllodes |
|---|---|---|
| Age | young adult | middle age (>35 y) |
| Size | 1-2 cm | 4-5 cm |
| Growth | slow | rapid |
| Remission | 30% | no |
| Multiple | 15% | single |
| Bilateral | 15% | unilateral |
| Cancer risk | - | 10% |
| Recurrence after treatment | rare | more common |

- Phyllodes is ER positive in 58% and PgR positive in 75%, but endocrine therapy has no role.
- Treatment:
  1. Whatever the grade, treatment of choice = wide excision with margin at least 1 cm. Enucleation alone is not acceptable (high local recurrence). No axillary staging or ALND needed (nodal spread rare).
  2. Indications for simple mastectomy: 2.1 large tumor-to-breast ratio / mass occupying most of the breast; 2.2 skin ulceration; 2.3 WLE cannot achieve negative margin; 2.4 in the past, a mass >4 cm -> simple mastectomy; now wide excision is still advised first, but for a giant phyllodes (>7 cm, some say 10 cm) simple mastectomy is the likely choice (tumor-breast ratio problem). After mastectomy no further treatment is needed if margin negative; if "close margin", consider post-op RT to chest wall.
  3. Chest wall invasion: en bloc resection and cover with flap.
  4. If diagnosed as fibroadenoma before surgery but pathology reports phyllodes: re-resection removing the scar with margin >=1 cm (debate for benign phyllodes: some advise observation, but the author prefers re-WLE).
  5. Distant metastasis: treat like soft tissue sarcoma; solitary metastasis can be resected; post-op RT for tumors >5 cm or involving chest wall; chemotherapy is not very effective.
  6. Recurrent phyllodes: re-excision with wide margins, no axillary staging, together with RT.

## Gynecomastia (p.223-224)
- Enlarged breast in a male; patient has a mass under the nipple. Graded in 3 grades by size.
- 2 main groups:
  1. Physiologic: 1.1 neonatal (first 2-3 weeks after birth; from maternal estrogen via placenta; usually regresses); 1.2 adolescence (boys in puberty, testis works more; besides testosterone, estrogen also rises); 1.3 senescence (older men: excess fat -> peripheral conversion of testosterone to estrogen).
  2. Pathologic: gonadal tumor, HCC, HIV, cirrhosis, chronic renal failure, and drug induced.
- Drug-induced: dozens of drugs; common: estrogens and hormones (e.g. pills), metronidazole (Flagyl), antifungals, cimetidine (Tagamet), spironolactone-type (Plasil is metoclopramide), digitalis, antihypertensives, drugs of abuse.
- Adolescent gynecomastia: observe until age 18; excision under 18 is not suitable.
- Treatment: 1. reassurance; 2. correct underlying etiology; 3. if surgery needed: subcutaneous mastectomy; 4. idiopathic: tamoxifen 40 mg OD for 1-4 months gives pain relief within 2 weeks and complete response in 70%.

## Galactocele (p.224)
- Firm mass in pregnancy or lactation, with or without pain; a milk-filled cyst near the areola.
- No surgical excision needed; if very painful, aspiration occasionally.

## Fat necrosis (p.224-225)
- Usually after blunt chest injury; a firm mass develops, lesion usually superficial with ill-defined border; skin dimpling in 40%.
- Clinical exam and mammogram are hard to separate from cancer, so biopsy to exclude cancer.
- Treatment: analgesic, proper breast support.

## Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia (p.225)
- Non-neoplastic stromal lesion, often a mass like fibroadenoma; mammogram shows cyst-like spaces.
- Histology resembles a vascular lesion and may be mistaken for angiosarcoma. Cause unknown.
- If suspected, excise with free margin because recurrence after surgery is very rare; afterward only observation.

## Summary: benign breast disease points to remember (p.225-226)
1. Acute mastitis: must (always) drain (the book's saying: forbid baby and father from sucking is wrong); [the book's point: breast abscess must be drained; mastitis gets antibiotic].
2. Mondor's disease: answer "usually requires surgery" is certainly wrong; most resolve spontaneously.
3. FCC: treatment with vitamin E is fine; but vitamin A, D, B2, B6, progesterone and diuretics are useless.
4. Fibroadenoma: using mammogram to help diagnosis is usually wrong, because it occurs in young women with dense fat-rich breasts; excision of all is also wrong: if the mass is <2 cm and the patient is not afraid of cancer, observe.
5. Phyllodes: wrong answers include: multiple masses; death from metastasis; LN involvement; FNA to confirm (should be core needle biopsy); hormonal treatment; ALND, RT, chemotherapy. Right: simple mastectomy (wide local excision with free margin 1 cm is also OK).

# BREAST CANCER (book p.226-)
- Breast cancer is the most common cancer and very heterogeneous in biology; understanding has gone down to gene and molecular level. Gene expression profiles divide breast cancer into subtypes (luminal vs basal), which matter for choice of treatment and prognosis.
- Understanding of breast cancer stem cells helps explain resistance to chemo and radiation.
- First know the basics related to breast cancer.

## Lymphatic drainage (p.226)
- Direction: superficial subepithelial plexus -> deep subcutaneous plexus. More than 97% of lymph drains to the axillary nodes first; the first node that receives drainage is the sentinel lymph node, the first group of lymph nodes; then onward drain to further nodes along the subclavian vein.
- A minority (3%) of lymph drains to the internal mammary nodes (deep nodes beneath the sternum).
- Any quadrant can drain to the axilla and to the internal mammary nodes, so internal mammary nodes do not necessarily receive only from the medial half and subareolar area.
- Clinically palpable nodes are truly cancer in only 33% on examination, so confirm with CNB whether a node is metastatic.

### Axillary lymph nodes (ALN) (p.227-228)
- Average about 35 nodes (up to 50 in some cadaver studies).
- Haagensen's classification divides axillary nodes into 6 groups (figure: lateral/axillary, apical/subclavicular, central, interpectoral (Rotter's), posterior/subscapular, anterior/external mammary):
  1. anterior group: along the edge of pectoralis minor (anterior/external mammary);
  2. lateral group: along the axillary vein;
  3. posterior group: along the edge of subscapularis muscle;
  4. central group: in the axillary fat, under pectoralis minor;
  5. apical group: at the top and inner side, above the upper edge of pectoralis minor; the deepest group;
  6. Rotter's (interpectoral) nodes: lie between pectoralis major and minor; drain directly into ALN level 3.
- Surgeons now use 3 levels, by relation to pectoralis minor, to indicate the extent of axillary dissection. Normally dissect level 1 and 2; if level 2 nodes are abnormal at surgery, remove level 3 too.
  - Level 1: nodes lateral to or at the lower border of pectoralis minor; axillary vein group, external mammary group, scapular group (= groups 1, 2, 3).
  - Level 2: nodes behind (under) pectoralis minor: central group and some subclavicular group (Schwartz adds Rotter nodes here) (= groups 4, 6).
  - Level 3: nodes medial to or above pectoralis minor: subclavicular group only (= group 5).
- Skip metastasis: 3% of axillary metastasis go to level 2 and 3 without passing level 1; 3% go to level 3 directly, skipping levels 1 and 2. Hence SLN biopsy has about 3% chance of false negative.
- Rotter node: between pectoralis major and minor, drains directly to ALN level 3.

## Breast cancer and genetics (p.228)
- Sporadic breast cancer: no family history of breast cancer in 2 generations; 65-75%.
- Familial breast cancer: one or more relatives (1st or 2nd degree) with breast cancer; 20-30%.
- Hereditary breast cancer: autosomal dominant inheritance (50% chance of passing to children), early onset, bilateral, associated with ovarian and colon cancer; 5-10%.
- Key genes: BRCA1, BRCA2 (tumor suppressor genes) and p53 (others exist).
- BRCA1 mutation: found in younger patients; uncontrolled cell division leads to hereditary breast cancer syndrome; cancers are often triple-negative, so prognosis is worse than other groups.

## Risk factors (p.229-230)
1. Female sex, age >40.
2. Previous cancer in the other breast.
3. Family history of breast cancer in first-degree relatives (blood relatives: sibling, parent, child), not in-laws.
4. Early menarche (<12 y), late menopause (>55 y), nulliparity (often because of no husband) or late first child (>35 y).
5. Oral contraceptive pills (slight increase) or hormone replacement therapy after menopause for more than 5 years.
6. History of chest wall RT.
7. BRCA1/BRCA2 mutation.
8. Familial genetic disease, e.g. Li-Fraumeni syndrome.
9. History of DCIS or proliferative lesion with atypia (ADH, ALH).
10. Others: alcohol, smoking, obesity (not strong).
- Factors that do NOT increase risk: e.g. tubal ligation, or breast prosthesis/augmentation.
- 5-year risk models: Gail model (calculated from age, menarche, parity and other parameters) and the Claus model (adds family history in more detail).
- Gail model: assesses an individual woman's chance of developing invasive breast cancer, using: (1) age; (2) age at menarche/menopause; (3) age at first live birth; (4) number of first-degree relatives with breast cancer (not second-degree); (5) number of prior breast biopsies; (6) atypical ductal hyperplasia found.
- Gail model gives a 5-year risk; a 5-year risk of 1.67% or more is the cutoff; sensitivity 28-49%, specificity 66-88%.
- Gail model is popular in the USA but not well suited for Asians or familial breast cancer; the Claus model suits patients with genetic involvement such as BRCA1, BRCA2 or familial breast cancer.
- The author's view: these models are not commonly used by Thai surgeons (even Schwartz p.556 mentions them).
- Apparently age is the most important risk factor.

## Screening for invasive breast cancer in the asymptomatic woman (p.231)
- Many guidelines (ACS, USPSTF, NCCN, ACR, ACOG, AMA); the author follows NCCN.
- Average-risk women (NCCN-style as presented):
  1. Age 20 and up: breast self-examination (SBE) monthly (and taught the proper technique); CBE from age 25, every 1-3 years until age 40; some advise a baseline mammogram once at 35; most start mammogram at 40.
  2. Age 40-75: SBE monthly; CBE every 1 year; mammogram every 1 year; option to add US yearly useful for dense breasts.
  3. Age 75 and up: screening is individualized by health status (e.g. bedridden/chronic ill: do not bother).

### Ductal hyperplasia (p.233)
1. Mild: cell layers above the basement membrane about 3-4 layers.
2. Moderate: cell layers >=5.
3. Florid: epithelial hyperplasia involving minor duct lumen >70%.
- ADH and ALH: lifetime breast cancer risk 4 times; cumulative incidence of breast cancer about 35% at 30 years; the risk correlates with the number of foci of atypia (more multifocal atypia = more risk). Chemoprevention lowers the risk of breast cancer by 50%; in a subgroup with atypia, tamoxifen reduced risk by up to 86%. Breast MRI and BCS have no role in this disease.
- Figure: progression normal -> hyperplasia -> atypical hyperplasia -> DCIS -> invasive cancer (breast cancer development; and cells from Pap smear/ductal lavage).

## Breast examination (p.233-234)
- Inspect and palpate; no need to percuss or auscultate.
- Patient undresses (the upper body) down to the waist, sitting, arms hanging down.
- Look at both breasts compared: size, skin (dimples, ulcers, orange peel), nipple (retraction, inversion); then ask the patient to raise the arms to show the lower breast, which may be hidden.
- Then lie the patient down and palpate the whole breast with a gentle hand; palpate axillary nodes and supraclavicular nodes (many prefer palpating nodes in the sitting position).
- Figure: steps A-G of the examination (arms at sides, raised, hands on hips, pressed together, palpation with flat fingers, lying position with a pillow, spiral/clock pattern).

## Mammography (p.234-236)
- One mammogram uses about 4 times the radiation of a chest x-ray; it does not significantly add breast cancer risk. Two purposes:
  1. Screening mammogram: the best screening tool, shows masses and microcalcifications. Start at age 40 (ACS guideline 2015: start at 45, every year until 55, then every 2 years); some advise a baseline at age 35. Screening finds cancer earlier, allows more BCS and lowers mortality. ACS and USPSTF screening guidelines do not give weight to breast examination.
  2. Diagnostic mammogram: for symptomatic patients (pain, mass, discharge).
- Indications: (1) suspected mass, pain or nipple discharge with nothing found; (2) follow-up after BCS or MRM; (3) screening reduces mortality by about 33% in women >50 years; (4) palpable mass wanting to know extent and invasion, especially before BCS, to see multicentricity or multifocality; (5) rule out synchronous contralateral cancer.
- Reporting uses BI-RADS. Mammogram cannot replace biopsy: tissue diagnosis is always required for cancer.
- Normal mammogram figure labels: normal lymph nodes, concave pectoralis, retroglandular fat, scalloped edge of glandular tissue, nipple, open inframammary fold; corpus mammae, crests of Duret, Chassaignac's bursa, nipple-areolar complex.
- Features suspicious for cancer: (1) irregular margin and asymmetry with spiculation; (2) stellate opacity; (3) cluster of microcalcification, fine pleomorphic or fine linear branching; (4) parenchymal distortion.
  - Note: cluster calcification should be at least 5 grains in 1 cm2 and localized to one area; scattered clusters throughout the whole breast suggest FCC.
- Position for viewing: craniocaudal (CC) and mediolateral oblique (MLO).
- If unclear, or to see microcalcification or margins better: spot compression with magnification.
- Limitations: very dense breast, breasts that had silicone injections; and difficulty separating a mass from a cyst (similar density), where US helps. Figure: in a fatty breast cancer is easy to detect; in a dense breast it is harder because dense tissue and cancer both look white.
- On MLO view axillary nodes are seen: normal nodes are bean-shaped with a lucent fatty center, <=1 cm; a round node with dense white center is consistent with a metastatic node.
- Reading a mammogram, step by step:
  1. Identification: check the film belongs to this patient.
  2. Which breast (or both), with both CC and MLO views.
  3. Adequacy: the whole breast seen in CC with retroglandular fat visible; in MLO the pectoralis muscle must be seen and the nipple in profile (a line from the nipple to the pectoralis muscle).
  4. Look from outside inward for skin thickening, skin or nipple retraction, architectural distortion.
  5. Composition (density): almost entirely fat; scattered fibroglandular; heterogeneously dense; extremely dense.
  6. Mass: seen in both views; report location, size, shape (round, oval), and margin (well circumscribed, lobulated, obscured, spiculated).
  7. Calcification:
     - 7.1 Benign calcification: usually in skin, blood vessels, or coarse "popcorn" calcification, larger than 2-3 mm.
     - 7.2 Suspicious for malignancy: small microcalcifications whose shape cannot be clearly defined (amorphous), fine linear or fine linear branching (thin lines but irregular), fine pleomorphic (varied size and shape). Also report distribution: diffuse/scattered (low chance of cancer); regional (spread over an area >2 cm2); group or cluster = 5 or more grains per 1 cm2; segmental = along a duct system.
  8. Axillary lymph node (see above).
  - Figure (shape and margin): shape = circular, oval, lobular, irregular; margin = circumscribed, obscured, microlobulated, ill-defined, spiculated. Figure of calcification morphology: amorphous (benign), amorphous (DCIS), fine pleomorphic, coarse heterogeneous, fine linear, fine linear branching.

## BI-RADS (Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System) (p.237-240)
- Reporting of mammogram abnormality from low to high; used lately: ACR 4th ed to 2019 (ACR 5th ed released last October with changes): (1) follow-up and outcome monitoring of US, mammogram and MRI; (2) updated details of breast composition; (3) details of breast assessment from US; (4) description of breast implants.
- Luckily the assessment categories are unchanged: BIRADS 0 to 6.
- BIRADS 0: incomplete study; mammogram evaluation not complete, cancer cannot be assessed. Recommendation: need additional imaging evaluation: repeat or additional views, compare with previous films, magnification view, spot compression or add US.
- BIRADS 1: normal study, no pathology; both breasts same size, symmetrical, good glandular tissue, no skin thickening, no architectural distortion, no mass, no calcification. Cancer risk 0%. Routine screening for age.
- BIRADS 2: benign finding (like category 1 but there is a definitely benign lesion), e.g. popcorn calcification, simple cyst seen on US as black (anechoic), or a fatty mass consistent with lipoma or hamartoma. Cancer risk 0%. Recommendation: routine yearly screening.
- BIRADS 3: probably benign ("probably" = not certain), cancer risk <2% (table: >0% but <=2%). Recommendation: initial short-term follow-up with a new mammogram every 3-6 months for 2 years (the affected side; if the other side has problems, repeat that side too, to avoid radiation exposure). If the lesion does not change in 2 years it can be downgraded to category 2; if still suspicious or uncertain, biopsy may be needed.
  - Probably benign lesions: (1) nonpalpable solid mass without suspicious finding; (2) intermediate microcalcifications (amorphous, single group of punctate microcalcification, coarse heterogeneous); (3) focal asymmetric density; (4) simple dilated duct without pathology, nipple discharge.
- BIRADS 4: suspicious for cancer: more than category 3 but not as much as 5; chance of cancer 3-94%. Subdivided (figure shows US 4A, 4B, 4C):
  - 4a low suspicion: looks like complicated cyst: on US hypoechoic cyst with echoes/tissue debris floating in it, more likely cyst or abscess; or complex cyst with a nodule in the cyst; more benign than cancer; cancer risk 3-10% (table: >2% to <=10%).
  - 4b moderate, half-half between benign and cancer: group of amorphous or fine pleomorphic microcalcification; risk 11-50% (table: >10% to <=50%).
  - 4c high suspicion: irregular solid mass with ill-defined border, or new group of fine linear microcalcification; more likely cancer than benign; risk 51-94% (table: >50% to <=95%).
  - Recommendation: biopsy.
- BIRADS 5: highly suggestive of cancer, probability >95% (table <=95%): must biopsy; never do mastectomy right away without waiting for pathology. Findings: (1) spiculated mass; (2) irregular high density mass; (3) new fine linear branching microcalcification.
- BIRADS 6: known biopsy-proven malignancy; the mammogram is done to evaluate the mass for multifocal or multicentric disease so that treatment can be planned. Recommendation: treatment as appropriate.
- Breast composition (density) is further divided into 4 grades by amount of dense tissue (figure: A fatty; B scattered fibroglandular density; C heterogeneously dense; D extremely dense). C and D are "dense breasts". (Breasts with low glandular tissue/more fat = grade 1 ... more than 75% glandular with less than 25% fat = grade 4.)
- Simple summary: BIRADS <=2: follow up every 12 months; BIRADS 3: if low suspicion of cancer, appointment every 3-6 months for 2 years, but if high suspicion do CNB; BIRADS 4 and 5: do CNB right away.

### Digital breast tomosynthesis (p.240-241)
- Digital mammogram with tomosynthesis = 3-D mammogram, now replacing the older 2-D mammogram: the x-ray tube rotates around the breast and gives tomo slices (figure: x-ray tube on a rotation arc, compression boards, flat-panel detector). Advantage: reduces overlapping tissue that obscures the breast, giving more accurate interpretation (a cancer easily missed on 2D was clearly seen on 3D).

## Ultrasonography (p.241-)
- Good imaging for breast disease in women <35 years; separates cyst from solid mass; look at the vascular pattern feeding the mass. Nowadays often done together with mammogram. (Figure: a mass seen on mammogram is only a cyst on US.)
- Not suitable as a screening tool because: (1) microcalcification is not clearly seen; (2) sensitivity and specificity depend on the reader and the quality of the machine.
- US criteria for a malignant lesion: (1) taller than wide; (2) hypoechoic texture; (3) irregular, spiculated or "sunray" appearance; (4) posterior acoustic shadowing.
- US criteria for a benign lesion (e.g. fibroadenoma): (1) wider than tall (ellipsoid); (2) hyper- or isoechoic texture; (3) smooth and well circumscribed with thin echogenic capsule; (4) absence of any malignant finding.
- Besides separating benign from malignant, US also assesses axillary node features more accurately than mammogram. Suspicious node on US: (1) circular (round) shape; (2) bulging cortex, cortex thickened >2 cm [as printed]; (3) echo texture at the center of the node without the black/white fat hilum (looks all uniform, from cancer spread to node); (4) cortical capsular feeding vessels.

## Breast MRI (p.242-244)
- Indications for breast MRI are increasing. As the sole screening tool it is still not accepted (unlike mammogram); use it in high-risk women, and especially young women whose mammogram is hard to read because the tissue is dense, e.g. hereditary breast cancer with BRCA1 or BRCA2 positive.
- American Cancer Society guideline 2007: annual MRI screening for cases with lifetime risk of breast cancer >=20% calculated using the Claus, BRCAPRO or Tyrer-Cuzick models.
- Figures: before and after gadolinium, enhancement of tumor and neovasculature; MRI of advanced cancer in left breast (mastectomy: >6 cm infiltrating ductal with 10+ nodes).
- Indications:
  1. Screening: use breast MRI together with mammogram (adds to, does not replace, mammogram) in very high-risk females: 1.1 BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation; 1.2 first-degree relative of a BRCA carrier but untested; 1.3 HBCS or HBOCS (hereditary breast (and ovarian) cancer syndrome); 1.4 lifetime risk of breast cancer >20%; 1.5 previous chest wall RT at age 10-30; 1.6 Li-Fraumeni syndrome, Cowden syndrome.
  2. Assess T staging / local extent in cases with a problematic mammogram + US: MRI shows size, number, margin, location, multifocality, multicentricity and chest wall invasion well.
  3. Work-up of axillary node metastasis with unknown primary (primary may be occult breast cancer): US + mammogram have lower sensitivity than breast MRI.
  4. Paget's disease of the nipple when mammogram + US find no breast tissue abnormality.
  5. Pre-op for BCS, because MRI sees multicentricity well.
  6. Detects DCIS better than mammogram.
  7. Evaluation of contralateral occult disease in lobular cancer.
  8. Separates cancer from fibrosis.
  9. Assess recurrent disease after surgery or RT (chest wall tissue has much fibrosis, hard to read on mammogram; MRI works better).
  10. Assess residual disease after surgery (mammogram compression would hurt the fresh wound).
  11. Assess response to chemo or neoadjuvant chemo to determine candidacy for BCS.
  12. Young women with very dense breast tissue, hard to read on mammogram: MRI is clearer.
  13. Rupture of silicone implant.
- Disadvantages of breast MRI: (1) cannot detect microcalcification well; (2) high false positives -> unnecessary CNB; (3) not widely available; (4) expensive.

## Positron emission mammogram (PEM) (p.244)
- PET scan used to image the breast like a mammogram; seems not yet workable (as the author puts it).

## Breast biopsy techniques (p.244-246)
- Palpable mass: if possible (not mandatory) consider imaging before biopsy: age >35: mammogram and US; age <35: US alone.
- 4 methods:
  1. FNAC: specificity >90% but sensitivity only 65%. Use a 23-25 G needle on a 10 cc syringe, pass through the mass back and forth about 15-20 times under continuous suction; smear the material on a slide and fix at once in 95% alcohol (if spray-fixing with 95% alcohol, spray from at least 1 foot away). Figure: FNAC technique steps (in, suction, back and forth, end suction, out).
     - Advantage: easy, convenient, good for suspected cyst, especially complex or complicated cyst.
     - Disadvantage: cytologist must be skilled; if FNA reads cancer it cannot separate invasive from non-invasive cancer (no basement membrane seen) and cannot test ER and PgR.
  2. Core needle biopsy (CNB): sensitivity 98%, specificity 100%. Use a thick needle, at least 14 G; may be a Tru-cut needle by hand or biopsy gun (spring-fired) or mammotome (vacuum-assisted needle); US can guide direction and site during the biopsy; take about 3-6 cores. Lay the cores on small paper to keep them straight, or wrap, and place in 10% neutral buffered formalin; prepare the specimen label.
     - CNB gives more tissue than FNA; now recommended: core biopsy > FNA.
     - Principle of Tru-cut (figure): needle with slot for specimen inside a sheath -> push needle into lesion -> slide sheath over the needle to cut the specimen -> withdraw needle, sheath and specimen.
     - Advantages: separates invasive from non-invasive cancer; can test ER, PgR and HER2. Put cores in 10% neutral buffered formalin; if CNB is from a lesion with microcalcification, x-ray the specimen to confirm calcium before formalin.
  3. Excisional biopsy: for a patient with a mass that is not large and not suspected of cancer; gives tissue diagnosis and treatment together. Indications: (1) triple assessment still cannot answer cancer or not, or FNA and core results disagree with imaging; (2) BIRADS 4 or 5; (3) BIRADS 3 mass that grows during follow-up.
  4. Incisional biopsy: not popular now because core biopsy can replace it; still used for ulcerating (fungating) masses.
- Notes:
  1. FNAC positive alone is not enough to guide a mastectomy (do not do mastectomy on it).
  2. The needle site or incision should lie along the line that can be removed together with the mass (in later surgery).
  3. If oncoplastic surgery is planned, choose core needle biopsy rather than excisional biopsy, because less breast skin is lost.
  4. A specimen from excisional biopsy needs all 4 margins marked (orientation for the pathologist: superior, inferior, medial, lateral).
- Anesthesia: local or GA. Theory: GA for masses >2 cm and deep ones. In practice the author uses GA only if the patient is not cooperative, or is scared; most Thai women are shy or anxious; local anesthesia is enough in >95% of cases.

## Non-palpable lesion (p.247-248)
1. Needle-localized biopsy (NLB): done with x-ray guidance; a needle containing a hook wire (the tip curved like a hook) is inserted to the lesion, the needle is withdrawn leaving the wire, then the surgeon cuts around the wire. Mammogram in mediolateral and CC views after placing the wire confirms the tip is at the microcalcification (watch for wire dislodgement/migration). Figure: suspect tissue, needle, wire inserted through needle, needle pulled out, ready for surgery.
   - After excision the specimen is sent to the mammography room again to confirm that the right area was removed, including the whole lesion, with the hook wire inside the tissue; mark specimen margins superior, inferior, medial, lateral; place in formalin and send to pathologist. Nowadays specimen x-ray is done in the OR itself, using US-guided CNB to take out microcalcification tissue and checking on film whether calcium is in the tissue or not; then a clip is left in the mass so that if it is cancer, BCS planning knows where the mass is.
2. US-guided excisional biopsy: instead of x-ray guidance, US shows the mass location (distance from skin). Surgeon operates as the radiologist indicates; or surgeon holds the US himself and marks the site on skin, or makes an incision and does intra-operative US (IOUS) in the OR. Place the probe about 1 cm away; view vertical and horizontal; after the mass is excised, check with US whether any mass remains. The lesion to be guided by US should be a mass (US cannot see microcalcification easily).
3. Stereotactic-guided biopsy: patient lies prone, the breast hangs through a table opening; suited to lesions with microcalcification. Problem in obese patients or those who cannot lie prone (severe kyphosis); some machines allow sitting. Limitations: very large breasts, lesion near the nipple, under the skin or deep against the chest wall are not suitable. Figure: stereotactic core needle biopsy; vacuum-assisted steps: position, vacuum, cut, remove. Specimen x-ray shows calcification within cores.
- For needle localization followed by surgical biopsy: after excision, x-ray the specimen to check that microcalcification is entirely removed and margins free, with the hook wire inside; if not all removed, cut more.

## Management of breast mass based on CNB (p.249)
1. Non-diagnostic or inadequate sample: repeat CNB.
2. Benign: mass <2 cm -> observe, follow up every 6 months for 2 years; mass >2 cm -> excise.
3. Atypia: surgical biopsy.
4. Suspicious: repeat core or excisional biopsy.
5. Malignant: treat as cancer (if all 3 tests positive).

## Indications for surgical biopsy after core needle biopsy (p.249-250)
- Some benign diseases can be diagnosed by core biopsy but still need excision because invasive cancer may hide in the mass and core missed the malignant cells. These lesions:
  - Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH): DCIS found in 12.5-36% and invasive cancer in 11-33% on excision; histology can hardly separate ADH from DCIS (and LCIS); excise the entire mass for pathology.
  - Atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH) (or lobular neoplasia (LIN), or sometimes called LCIS): whatever the pathologist calls it, do excisional biopsy.
  - Papillary lesions or papilloma (e.g. intraductal papilloma): no added cancer risk in itself, but ADH or DCIS may coexist, and atypia or invasion is hard to judge on core; papilloma with atypia has the highest risk of concurrent malignancy, >20%.
  - Mucin-producing lesions (mucocele-like lesions): dilated ducts and acini forming sacs filled with mucin; can coexist with ADH, DCIS and IDC.
  - Radial scar and complex sclerosing lesions: radial scar is often a small mass; on histology cells grow in the breast with a central fibroelastic core and ductules/lobules radiating like a star; if >6 mm, excision is recommended. Complex sclerosing lesion is larger than a radial scar; core cannot tell atypia and cannot separate from IDC.
  - Flat epithelial atypia (FEA) (or ductal intraepithelial neoplasia): epithelial cell change lining the inner duct surface; FEA may coexist with DCIS.
  - Fat necrosis; phyllodes tumor (excise the whole mass with ~1 cm margin since it grows and has malignant potential); pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia; vascular proliferation that is not simple hemangioma.
- Also excise when: FNA or core reports probably benign but not convincing; triple assessment doubtful or discordant (benign and imaging discordant); the patient is bored/worried and wants the mass out; cyst where aspiration gives blood or recurs after 2-3 aspirations; lack of concordance between mammographic appearance and histologic diagnosis.
- Observation: if the pathologist reads atypia, whatever the nature, do excisional biopsy.

## Excisional biopsy (p.250)
- Used when CNB finds the lesions above, or CNB is non-diagnostic or discordant with imaging.
- Types: (1) palpable excisional biopsy; (2) non-palpable excisional biopsy via needle or hook wire with US guidance.
- Remove the specimen as a single piece, not piecemeal.

## Lobular neoplasia (LIN) (p.251)
- Comprises atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH) and lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS); the difference is that LCIS has greater cell density and a larger extent of lesion. Figure: normal lobule vs LCIS (abnormal cells filling the lobule).
- LIN originates from abnormality in terminal duct lobular units. LIN is not yet considered cancer but a marker of cancer: it raises the risk of both invasive lobular and invasive ductal cancer in the future 8-10 times (considered small), and most patients are premenopausal under 45 years, so treatment should preserve the breast.
- LIN has no typical mammogram features unlike DCIS; it is diagnosed from tissue (LIN from CNB). Then do excisional biopsy to remove the whole lesion and exclude invasive cancer, found together in about 21%.
- If excision shows LIN with positive margin or multiple foci and cannot be fully removed: classic LCIS involving <4 ductal-lobular units in 1 single core and concordant with imaging: no need to re-excise but follow up closely. If discordant, or pleomorphic microcalcification (pleomorphic LCIS, a variant of LCIS), or involving >4 ductal-lobular units in 1 single core (multifocal / extensive LCIS): go back and re-excise to free margins.
- Question of mirror-image biopsy of the contralateral breast for bilateral LIN: now no longer done.
- Next step: counseling on risk reduction of LIN.
  - Close follow-up: CBE every 6-12 months, +/- SBE monthly, + mammogram or US every year and surveillance (the most accepted option).
  - Tamoxifen for 5 years (Schwartz recommends giving to all, but overall close follow-up is no worse). Cameron advises it in high-risk premenopausal patients calculated with the Gail model; weigh benefit vs harm: reduces risk of invasive breast cancer by 50%; risks: thromboembolic disease and uterine malignancy. Aromatase inhibitors (AI) await study results.
  - Prophylactic bilateral mastectomy: because invasive cancer after LIN occurs equally in both breasts, usually invasive ductal carcinoma. Removing both is slightly excessive for LCIS, but mastectomy has a role in BRCA1/BRCA2 positive or strong family history or patients who cannot accept the future risk. Only the breast is removed, without ALND.

## AJCC / TNM staging, 8th ed 2017 (p.252-254)
- Clinical staging is prefixed "c" (uses clinical + imaging data); pathological staging is prefixed "p" (uses pathology). Prefixes: r = recurrent tumor; m = multiple foci of invasive carcinoma; y = post-treatment (tumor already had neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment).
- T stage = tumor size (measured in mm; remember in cm for clinical staging):
  - cT1: not more than 2 cm in greatest dimension.
  - cT2: >2 cm but not more than 5 cm.
  - cT3: >5 cm.
  - cT4: any size with direct invasion of chest wall, or invasion of skin with nodule or ulceration; invasion only to dermis is not T4.
  - cT4a: fixed to chest wall (invasion of pectoralis muscle alone is not T4).
  - cT4b: ulceration/skin nodules/swelling, macroscopic satellite nodule(s) in the same breast skin, or peau d'orange.
  - cT4c: both cT4a and cT4b.
  - cT4d: inflammatory carcinoma (skin looks inflamed).
- N stage = lymph node spread (cN is less important; pN, from pathology, is the one that matters for adjuvant treatment):
  - pN0: no cancer cells in nodes, or a cluster of cancer cells not more than 2 mm (cN0 = no node on imaging + physical exam). [book wording as read; the pN0(i+) line below is the 0.2 mm threshold]
  - pN0(i+): cancer cell clusters in nodes, size not more than 0.2 mm and not more than 200 cells.
  - pN0(mol+): no cancer cell clusters on microscope, but found by molecular testing.
  - pN1: cancer in axillary nodes level 1 or 2 (cN1 = movable nodes on imaging/PE).
  - pN1mi: micrometastases: clusters >0.2 mm and/or >200 cells, but not >2 mm.
  - pN1a: 1-3 axillary nodes with at least one metastasis >2 mm.
  - pN1b: internal mammary sentinel node(s) with metastasis >2 mm (no axillary node).
  - pN1c: pN1a together with pN1b.
  - pN2: 4-9 axillary nodes, or clinical metastasis to internal mammary nodes (cN2 = fixed or matted palpable nodes).
  - pN2a: 4-9 axillary nodes with at least one >2 mm.
  - pN2b: clinical internal mammary node metastasis without axillary node involvement.
  - pN3: 10 or more axillary nodes, or clinical metastasis to level 3 (infraclavicular) nodes (cN3).
  - pN3a: >=10 axillary nodes or infraclavicular nodes positive, with metastasis >2 mm.
  - pN3b: pN1a or pN2a with clinical internal mammary node metastasis.
  - pN3c: metastasis to supraclavicular nodes (palpable supraclavicular node is cN3c).
- M stage: metastasis to cervical nodes or contralateral nodes, or to lung, liver, bone, brain. M0 = none; cM1 = clinical + imaging metastasis; pM1 = histologically proven metastasis >2 mm.
- Figure: example of pN0(i+), isolated tumor cells <=0.2 mm in a node.
- AJCC 8th ed also adds tumor grade (Nottingham criteria), ER, PgR, HER2 and molecular markers into a "prognostic stage" (not detailed here).

## Preoperative work-up (p.255)
- Early breast cancer (stage 1, 2A, 2B, 3A): 1. pathological review; 2. ER, PgR, HER2 status; 3. review mammogram and US of the breast; 4. CBC, LFT; 5. chest x-ray; 6. additional studies only if suspicious: 6.1 bone pain and high ALP -> bone scan; 6.2 hepatomegaly, abdominal pain, abnormal LFT -> CT liver; 6.3 pulmonary symptoms or abnormal chest x-ray -> CT chest.
- Locally advanced breast cancer (stage 3B: T3N1... as printed "3B:T3N1"): 1. same as early breast cancer (items 1-5); 2. bone scan, CT abdomen (with or without pelvis).
- Histopathology of breast cancer: 2 groups: (1) carcinoma in situ; (2) invasive breast cancer: 80% invasive ductal carcinoma, 10% invasive lobular carcinoma; the rest are small groups: Paget, medullary, mucinous or colloid, papillary, tubular, adenoid cystic, squamous cell and apocrine.

# Management of breast cancer (p.255-)
## Stage 0: ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)
- Stage 0 = pure noninvasive or in situ carcinoma, i.e. DCIS. "In situ breast cancer" = not yet invading through the basement membrane: local disease, no lymph node metastasis; treatment focuses on local treatment to prevent recurrence and prevent progression to invasive cancer.
- DCIS: breast cancer of the duct that has not invaded through the basement membrane of the duct, early breast cancer; however DCIS can coexist with invasive cancer in 20-25%.
- Classified as stage 0.
- With screening mammogram, DCIS is found more: 40 years ago only 5% of breast cancer, now up to 25%.
- Abnormality on mammogram: abnormal calcification in 80-85%.
- Signs and symptoms: most patients have none; most are found by chance on mammogram (branching microcalcification); a minority present with a breast mass.
- Figure: extensive DCIS with pleomorphic calcification; fine linear branching microcalcification following the duct.
- Breast MRI finds early DCIS and assesses high grade better than mammogram, but NCCN says MRI is an optional, not routine, test as long-term outcome data are lacking.
- Diagnosis from CNB, which helps exclude invasive cancer. Ask the pathologist to test ER, PR, HER2 and Ki67 as they are used in classification of DCIS; NCCN says ER alone is enough (no targeted therapy given).
- Classification of DCIS: 3 grades:
  1. High grade: high mitosis, central zone or comedo necrosis, ER and PR weakly positive or negative, HER2 negative, Ki67 >30% (fast dividing); triple negative = basal-subtype DCIS, with high recurrence and progression to invasive cancer.
  2. Intermediate grade: between high and low.
  3. Low grade: low mitosis, nuclei fairly uniform, cribriform pattern, ER and PR strongly positive, Ki67 <30% (slow dividing).
- Schwartz (2019 p.564) uses a simple grade from the 2012 breast pathology consensus based on histologic subtype, necrosis and nuclear grade:

| Histology | Cytology | Necrosis | Calcification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comedo | High grade | Extensive | Branched |
| Intermediate | Intermediate | Limited | Limited |
| Non-comedo* | Low grade | Absent | Microfoci inconsistent |
(*cribriform, solid or micropapillary.) Comedo necrosis indicates intermediate or high grade.
- Figure: high-grade DCIS, comedo type (central necrosis).

### DCIS: surgical treatment (p.257-259)
- Aim: prevent progression to invasive cancer.
1. Wide local excision (lumpectomy) alone: consider in low-grade DCIS with low chance of local recurrence, e.g. postmenopausal women with: 1.1 size <2.5 cm with free margin >2 mm; 1.2 low-grade non-comedo necrosis, cribriform subtype. Recurrence about 10% at 5 years.
2. Wide local excision + whole breast RT +/- boost to tumor bed: the most recommended (category 1) because whole-breast RT reduces local recurrence by up to 50%, and when recurrence occurs up to 50% of it is invasive cancer. RT reduces local recurrence but does not improve survival.
   - NCCN recommends whole breast RT in high-risk patients: (1) palpable mass; (2) larger size (>2.5 cm); (3) high-grade DCIS; (4) age <50 years.
   - Do not use RT when: 2.1 previous RT, large breasts, small tumor-bearing... (as read: prior RT, large/pendulous breast, large mass with small breast); 2.2 cannot get free margin, e.g. multicentricity, diffuse calcification, or persistent positive margin >=2 times - in these cases total mastectomy works better.
   - WLE + RT: recurrence about 7% at 5 years and overall survival the same as total mastectomy.
3. Total mastectomy +/- SLN biopsy +/- reconstruction: still a standard treatment; use when local excision + breast RT cannot be done, or patient preference (fear of cancer). Overall survival about 98%, local recurrence 0-2%.
   - Indications for mastectomy: (1) multicentric or widespread disease at several sites (NCCN: >=2 quadrants); (2) unattainable negative margins; (3) large tumor relative to small breast size; (4) radiographic diffuse microcalcification; (5) (continues below).
   - Nowadays for total mastectomy with oncoplastic plan, check whether DCIS is >2 cm away from the nipple-areolar area; if so, preserve the nipple-areolar area for reconstruction.
- Van Nuys Prognostic Index (VNPI) (optional to remember):

| Score | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size (mm) | <=15 | 16-40 | >=41 |
| Margin width (mm) | >10 | 1-9 | <1 |
| Pathological classification | non-high grade without comedo necrosis | non-high grade with comedo necrosis | high grade (grade 3) |
| Age (years) | >60 | 40-60 | <40 |
- Total score 4-6: excision alone; 7-9: excision + RT; 10-12: simple mastectomy + reconstruction.
- Currently the surgeon, if finding a mass <=2.5 cm that is intermediate or high grade, does wide local excision with clear margin at least 2 mm together with RT; RT reduces local recurrence by 50%.
- Axillary dissection is not needed because the chance of node spread is very low, except for a case of WLE where the lesion lies in the tail (which may disturb a later SLN study), or total mastectomy where pathology afterward finds invasive cancer (so SLN study is advised first).
- Chemoprevention in DCIS treated with BCS and ER positive: 1. premenopausal: Schwartz and NCCN advise tamoxifen 20 mg OD for 5 years in every ER+ DCIS (Cameron advises tamoxifen in DCIS); 2. postmenopausal: tamoxifen or AIs. Hormonal treatment reduces local recurrence by 44%. For HER2+, trastuzumab is still in trials.
- SLN biopsy is not usually done (chance of positive LN <1%); do it when: 1. DCIS mass >2 cm; 2. patient is to have total mastectomy (if SLN is not done, and invasive cancer appears in the specimen, SLN after mastectomy is difficult); BCS + RT patients need not; 3. high-grade disease or comedonecrosis (higher chance of finding invasive cancer); 4. microinvasion or micrometastases.
- Surveillance: follow up with history and examination every 6-12 months for 5 years, then once a year; mammogram once a year, together with tamoxifen for 5 years.
- Figure (DCIS algorithm): history and physical exam, diagnostic bilateral mammogram, review of pathology, determine receptor status -> (a) BCS alone (USC/VPNI <7: favorable pathology, small tumor <=15 mm, wide margin of excision >=10 mm): close surveillance in prospective RCT, consider chemoprevention with tamoxifen for ER+; (b) BCS: whole breast radiation, consider tamoxifen for ER+; (c) mastectomy + SLN biopsy (multicentric disease, positive margin, radiation contraindicated).

## Invasive breast cancer and treatment options (p.259)
1. Early breast cancer: stage I (T1 N0 M0 or T1 Nmi M0), IIA, IIB (T1, T2, N0, N1, M0) up to stage IIIA (T3, N1, M0).
2. Locally advanced cancer: stage IIIB onward (T3 through T4d, N2-N3, M0).
3. Metastatic cancer: stage IV and recurrence (M1).
- Two groups of treatment: (1) locoregional: surgery, RT (cryoablation such as RFA is only being tried on small lesions <1.5 cm, still in study, not standard); (2) systemic: endocrine therapy (ET), chemotherapy (CT), targeted therapy.
- Before treatment assess extent with AJCC TNM 8th ed 2017; check biomarkers ER, PgR, HER2, and immunohistochemistry such as Ki67 (rate of tumor cell division).

### Early invasive breast cancer (p.259-260)
1. After tissue diagnosis and ER/PR/HER2 status, further work-up (NCCN): 1.1 review both mammograms; if breast very dense and unsure of occult tumor, send breast MRI; for stage I-IIB: CBC, LFT (esp. ALP); if ALP high do CT or MRI whole abdomen; CT chest only if suspicious symptoms such as cough with blood; bone scan only for bone pain or high ALP. 1.2 Stage IIIA (tumor >5 cm with axillary nodes, not more than 4 nodes... as printed): same as stage I-II but add CT or MRI whole abdomen, CT chest, and bone scan right away; PET/CT is only an option.
2. Treatment summary: early breast cancer = local treatment + systemic treatment. Surgery is the main treatment: breast conserving therapy (BCT) = breast conserving surgery + axillary treatment + RT = treatment of choice; MRM +/- reconstruction is an option for those with contraindications to BCS; reconstruction can be immediate or delayed.
3. If the tumor is rather large in stage IIA (T2N0M0) or IIB (T2N1M0 or T3N0M0) and the patient wants BCS, give neoadjuvant chemo (3-4 cycles); when the mass shrinks, BCS may be allowed.
4. SLN, RT, systemic treatment (endocrine, chemotherapy, targeted) are used when indicated (see later):
   - 4.1 hormone receptor positive: cancer spreads more slowly; treatment = endocrine therapy.
   - 4.2 HER2 positive: more aggressive; treatment = targeted anti-HER2 therapy; when giving anti-HER2, chemotherapy must also be given.
   - 4.3 triple negative (ER, PgR, HER2 all negative): chemotherapy.
5. BCS normally must be followed by post-op RT, with an exception in patients with <5% chance of recurrence: per the Milan randomized trial used as reference, criteria: age >70 years, tumor <=1 cm, node negative, negative margin, ER positive -> BCS + SLN without RT; give tamoxifen for 5 years.

### Locally advanced breast cancer (p.260-261)
1. During core needle biopsy leave a clip (image-detectable marker) in the mass as a landmark, because with induction chemo the mass may disappear completely (complete response, CR); otherwise it is unknown where to operate. Before anthracycline chemo, do echo or MUGA scan; check nodes: with palpable node do axillary US first; if -ve node do SLNB; if +ve node do FNA at once, before chemo. Play safe: after chemo, clip the node under x-ray/US; then re-stage N, plan:
   - 1.1 cN0 -> ycN0: do SLN; 1.2 cN+ -> ycN0: do SLN; 1.3 cN+ -> ycN+: do ALND.
   - Before surgery do metastatic work-up: CXR, bone scan, CT chest, abdomen.
2. PET scan: NCCN recommends in stage 4 or recurrent cancer.
3. Induction chemotherapy with anthracycline group, about 4 cycles; if responding well, continue to complete 4-8 months; if HER2+, add Herceptin (trastuzumab) to chemo to increase response.
4. About 65% of masses shrink and are downstaged; do surgery within 3 weeks after completing chemo.
5. Choose BCS before MRM; from the NSABP B-18 and B-27 studies, neoadjuvant chemotherapy in locally advanced breast cancer gives an overall pathologic complete response of about 13-36% (a minority of patients). After surgery give PO RT to the chest wall to cover the internal mammary node (not removed) and supraclavicular region, + CMT +/- ET +/- trastuzumab; MRM alone for locally advanced cancer is rarely done nowadays.
   - Remember: neoadjuvant hormonal therapy has fewer complications than chemo but is not popular as response rate is low and takes long; keep it as an option for old patients who cannot tolerate chemo.
6. If the mass does not shrink, there are options to make it operable until MRM can be done: 6.1 change anthracycline to taxane; 6.2 preoperative RT; 6.3 if HER2/neu overexpression, give trastuzumab (Herceptin).
- Guideline in Schwartz p.586 for selected stage IIIA and IIIB (figure, Schwartz 11th): clinical stage IIIA and IIIB -> work-up for metastatic disease -> neoadjuvant systemic therapy -> assess clinical response: complete or partial response -> surgical therapy -> adjuvant therapy and follow-up; no response -> consider alternative systemic therapy -> individualize local-regional therapy.

## Surveillance and follow-up in invasive breast cancer, early and locally advanced (p.261)
1. History and PE every 3-6 months for 5 years, then once a year; in addition to general history (eating, drinking, sleeping, eyes, headache, back pain...) follow up.
2. Examine chest wall, scar, axilla and above the clavicle; look at the arm for swelling and shoulder movement; palpate the liver. For patients on tamoxifen: PV exam yearly, no need for endometrial biopsy or transvaginal US; for those on AI measure bone density as a baseline.
3. Mammogram and breast US yearly (baseline).
4. LFT, CEA, CA 15-3, chest x-ray, US upper abdomen, CT chest and abdomen, PET scan, breast MRI, bone scan are not routine; order only when suspicious or symptomatic.
5. Patients on tamoxifen see a gynecologist yearly; those on AIs check bone density as a baseline.
6. Patients with BRCA1/BRCA2 mutation: add breast MRI as an extra option.
7. Keep body weight with BMI 20-25.

## Stage IV metastatic breast cancer and recurrent breast cancer (p.262-265)
- Aims of treatment: 1. know that cure is not possible: weigh longer survival against side effects and cost; 2. palliate symptoms of cancer; 3. good QoL.
- Biopsy every case if possible (local recurrence at chest wall or axilla is easy; if distant, biopsy if safe) and send for cancer diagnosis and new ER, PR, HER2 (second time may differ from first).
- Work-up: CBC, LFT (ALP), CT chest/abdomen +/- pelvis, bone scan; MRI brain if brain symptoms (esp. raised intracranial pressure); MRI spine if symptoms of cord compression.
- Principles of management:
  1. Do not answer that the patient should go and be reborn, prepare the will, or let relatives go book the temple (i.e. no hopeless attitude).
  2. Consider local control of breast and axilla with surgery or RT or both.
  3. Systemic treatment = hormonal treatment, chemotherapy, anti-HER2, considered in every case.
- Principles of hormonal (anti-hormone) treatment in stage 4:
  1. Patient should be ER and PgR positive.
  2. No visceral metastases that are dangerous (lung, liver, brain), since those patients die fast and anti-hormone is too slow: they need chemo.
  3. If the receptor was positive earlier but unknown at the metastatic stage, consider anti-hormone when: 3.1 disease-free interval >2 years; 3.2 metastatic site is indolent e.g. node, skin, soft tissue, bone, pleura; 3.3 age >50; 3.4 menopause already; 3.5 patient previously responded to anti-hormone.
  4. Use one anti-hormone at a time, not several together (no survival gain).
  5. Change anti-hormone when the disease progresses (drug failure); if the disease is stable (improved, or stable disease), continue the same drug.
- Type of drug depends on pre- or postmenopause:
  - Premenopausal: main drug is still tamoxifen; if using tamoxifen, choose a patient who never had it, or stopped it more than 1 year ago. If the patient took tamoxifen and cancer progressed or metastasized within 1 year: do ovarian ablation (bilateral oophorectomy, RT of ovary, or LHRH agonist such as leuprolide, goserelin): response rate 15-50% lasting about 1 year. If tamoxifen fails and the patient refuses oophorectomy, give progestin (megestrol acetate) or AIs to buy time.
  - Postmenopausal: tamoxifen and AIs (anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane); AIs have a better response rate but overall survival is not different. Postmenopausal patients never on AI can start AI right away; if on AI within 1 year, change to the other class: steroidal aromatase inhibitor such as exemestane. Newer anti-hormone agents (CDK 4/6 inhibitor or mTOR inhibitor) buy more time (the doctors in charge look further).
- Principles of chemotherapy in stage 4:
  1. ER, PgR negative patients; 2. resistance to anti-hormone; 3. disease spread to lung, liver, brain (fast-death); 4. do not give together with anti-hormone: finish chemo first; 5. if adjuvant chemo was given more than 1 year ago may use the same group again; if <1 year, change the group (whatever drug, if the disease is old...). Normally give 6-8 cycles then stop.
- Principles of anti-HER2 in stage 4:
  1. Must be HER2 +ve by immunohistochemistry 3+ and FISH positive; 2. give together with chemo, in this setting often a taxane; 3. before trastuzumab check heart function (EF >50%); 4. during therapy check heart every 3 months; 5. duration about 1 year.
- Cases with visceral metastasis (fast-growing, serious): 1. chemotherapy as the base (Cameron recommends taxane); 2. if ER+, add AI at once; 3. if HER2+, trastuzumab; for those who had trastuzumab before, use capecitabine with lapatinib instead.
- Without visceral metastasis: check ER, PR, HER2 and treat according to results.

### Treatment by metastatic site (p.264-265)
1. Bone: Batson's vertebral venous plexus runs from skull base to sacrum, so breast cancer spreads to the spine often; suspect when a patient has back pain, fracture, or LFT shows high ALP -> bone scan + plain film of the painful site. Figure: pathological fracture of the right humerus.
   - 1.1 Reduce bone pain with RT. 1.2 Pathological fracture: prefer surgery (ORIF) over RT. 1.3 Give biphosphonate or zoledronic acid in every case with bony metastasis to reduce risk of fracture from bone loss and reduce bone pain; zoledronic acid 4 mg IV over 15 minutes every 1-3 months for about 1-2 years, together with calcium and vitamin D; newer drugs such as denosumab or pamidronate are still being studied. 1.4 If ER+, give ET. 1.5 If ER-, give CMT, up one grade.
2. Brain: 2.1 patient fit for surgery with expected survival >3 months and a localized or solitary lesion, mass larger than 3 cm with increased intracranial pressure: craniotomy to remove the mass followed by post-op RT. 2.2 Patient unfit, very high risk or multiple lesions: steroid together with RT; if <=3 masses each <=3 cm, boost with stereotactic radiosurgery to the tumor.
3. Spinal cord compression: 3.1 RT gives the best result, together with IV steroid. 3.2 Surgery gives results not worse than RT: operate when the patient has reached the RT dose limit from many previous RT, or needs spinal stabilization, e.g. bony destruction or fracture at the vertebral body.
4. Liver: if resectable, resect with clear margin, then chemotherapy up one grade; (read more in the liver chapter, management of hepatic metastasis).
5. Lung: isolated lesion (found in 15%): prefer resection to RT; if unresectable or with SVC syndrome, RT is the main treatment. Avoid resection in cases with disease-free interval <3 years, more than 1 lung nodule, and tissue diagnosis of lung lesion showing hormone-negative tumor.

## Locoregional recurrence (p.265-266)
- Recurrence is most often seen in the first 2 years.
- Local recurrence: in the ipsilateral breast after BCS, or at the chest wall after MRM. Local recurrence after BCS has 3 types:
  1. True recurrence: at the original lumpectomy site or within boost volume;
  2. Marginal miss: adjacent to the original lumpectomy site but outside the boost volume;
  3. Elsewhere recurrence: in another quadrant.
- Regional recurrence: in chest wall muscle or lymph nodes of the axilla and internal mammary.
- Distant recurrence: cancer spread to other organs, e.g. liver, lung, bone.
- Treatment of locoregional recurrence: principle: tissue diagnosis first, to know whether the recurrence is cancer or just normal fibrosis. If cancer: recurrence or new primary. Re-stage and check ER, PgR, HER2 at the metastatic site.
- Treatment depends on previous treatment:
  1. Local or chest wall recurrence without spread elsewhere:
     - 1.1 Previous BCS + RT: do total mastectomy +/- SLN if ALND level 1, 2 was not done before, or MRM, followed by chemo +/- hormonal treatment +/- trastuzumab, without RT (already had it with BCS).
     - 1.2 Previous MRM: choose between RT and wide local excision; if the chest wall mass can be removed by WLE, choose WLE first with margin 2-3 cm and cover the defect with graft or flap, followed by chemo +/- hormonal +/- trastuzumab; if unresectable, choose RT even if the patient had RT before, if re-irradiation can be done safely (selective case). Figure: chest wall resection with flap cover.
  2. Regional (lymph node) recurrence: if palpable node: surgical resection + RT if possible; if recurrence at supraclavicular or internal mammary: resect if possible, if not resectable give RT.
  3. Systemic treatment: consider case by case, up one grade (change to a stronger regimen).

## Combination of adjuvant therapy (p.266)
- If chemo is combined with AI or RT: give chemo first until the course is finished, then AI or RT.
- Trastuzumab can be given together with AI. RT can be given with AI. RT can be given with trastuzumab, but RT technique must be cardiac-sparing RT.

## Preoperative systemic treatment (p.266-)
- Advantages: BCS becomes possible; patients may change from unable to operate to operable; but do not over- or under-treat.
- Disadvantage: disease progression during therapy.
- Candidates:
  1. Patients with inoperable breast cancer: inflammatory breast cancer; bulky or matted N2 axillary nodes; N3 nodal disease; T4 tumors.
  2. Patients with operable breast cancer who want BCS but the tumor is large relative to the breast (as the book describes, truncated).
- Non-candidates for preoperative systemic therapy: (1) extensive in situ disease but extent of invasive cancer not well defined; (2) poorly delineated extent of tumor preoperatively; (3) tumor not palpable or not clinically assessable.

## Surgical management (p.267-)
- Consists of breast conserving surgery (BCS), total mastectomy +/- reconstruction, and surgery of the axillary nodes.

### Breast conserving surgery (BCS) (p.267-269)
- Currently the first-choice surgical treatment for breast cancer.
- Before BCS check by mammogram +/- US where the mass is and how large; breast MRI is increasingly used as it helps define the cancer location and detect multicentric and multifocal disease, but beware its fairly high false positives.
- BCS = excision of the cancer with a rim of normal tissue; 3 ways:
  1. Lumpectomy: remove the mass with margin all around >1 mm is OK (in places with limited tissue).
  2. Wide local excision: remove the mass with margin all around at least 1 cm.
  3. Quadrantectomy: remove the whole quadrant of breast; the breast deforms more than other methods.
- BCS specimens must be labeled by margin at 3, 6, 9, 12 o'clock or named as medial, lateral, superior, inferior, anterior, posterior margin, for the pathologist.
- Margin status in infiltrating ductal carcinoma:
  1. Positive margin = tumor cells at the inked edge of the specimen.
  2. Negative margin: formerly meant gross margin >1 cm; the current consensus is only "no ink on the tumor" = negative margin.
- In the past, a positive margin case was re-excised within 1 month; now this has changed because even with positive margin, with adequate RT dose the local recurrence rate at the surgical bed is no different from negative margin cases.
- For DCIS the margin usually required is >=2 mm. St. Gallen 2015 accepts: tumor not touching ink / ink-free margin, but not for DCIS, or in young patients, lobular histology, extensive intraductal component, or unfavorable biological subtype.
- Invasive ductal carcinoma: only need tumor not touching the ink margin.
- Absolute contraindications to BCS:
  1. Multicentricity, or lesions in more than 1 quadrant, or mammogram showing diffuse malignant-appearing microcalcification (NCCN) (this is now seen more as a relative contraindication).
  2. Persistent or diffusely positive pathological margin after >2 attempts (widespread disease that cannot be removed with one incision), often with a cosmetic problem.
  3. Pregnancy, especially in the 1st trimester (cannot give RT).
  4. Homozygous (biallelic inactivation) for ATM mutation (NCCN).
- Relative contraindications:
  1. Extensive vascular invasion or extensive intraductal component.
  2. Prior RT to the chest wall or breast (repeat RT has many side effects, but relative because it depends on the dose used the first time).
  3. Size >5 cm or large, pendulous breast (RT often cannot cover; the opposite non-cancer side needs breast reduction too).
  4. Small breast with large tumor (large tumor-to-breast ratio): the breast is ugly afterwards.
  5. Tumor close to the nipple (nipple must be removed, still not as beautiful).
  6. Positive pathological margin (not persistent or diffuse).
  7. Connective tissue disease such as scleroderma, active SLE (should not get RT, except rheumatoid arthritis).
  8. BRCA1, BRCA2 mutation, with high risk of subsequent breast cancer (NCCN: woman with known or suspected genetic predisposition to breast cancer).
  9. Underlying SLE or scleroderma: vasculitis; RT to skin may cause skin necrosis or fibrosis.
  10. Patient preference: does not want BCS, fears RT, or has no time for RT.
- For multifocal disease, nowadays not considered a contraindication to BCS.
- After BCS give RT to the breast within 4-8 weeks, otherwise recurrence at the breast is 30-40%. Also give boost to the tumor bed in cases at high risk of breast recurrence: age <=50 years, axillary LN positive, lymphovascular invasion, close margins.
- Since about 90% of recurrence after surgery is around the tumor, studies compare whole breast RT (takes at least 5-6 weeks) with partial breast RT (only lumpectomy bed + surrounding tissue; no restriction on tumor location even in medial quadrant lesions): shorter treatment, patient need not come to hospital as often, and chemotherapy can start faster.
- ASTRO and the American Society of Breast Surgeons indications for partial RT: (1) invasive ductal cancer in age >45; (2) tumor size <=3 cm; (3) negative margin; (4) negative node; (5) no lymphovascular invasion.
- Factors that increase risk of local recurrence after BCS: (1) young age at diagnosis (<=40); (2) triple negative breast cancer.
- Notes: multicentricity = foci of cancer >=5 cm apart or in a different quadrant; multifocality = foci of cancer <5 cm apart.
- Video-assisted endoscopic breast conserving surgery: promoted in the last 10 years; advantages: operate through the scope via a small wound hidden in the axilla or areola so the scar is invisible, more cosmetic than ordinary BCS; outcomes of margin, recurrence, survival are not different from open; candidates should have tumor <=3 cm (early cancer), solitary (not multicentric), no skin, nipple-areolar complex or chest wall invasion; follow-up another 10 years.

### Total mastectomy (p.269-270)
- The standard treatment nowadays: remove all breast tissue and the nipple-areola complex +/- axillary dissection +/- immediate or delayed reconstruction.
- In the past total mastectomy was done together with ALND, removing the axillary nodes to get at least 10 nodes = modified radical mastectomy (MRM) (MRM = total mastectomy + ALND).
- Indications for mastectomy (= patients unsuitable for BCS): 1. multicentric/multifocal + diffuse microcalcification; 2. positive margin post-BCS after several attempts at excision; 3. locoregional recurrence after BCS; 4. large mass, small breast, ugly after excision; 5. cannot have RT; 6. BRCA1/BRCA2 mutation.
- Mastectomy types:
  1. Madden or Auchincloss: preserve both pectoralis major and minor; ALND removes only level 1 and 2 (level 3 only if palpable). This is the method used now.
  2. Patey: remove pectoralis minor along with ALND level 1, 2, 3.
  3. Halsted radical mastectomy: removes pectoralis major and minor with ALND level 1, 2, 3; no longer used.
  - MRM advantage: RT is not routinely required, and a flat chest wall after surgery detects chest wall recurrence easily. Disadvantage: pain, arm swelling, with axillary dissection, numbness, loss of self-image.
- Nipple-sparing mastectomy (NSM): may be used for small cancers <2.5 cm located >4 cm from the nipple (low chance of nipple involvement); if the nipple is to be kept, send frozen section of the tissue under the nipple; if cancer cells found, do not keep the nipple. Otherwise: patient age <45 years, non-smoker, no neoadjuvant chemoRT; nipple must be normal, no wound, not Paget or inflammatory breast cancer, and no microcalcification at the nipple on mammogram.
- Skin-sparing mastectomy (SSM): used more in early cancer: keep native skin and inframammary fold, remove the breast when reconstructing a new breast so it looks good; local recurrence is not higher than with skin removal. Beware of skin flap necrosis, seen in smokers; so heavy smokers should not use this technique.
- NSM and SSM remove cancer and also emphasize cosmetics; suitable for patients without plan for post-op RT (RT should be delayed until the course is completed) and immediate reconstruction should be done at a breast center.
- Historical: in the Roman era mastectomy used a knife or hot instrument, done in under minutes (figures of ancient mastectomy).

### Approach to the axillary lymph node (p.271)
| Finding | Approach |
|---|---|
| Clinically negative or not palpable | SLN study |
| Radiographically suspicious | US-guided FNA or core: positive -> ALND; negative -> SLN study |
| Clinically suspicious or positive | FNA or core |

### Axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) (p.271-)
- Nodal status is the most important prognostic factor.
- In patients with clinically positive nodes, do US-guided core needle biopsy, send the pathologist for H&E and ask a specialist to read carefully; if positive do ALND level 1, 2.
- Level 3 dissection is done when there is gross disease at level 2 or 3.
- Problem: if the pathologist reads equivocal, do sentinel node.
- Purposes of ALND: 1. staging the axilla: need more than 10 nodes, to plan adjuvant treatment; 2. controlling axillary disease to reduce recurrence; 3. increase survival; 4. help give prognosis.
- Madden ALND: remove only level 1, 2; used with T1-3, M0.
- Halsted ALND: remove level 1, 2, 3; used when gross level 2 involvement is found at surgery.
- Currently: do ALND level 1, 2 only; remove level 3 only when level 3 nodes are palpable (gross metastasis) or at surgery suspected to be level 2 positive: then remove level 3 too; the node count obtained should not be fewer than 10 nodes.
- Indications for ALND (NCCN 2019): 1. clinically positive or FNA-positive axillary metastasis; 2. T4; 3. inflammatory breast cancer; 4. SLND failed; 5. macrometastasis in SLN with >=3 positive nodes in a BCT case.
- Perform ALND slowly and gently; keep the field free of bleeding; risk of complications: avoid injury to the upper brachial plexus, the thoracodial (thoracodorsal) nerve and the long thoracic nerve which lies at the side of the chest wall (causing winged scapula etc.). (Read complications in the operative chapter.)

### Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) (p.272-275)
- The SLN is the first lymph node receiving drainage from the breast cancer, the first station before cancer cells spread to the next nodes; the SLN is a group of 3-5 nodes.
- SLN is one option to decide whether to do ALND or not: if SLN negative, ALND is not needed, reducing complications and morbidity from unnecessary ALND. Figure: injection sites, lymphatic drainage pathway, sentinel node, large excision cavity.
- Mapping agents:
  1. Blue dye:
     1.1 Isosulfan blue 1%: inject 3-5 cc subcutaneously, massage gently 5-10 minutes; the dye binds protein and travels along lymphatic channels; after 5-10 minutes incise at the axilla. Disadvantages: urticaria; anaphylaxis (1% life-threatening reaction); interferes with pulse oximetry (SpO2 reads falsely low because the blue colour interferes with oxygen saturation measurement).
     1.2 Methylene blue: disadvantage skin necrosis.
  2. Radioisotope (technetium-99m labeled sulfur colloid 0.1-0.3 mCi): inject and wait about 20 minutes, then use a gamma probe to find the point with the highest radioactivity (loudest sound), or a count >10 times the surrounding tissue; lymph scan (imaging) may be taken to help find the node. Higher identification rate than blue dye; disadvantage: expensive, requires coordination with the nuclear medicine department (difficult).
  3. Combined 1+2: highest SLN identification rate >95% and lowest false-negative rate.
  4. Indocyanine green (ICG).
- Injection sites: 1. subareolar (periareolar) injection into subdermis around the areola: the dye runs to the axillary nodes of the same side fastest; 2. peritumoral injection (around the mass), suited to recurrent breast cancer; 3. intratumoral injection.
- In practice, isosulfan alone is still highly accurate and cheap, suitable for countries with limited resources, especially with high identification rates in surgeons with much experience. Inject about 5 cc at the subareolar, subdermal, massage 5 minutes, open a small wound at the axilla, dissect along the blue-stained lymphatic vessel under the clavipectoral fascia to find the blue-stained nodes (blue nodes), nodes that are firm, palpable and enlarged; take out all; even without blue colour a hard enlarged palpable node should be taken because tumor in the node blocks dye entry. Remove at least 3 nodes (or more) for examination; residents doing this often can reach SLN identification rate >90%. Figure: radioactive substance or dye injected near tumor -> probe finds sentinel nodes -> tumor and sentinel nodes removed.
- Indocyanine green (ICG): used in liver function testing; as a fluorescent dye 10 mg dissolved in water; inject around the areola and massage 5 minutes so it runs along the lymphatic to the SLN; use a camera that detects fluorescent light; look at the monitor to see the lymph vessel under the skin, then remove the node; check with the camera that fluorescence is in the removed node. Identification rate from Japanese reports up to 99%. Do not use in patients allergic to iodine, or pregnant/breastfeeding women. ICG mapping is another option but larger studies are needed.

#### Indications for SLNB (p.274)
1. Clinically negative nodes; 2. T <5 cm (T1 & T2); 3. no prior systemic treatment (CT and ET); 4. surgeon should have experience of more than 20 SLNB cases; 5. no contraindication.
- Former contraindications now being reconsidered:
  1. Clinically palpable nodes with FNA/CNB negative: trend now is not contraindicated.
  2. T >5 cm (usually has node involvement already).
  3. Previous breast surgery or axillary surgery (SLNB or ALND): can be done now, injecting near the old surgical scar or around the margin of the areola.
  4. DCIS: nodes involved in not more than 0.5%, very few; do SLNB only with indications due to higher chance of invasive cancer: 4.1 DCIS to have mastectomy; 4.2 palpable DCIS or DCIS size >2 cm; 4.3 high-grade DCIS.
  5. (item 5 as numbered in the book is missing in the page; list continues with 6.)
  6. Previous neoadjuvant chemotherapy or previous hormonal therapy (some report against: even if neoadjuvant given, tumor lysis was thought to obstruct lymphatic vessels of the node, preventing dye from flowing into the node; studies show sensitivity and specificity are not different from the non-neoadjuvant group).
  7. Pregnancy or lactation, since isosulfan is FDA category C (possible teratogen).
  8. Allergy.
  9. Multifocal or multicentric lesion (inject at every site).
- Now contraindications to SLN study are being reduced gradually if done with care, after discussing with the patient: multifocal & multicentric (formerly contraindicated, no longer), male breast cancer, patients who had neoadjuvant chemotherapy and were clinically node-negative at presentation; even pregnancy is acceptable if >30 weeks (reports using Tc-labeled sulfur colloid show the radiation dose to the fetus is very small), while isosulfan remains contraindicated in pregnancy.
- Contraindications that remain absolute: 1. FNA or core biopsy proven positive axillary node, or clinically N2; 2. inflammatory breast cancer (IBC); 3. distant metastasis.
- USA standard: surgeon doing SLN alone should train and have done more than 20 cases.
- The SLNs removed are sent as frozen section or fixed in formalin; pathologist stains with H&E; immunohistochemistry (IHC) of SLN is not yet used to decide treatment.
- Removing 3-4 nodes increases accuracy to 98% - better than taking only a single node, which has false-negative up to 17%.

#### Approach to sentinel node metastasis (p.275-276)
- Intraoperative frozen section takes about 30 minutes; staining uses ordinary H&E to see whether cancer cells are in the sentinel node; if frozen is positive it can be trusted, but sections must be fine (standard serial 2-3 mm); if the pathologist is unsure, do not rush: close the wound and wait for the permanent result in 2-3 days.
- NCCN still uses H&E as the deciding stain, without needing IHC or molecular methods such as PCR (DNA of cancer cells at the gene level; still under study).
- Even grinding the SLN and measuring CK19 mRNA gene by one-step nucleic acid amplification assay (OSNA assay) to measure tumor load is a bit much (not used).
- Number of positive SLN: ACOSOG Z0011: need >=3 positive nodes to consider ALND; if only 1-2 positive nodes, ALND adds no 5-year overall survival or disease-free survival; whole breast radiation only (but not partial breast RT).
- 3 levels of positive SLN by size of the metastatic cell group in the SLN (mm):
  1. Tumor cluster or isolated tumor cells (TNM calls pN0 (i+)): <0.2 mm, not more than 200 cells.
  2. Micrometastasis (pN1mi): 0.2-2 mm, cells >200.
  3. Macrometastasis (pN1): >2 mm.
- Action once the SLN result is known:
  1. SLN negative: axillary recurrence <1%: no ALND.
  2. Tumor cluster only = N0 = negative node: no ALND.
  3. Micrometastasis: most believe that if positive 1-2 nodes, in cases treated with BCS + RT, ALND is not needed since local recurrence, disease-free and overall survival are no different from ALND; but if >2 positive nodes, no conclusion yet.
  4. Macrometastasis (>2 mm): now debated whether to do ALND, though there is still pressure to do it, since figures state that macrometastasis has up to 50% chance of spread to non-SLN; but those against ALND say that if you do it routinely, 50% is done for nothing. St. Gallen 2017 advice: avoid ALND if macrometastasis not more than 2 nodes; ACOSOG Z0011 trial: skip ALND in patients with SLN removed >=3 nodes and positive <=2 nodes in T1-2 N0 patients treated with lumpectomy + whole breast RT + systemic treatment.
     - NCCN recommendation (the clearest): ALND may be omitted if all 5 criteria are met: 4.1 T1-T2 tumor (size not more than 5 cm); 4.2 positive SLN not more than 2 nodes; 4.3 BCS surgery; 4.4 plan for whole breast RT that irradiates the axilla as well (tangential field); 4.5 no neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
  5. If no SLN is found, or macrometastasis >3 nodes, or the surgeon cannot tell whether it is reactive hyperplasia or metastasis: do ALND level 1, 2 as usual.

## Radiation therapy (RT) (p.276-)
- Purpose: local control.
- Indications:
  1. every BCS; 2. T >5 cm (T3) and T4 lesion; 3. inflammatory breast cancer; 4. pectoral fascia or pectoralis muscle involvement (chest wall invasion); 5. lymphovascular invasion; 6. close margin or free margin <1 mm; 7. axillary lymph node status: 7.1 positive >=4 nodes; 7.2 gross extracapsular invasion even in a single node; 7.3 diameter >2 cm; 7.4 fixed or matted nodes >3 nodes; 7.5 inadequate LN dissection; 8. palliative treatment for stage IV or recurrent cancer; 9. age <45 years.
- Adjuvant RT after surgery can be delayed about 2-7 months without affecting local recurrence rate. Generally RT is preferred within 4 to 8 weeks after surgery; but if chemo is also needed, give chemo until finished, then RT.
- But waiting for RT should not be longer than 6 months.
- Remember: node negative, size 5 cm or less, margin >1 mm: no post-op RT needed.
- Problem: positive nodes 1-3 nodes still debated whether to give RT; but give RT in the high-risk type for local recurrence: 1. angiolymphatic invasion; 2. perinodal invasion; (list continues on next page).
  3. inadequate margin; 4. inadequate ALND; 5. T3 or more; 6. if T2, give RT when the mass is large but the breast is small, or there is lymphovascular invasion; 7. poorly differentiated tumor; 8. triple negative tumor.
- Axillary RT: survival is no different from ALND but local control is worse than ALND, especially in the clinically positive node group, and axillary RT cannot assess the extent of the cancer in nodes. Usually no RT to the axilla is needed; use axillary RT for cases where ALND cannot be done, or ALND done but nodes could not be fully removed (clinically matted nodes or extracapsular invasion). RT to the axilla should use a 3D technique to reduce radiation dose to lung and heart.
- Complications of axillary RT: 1. lymphedema; 2. shoulder and arm stiffness; 3. rib fracture; 4. brachial plexus damage; 5. pneumonitis.
- Types of RT (to know for tumor conference):
  1. External beam RT: radiation from outside to the whole breast, with lumpectomy reduces local recurrence by up to 50%; disadvantage: takes time, about 5-7 weeks, full course should not exceed 8 weeks, continuous; interruption between sessions should not exceed 7 days. Newer techniques that increase breast dose with less effect on surrounding tissue: 1.1 IMRT (Intensity Modulated RT): radiation from many directions focused on the breast according to the shape of the mass; advantage: can avoid surrounding organs, especially heart and lung, while the breast gets full dose and extra boost dose to the tumor bed; 1.2 IGRT (Image Guided RT), using 3D CT as guide; results further improved.
  2. Brachy RT: also external-beam-like but delivered at short range via catheters placed around the lumpectomy cavity; advantage: 1 week to full dose RT.
  3. Targeted intraoperative RT: boost radiation into the tumor bed during the operation in the OR, similar to what is used for retroperitoneal soft tissue sarcoma; reduces radiation time by 5-10 sessions; currently studies use it in early breast cancer with results equal to conventional whole breast RT.
- NCCN guideline for post-op RT:
  1. Node positive: 1.1 >=4 positive nodes: irradiate the chest wall, supra- and infraclavicular, and internal mammary node; 1.2 1-3 positive nodes in the high-risk group for recurrence: irradiate chest wall with supraclavicular and infraclavicular nodes, including internal mammary node.
  2. Node negative: 2.1 size >5 cm or close margin: irradiate chest wall with supraclavicular +/- infraclavicular and internal mammary node; 2.2 size <=5 cm but close margin or margin <=1 mm: RT to chest wall; 2.3 tumor <=5 cm and margin >=1 mm: no RT.

## Adjuvant systemic treatment (p.277-)
- Breast cancer treatment is now a multimodality approach; each patient is considered in all aspects; patients in the same stage may need different treatment, which St. Gallen calls "personalization of treatment".
- Adjuvant systemic treatment = additional treatment after surgery: chemotherapy, hormonal therapy and targeted therapy, even if no residual disease is seen after surgery; purpose: increase disease-free survival and overall survival.
- Considerations in choosing treatment: 1. patient factors: age, underlying disease, performance status; 2. tumor factors: 2.1 histology, e.g. grading, lymphovascular invasion; 2.2 biologic markers by immunohistochemistry (IHC): ER, PgR, HER2 and Ki-67; 2.3 DNA or gene analysis dividing into 4 subtypes: luminal A, luminal B, Erb-B2 and basal-like (as St. Gallen emphasizes).
- IHC for ER, PgR, HER2 and Ki-67 is still the test used widely, unlike gene-level tests that cost hundreds of thousands of baht each time, can be done only in big centers, and whose reliability is secondary; therefore IHC remains the standard guideline in choosing treatment.

### Biomarkers of breast cancer (p.278-279)
- Tested by immunohistochemistry (takes about 6 hours); interpret by intensity and staining location (nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane).
- Estrogen receptor (ER): nuclear staining of the cancer cells; report the percentage of stained cells from 1% upward, with % of stained cells; also report whether staining is weak, intermediate or strong. Stained >=1% = ER positive; <1% = report ER negative.
- Progesterone receptor (PR): read similarly; helps separate luminal A from luminal B: luminal A should have >30% stained together with Ki67 <20% (as printed here).
- HER2: look at cell membrane or cytoplasm staining (complete or not, strong or weak); 2 methods:
  1. IHC: tests whether the HER2 protein is overexpressed: 1.1 stain 0-1+ = HER2 negative; 1.2 2+ = equivocal, must confirm with ISH; 1.3 >=3+ = HER2 positive: complete membrane staining with strong stain, found in >10% of invasive cancer.
  2. In situ hybridization (ISH): assesses the level of HER2 gene amplification as a ratio to the number of centromeres in the chromosome; 3 ways: 2.1 FISH: fluorescent, ratio with the number of centromeres: FISH positive if >=2.2 = HER2 positive; 2.2 CISH (chromogenic): average HER2 copy number signal per cell: >=6 = HER2 positive; 2.3 DISH (dual-color silver-enhanced): HER2/Chr 17 ratio: ratio >=2 = HER2 positive; ratio <2 = HER2 negative.
- Ki-67: a marker of cell division, helps in choosing treatment, e.g. separating luminal A from luminal B (HER2 negative); Ki-67 correlates with disease-free survival. Positive test = cancer cells in the invasive component stained at the nucleus; if not stained = negative; report the % of positive cells to indicate proliferation. Ki-67 >14 = high proliferation rate.
- With understanding of biomarkers, breast cancers are classified using array gene expression profile into 4 luminal subtypes (molecular classification or intrinsic molecular subtype) that indicate cancer severity: 1. ER+ group has 2 groups: luminal A, B; 2. overexpression Erb-B2 (HER2); 3. basal-like subtype = triple-negative breast cancer (also fits gene microarray into this group).

### Intrinsic subtypes and systemic treatment recommendations (p.280-281)
- Intrinsic molecular subtype = classification into subtypes by molecule; surrogate molecular subtype = classification by IHC, not gene expression. Luminal comes from molecular expression of the tumor compared with luminal epithelium of normal breast tissue.

| Intrinsic molecular subtype | Surrogate molecular subtype | Systemic treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Luminal A: found in 40% (progresses slowly, responds best to treatment, lowest risk of recurrence 1-5%/10 years) | Luminal A-like: 1. ER & PgR strongly positive; 2. HER2 negative; 3. Ki-67 <14% | Endocrine therapy alone |
| Luminal B: found in 20% (compared with luminal A it progresses faster, responds worse, with a higher chance of recurrence 2-8%/10 years) | Luminal B-like (HER2 negative): 1. ER +ve; 2. HER2 -ve; 3. and at least one of: 3.1 Ki-67 >14%; 3.2 PgR negative or low | Endocrine therapy + chemotherapy |
| | Luminal B-like (HER2 positive): 1. ER +ve; 2. HER2 +ve; 3. any Ki-67 or PgR | Chemotherapy + anti-HER2 + endocrine therapy |
| HER2 overexpression: found in 10-15% | HER2 +ve (non-luminal): 1. HER2 +ve; 2. ER & PgR -ve | Chemotherapy + anti-HER2 |
| Basal-like: found in 15-20% (progresses fast, recurs fastest, poor prognosis) | Triple negative: 1. HER2 -ve; 2. ER & PgR -ve | Chemotherapy |

- Figure (summary of molecular subtypes): Basal 15-20% of breast cancers; HER2-E 10-20%; Luminal B 20-30%; Luminal A 40-60%. Receptor expression: HER2+ highest in HER2-E, ER+ highest in luminal; histologic grade high in basal -> low in luminal A; prognosis poor in basal -> good in luminal A; response: chemotherapy in basal, HER2 therapy in HER2-E, hormone therapy in luminal.

### Summary of adjuvant treatment (p.281)
1. RT: indications as before; if chemo is also needed give chemo first, then RT, but not later than 6 months.
2. Endocrine treatment: 2.1 if hormone positive give the drug at once: premenopausal main drug is still tamoxifen; postmenopausal give 3rd generation AI; 2.2 methods: upfront, sequential treatment (switching) and extended 10 (years); 2.3 if chemo must be given, finish chemo first, then give hormone.
3. Chemotherapy: 3.1 for AC regimen check LVEF; if <50% (cardiotoxic), do not use AC; 3.2 non-AC regimen main drug is taxane; 3.3 triple-negative group benefits from chemo; gene-level studies are under way to see which patients should get which chemo.
4. Targeted therapy: 4.1 HER2 +ve with tumor size >0.5 mm [as printed; context: small tumors]: give anti-HER2 together with chemo at once; 4.2 anti-HER2 is cardiotoxic so it should not be given together with AC.

### Endocrine therapy and receptor status (p.281)
| Receptor status | Response rate |
|---|---|
| ER +ve, PR +ve | 75-80% |
| ER +ve, PR -ve | 25-30% |
| ER -ve, PR +ve | 40-50% |
| ER -ve, PR -ve | <10% |
- Therefore only ER-negative, PR-negative patients should not receive hormonal therapy.

## Endocrine therapy for breast cancer (p.282-)
- Mostly given after surgery; neoadjuvant use is not popular since the mass shrinks slowly: maximum response takes 6-7 months, delaying surgical treatment.
- Give to every hormone-receptor-positive patient (ER+ or PgR+), with few exceptions in very-low-risk patients, e.g. T1a.
- Response depends on the % of ER and PgR; if both positive the response is very good; even low positive (>=1%) should get anti-hormone. However, patients with low-positive ER or PgR may not benefit from endocrine treatment alone; they may be candidates for chemo.
- If both endocrine and chemotherapy are needed, give chemo first until finished, then endocrine, because giving them together makes cancer cells divide more slowly and so chemo works less well.
- Three main groups of anti-hormone drugs:
  1. Antiestrogen: tamoxifen, raloxifene, fulvestrant.
  2. AIs: non-steroidal: anastrozole, letrozole; steroidal AI: exemestane.
  3. LHRH analogs: goserelin, leuprolide, buserelin.
- Menopause criteria: 1. previous bilateral oophorectomy; 2. age >=60; 3. age <60 with no menses for 12 consecutive months or natural menopause, without prior chemo or anti-hormone drugs such as tamoxifen/toremifene or ovarian function suppression, and FSH and estradiol in the postmenopausal range; 4. age <60 but previously on tamoxifen or toremifene, FSH and estradiol must be in the postmenopausal range.
- Use depends on pre- or post-menopause and on risk of recurrence.
- Premenopause: estrogen source is the ovary; the popular drug is tamoxifen (an antiestrogen), 20 mg/day for 5 years; AIs are not recommended in premenopausal or perimenopausal women. Consider stopping tamoxifen if abnormal vaginal discharge or thromboembolic complication or CVA appears; if tamoxifen is contraindicated, use ovarian function suppression instead.
- Methods in premenopause (main drug tamoxifen): 1. Upfront monotherapy: tamoxifen alone 5 years reduces mortality by about 10%; later large trials of tamoxifen alone for 10 years found fewer recurrences and better survival than 5 years, but complications are higher, esp. pulmonary embolism, stroke, endometrial cancer. 2. Sequential treatment switching: because a patient who begins premenopausal may become postmenopausal along the way: tamoxifen 5 years first, then AIs 5 years.
- Postmenopause: estrogen comes from fat; aromatase enzyme converts androstenedione to estrogen; so another group blocks aromatase, the origin of AIs. Both drugs can be given, but AIs are preferred over tamoxifen because of better disease-free survival.
- Methods in postmenopause (3 ways, results about equally good): 1. Upfront monotherapy: a single AI for 5 years. 2. Sequential switching: tamoxifen 2 years then AIs 3 years, or AIs first 2 years then tamoxifen 3 years. 3. Extended sequential treatment, total 10 years: tamoxifen 5 years then AI 5 years; overall survival is significantly better, especially in cases with spread to lymph nodes.
- Currently extended monotherapy is starting: tamoxifen alone 10 years seems better than 5; AIs alone 10 years look better than 5; but the disadvantage is higher drug complications.
- Risk of recurrence in hormone-receptor-positive patients:
  1. High risk: lower ER or PgR level, grade 3 (poorly differentiated), high proliferation index, node positive >=4 nodes, lymphovascular invasion, size >=5 cm. Give sequential endocrine therapy; also chemotherapy.
  2. Intermediate risk: size 2.1-5 cm, node +ve 1-3 nodes, grade 2 (moderately differentiated), intermediate proliferation index. Use endocrine as the main treatment, single drug or sequential; chemotherapy by indication.
  3. Low risk: higher ER or PgR level, grade 1 (well differentiated), low proliferation index, no lymphovascular invasion, size <=2 cm, node negative. Endocrine monotherapy for 5 years, no chemo.

### Antiestrogen (p.284)
- 2 groups: 1. SERM (selective estrogen receptor modulation): tamoxifen and raloxifene; 2. SERD (selective estrogen receptor down regulator): fulvestrant.
- Fulvestrant: better than tamoxifen in that it binds estrogen receptor and also destroys the receptor; less stimulation of uterine endometrial tissue; suited to patients with visceral metastasis and no response to AIs; disadvantage: must be injected intramuscularly, inconvenient.

### Tamoxifen (p.284)
- The first anti-hormone drug used; reduces recurrence effectively. 5 years of treatment reduces recurrence by 12% and mortality by 9% whether premenopausal or postmenopausal; it also reduces risk of cancer in the contralateral breast.
- Side effects: 1. Important and worth stopping the drug: DVT, pulmonary embolism, endometrial cancer, drug hypersensitivity, or drug interaction (e.g. Prozac, rifampicin, Coumadin); so do not use in patients with history of DVT or PE or risk of endometrial cancer. 2. Hot flash is the most common (50%). 3. Minor/rare: ocular toxicity, thrombocytopenia, leucopenia, non-specific neurological symptoms. 4. Prolongs effect of warfarin, calcium channel blocker, erythromycin and cyclosporine.
- Not considered complications of tamoxifen: osteoporosis; ischemic heart disease; weight gain; depression; no increase in cholesterol level.
- Notes: 1. Patients on tamoxifen must be monitored for symptoms of endometrial hyperplasia or cancer: abnormal vaginal bleeding, spotting, staining, leucorrhea; if present send transvaginal US; no routine transvaginal US in asymptomatic women (cannot detect early cancer); if symptomatic and endometrium thickened, do endometrial biopsy or D&C; a patient with only a thick endometrium and no abnormal bleeding need not stop tamoxifen. 2. Patients who must take anticoagulants should not get tamoxifen. 3. Antidepressants such as paroxetine, fluoxetine reduce the effect of tamoxifen. 4. In chemoprevention for postmenopausal women, raloxifene gives results as good as tamoxifen but with fewer side effects.

### Ovarian function suppression (OFS) (p.285)
- OFS to stop ovarian function, whether by surgery, radiation, or LHRH inhibitors, can reduce risk of recurrence by 4.2% and reduce mortality by 2.7%; used in premenopausal patients.
- Usually tamoxifen is the main treatment since OFS gives rather poor quality of life.
- ASCO 2016 recommends OFS together with tamoxifen in premenopausal patients at high risk of recurrence.

### Aromatase inhibitors (AIs) (p.285)
- Mechanism: block the aromatase enzyme which converts androgen to estrogen, thus lowering estrogen; used only in women past menopause.
- AIs in widespread use are third generation: anastrozole, letrozole, vorozole, which are specific to inhibition of estrogen production.
- Because estrogen is suppressed, osteoporosis and bony fracture can occur; so do not use AIs in premenopausal women; AIs have heart problems, so be careful in patients with ischemic heart disease.
- Advantage: no severe side effects like tamoxifen's, e.g. thromboembolism and endometrial cancer.
- Disadvantages: 1. Arthralgia in 36%; bone loss 2.6% per year with clearly increased fracture risk; patients often stop drug themselves; so measure bone density before giving AIs; if T score <-2.0, give bisphosphonate, calcium and vitamin D. 2. Cardiovascular disease: AIs raise cholesterol, increasing risk of IHD.

## Chemotherapy (p.285-286)
- Adjuvant chemotherapy, when indicated, reduces recurrence and mortality significantly.
- Indications: 1. triple negative breast cancer with size >0.5 cm; 2. HER2 +ve breast cancer; 3. high-risk patients who should get anti-hormone and chemotherapy too, e.g. grade 3 (poorly differentiated), lymphovascular invasion, high proliferation index, size >=5 cm; 4. when both adjuvant chemo and adjuvant hormonal therapy are needed, give chemo until the planned number of cycles is complete, usually 6-8 cycles (the number depends on the regimen), then adjuvant hormonal therapy.
- Three groups of drugs:
  1. Non-anthracycline based regimen (CMF): use in low risk of recurrence.
  2. Anthracycline based regimen: choose FAC x 6 or CAF x 6 (doxorubicin is the main drug).
  3. Taxane based regimen, e.g. paclitaxel or docetaxel: consider in failure of anthracycline-based regimen or high risk of recurrence.
- Nowadays the CMF formula is going out of fashion; most are started with anthracycline in both pre-op and post-op settings.
- Patients who had pre-op (neoadjuvant) chemo, e.g. inflammatory breast cancer (IBC), should continue chemo after surgery until complete.
- If drug resistance occurs (recurrence within 6-12 months after completion, or cancer progresses during therapy, or no response after 3-4 cycles): 1. previously on anthracycline: change to taxane; 2. if resistant to taxane too: change to ixabepilone and capecitabine, as a single agent or together.
- Drug toxicities: anthracycline: pericarditis-myocarditis syndrome; cyclophosphamide: hemorrhagic cystitis, pulmonary fibrosis; taxanes: seizure, arrhythmia; gemcitabine: pulmonary toxicity.

## Targeted therapy (p.286-)
- 2 groups: 1. HER2 inhibitors: trastuzumab, lapatinib, pertuzumab and others ending in -ib; 2. angiogenesis inhibitor: bevacizumab.
- HER2 is a growth factor, an EGF receptor. Overexpression is found in poorly differentiated cancers that grow fast, recur often, and have poor prognosis.
- Testing for HER2 has 2 techniques: 1. in situ hybridization = FISH and CISH: FISH ratio positive if >=2.2, or CISH ratio positive if >=6 means HER2 +ve; 2. IHC staining: IHC 3+ = tumor cells with intense and complete membrane staining in >30% (as printed here) = HER2 +ve.
- If the FISH assay is positive, no need to confirm with IHC, because FISH accuracy is high; targeted therapy can be given at once.
- IHC 3+ = HER2 +ve; IHC 2+ cannot be concluded, so confirm with FISH.

### Trastuzumab (p.287)
- Humanized monoclonal antibody specific to the HER2 receptor on the cancer cell surface. Used as adjuvant therapy in HER2/neu overexpression: Herceptin IV every 3 weeks for 1 year; it reduces recurrence and improves survival significantly.
- Prospective randomized trial: trastuzumab reduces risk of death by more than 39%.
- Beware cardiac problems, especially when used with chemo. With an anthracycline group, give trastuzumab after the anthracycline course; if given together with chemo, the chemo should be a taxane.
- Before using: MUGA scan or 2D-echo to assess cardiac contraction; ejection fraction (EF) from MUGA should be >50%; repeat echo or MUGA every 3 months and after completing the drug, every 6 months for 2 years.
- If heart function falls >15%, stop the drug for 1-2 months and recheck; if improved, continue; if not improved, or signs of CHF, or during the course tumor enlarges or distant metastasis appears, stop the drug.
- Dose and schedule: 1. every 3 weeks: start with 8 mg/kg over >90 minutes, then 6 mg/kg every 3 weeks, about 17 times, each over about 30 minutes. 2. Every 1 week: start 4 mg/kg over >90 minutes, then 2 mg/kg weekly x12, then 6 mg/kg every 3 weeks for another 13 times.
- Bevacizumab: humanized monoclonal antibody against IgG1 (as printed) directed against VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor); breast cancer has increased VEGF, so trials in early and advanced breast cancer. Summary: bevacizumab is clearly useful in: 1. triple negative breast cancer; 2. together with taxane as 1st-line treatment in metastatic disease, or with capecitabine as 2nd-line. For early stage, whether to use it is awaiting results; likely to be used like trastuzumab.

### Bisphosphonate (p.287-288)
- Names: zoledronic acid and clodronate. Purpose: prevent bone loss when cancer goes to bone, reducing fracture risk, which is accepted.
- It was reported that zoledronic acid has antitumor effect (believed to shrink the cancer), but St. Gallen says this belief is not true.
- Before giving, have a dentist check the mouth because of reported osteonecrosis of the jaw; avoid tooth extraction while on the drug.

## St. Gallen consensus 2017 (p.288)
- Held in Vienna, emphasizing treatment of early breast cancer.
- Free margin of DCIS must be 2 mm or more, while for invasive cancer only "no tumor on ink" (no cancer cells at the inked margin).
- Chemoprevention for DCIS to reduce recurrence: choose tamoxifen or an AI.
- Multifocal / multicentric invasive cancer can have BCS if all tumors can be removed with a good cosmetic result and RT after surgery.
- For nipple-sparing, must biopsy the tissue behind the nipple to confirm no cancer.
- After BCS with SLN +ve not more than 2 nodes: give RT without ALND; but if mastectomy with SLN +ve not more than 2 nodes: choose RT or ALND.
- Other points on RT and chemo not yet read (awaiting 2019).

## St. Gallen consensus 2019 (p.288-289)
- Held in Vienna again this year, focusing on primary treatment of breast cancer, local and systemic.
- Surgery: BCS with positive margin: no clear conclusion yet, tends toward more RT. Also, nipple-sparing in centrally located tumor near the nipple, and skin-sparing technique in inflammatory breast cancer, could be done; votes on it were split.
- Axilla: SLNB may be omitted in specific low-risk situations, e.g. T1 luminal A in age >70; most advise axillary US evaluation; for T2 lesions few agree to omit SLNB.
- Endocrine therapy: duration of adjuvant endocrine therapy in premenopause: stage 1: AI or tamoxifen for 5 years; stage 2 and 3: 10 years (postmenopause: stage 1 give 5 years; stage 2 (N0) give AI 5 years; stage 2 (N+) and up give 10 years).
- Chemotherapy: neoadjuvant vs adjuvant chemotherapy (read the NCCN guideline instead).
- Women treated for breast cancer, ER+ and on anti-hormone: when they may have a child: no conclusion; wait for positive trial.
- Genetic counselling: not routine, but all agree to do it in cases with clear family history of breast cancer and breast cancer patients aged <35 years.
- DCIS: treatment still debated; agreed that in small DCIS in patients <50 years (as printed: age under 50?) G1 or G2 with margin >5 mm is adequate treatment without RT; anti-hormone still debated.
- Next consensus: March 2021 in Vienna.

# Special types of breast cancer (p.289-)
- Hereditary, bilateral, Paget, in male, during pregnancy, inflammatory (read carefully).

## Bilateral breast cancer (p.289)
- A patient with breast cancer has 5 times the risk of cancer in the other breast.
- Synchronous is rare; most are metachronous (interval >3 months).
- High risk of the other side: 1. cancer at age <45; 2. familial or hereditary; 3. invasive lobular carcinoma or LCIS.
- Treatment: 1. synchronous lesions: treat according to stage, using the higher-stage side as the basis; BCS or MRM can be done on both sides at the same time; RT can be given tangentially on both sides at once to avoid heart and lung. 2. Metachronous: treat as a recurrence case.
- In patients with BRCA1 and BRCA2 gene mutation and family history of breast cancer, risk of contralateral breast cancer within 5 years is as high as 31%; this raises the question of prophylactic mastectomy in BRCA1/2 mutation carriers (see HBCS).

## Paget's disease (p.289-291)
- Rare (<1%) lesion at the nipple-areolar area; the cancer that coexists is in situ 10%, invasive 90%; multicentric or multifocal 32-40%.
- Hallmark: Paget's cells = neoplastic cells in the epidermis of the nipple-areolar complex (NAC); they originate from mucosal cells of the duct and spread up through the epidermis of the nipple skin.
- Patient age about 50-70; often presents with eczema-like itchy rash at the areola and nipple, maybe bleeding, ulceration; the nipple is red or scaly, with fluid discharge; symptoms usually begin at the nipple, then spread to the areola; some have a mass at the nipple or palpable.
- Most are diagnosed late because they are treated as skin disease, thought to be contact dermatitis or allergy; but contact dermatitis is bilateral and affects the areola without lesion at the nipple.
- When Paget is suspected: triple assessment (mammogram + US); advise both breasts; mass found on palpation or imaging in ~90%; if a mass is found do CNB; if the result is cancer it is usually invasive intraductal carcinoma; if there is no mass it is usually DCIS.
- If mammogram + US find no mass, NCCN recommends breast MRI next.
- Biopsy: directly at the NAC do a full-thickness biopsy including the epidermis (location of Paget cells and lactiferous duct); if Paget cell +ve and mammogram + US are unclear, use breast MRI to see disease extent.
- Paget cell features: large cells with vacuolization, clear/pale cytoplasm, large round or oval nuclei arranged in the epidermis, not crossing the basement membrane into stroma.
- Treatment: like breast cancer without Paget: WLE of the nipple and areola with clear margin, axillary staging, followed by RT; or mastectomy + axillary staging. NAC reconstruction after RT 4-6 months.
- NCCN 2019 divides patients into 2 groups by triple assessment, whether there is a breast lesion:
  - Group 1: with breast lesion: core biopsy at the breast lesion together with full thickness biopsy of the NAC lesion, then read pathology:
    1. Breast & NAC biopsy negative: follow up and re-biopsy if the wound does not heal.
    2. Breast DCIS & NAC Paget: treat as DCIS.
    3. Breast invasive cancer & NAC Paget: treat as invasive breast cancer.
    4. Breast negative for cancer & NAC Paget positive: consider breast MRI & tissue sampling of the suspicious part. Options: 4.1 BCS + remove NAC followed by whole breast RT; 4.2 total mastectomy +/- sentinel lymph node study +/- reconstruction; 4.3 central lumpectomy including NAC followed by whole breast RT.
  - Group 2: no breast lesion: full thickness biopsy at the NAC lesion and read: 1. if +ve for Paget treat as item 4; 2. if -ve treat as item 1.

## Hereditary breast cancer syndrome (HBCS) (p.291-)
- A syndrome of interest to residents because it is examined, every year.
- In HBCS the subgroup most often found is hereditary breast ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOCS), with mutation of BRCA1 or BRCA2 genes.
- Cases to suspect HBCS: 1. cancer before age 40; 2. patient or relatives with cancer of ovary or colon; 3. relatives with breast cancer at age <50; 4. patient or relatives with bilateral breast cancer; 5. male relatives with breast cancer. (Figure: MRI of bilateral breast cancer with nodes.)
- When a suspected genetic patient is found: 1. ask family history of breast cancer, ovary, thyroid, and CRC, including relatives with cancer before age 40, or helpful: bilateral breast cancer, or male relatives with breast cancer; 2. if history fits HBCS, send for genetic counseling, down to the BRCA test level; 3. treatment is the same as non-HBCS.
- HBCS is autosomal dominant; approach relatives too (many offspring).
- Recommendation for genetic testing: 1. known BRCA1 or 2 mutation in the family; 2. patient or close relatives with breast cancer diagnosed <45, or 2 relatives with breast cancer one of whom is <50 (age >50 is mostly sporadic breast cancer, not an indication for genetic testing); 3. patient or close relatives with male breast cancer, ovarian or fallopian tube cancer; 4. multiple pancreatic cancer.
- Approach to relatives who are BRCA +ve but not yet cancer, screening: 1. teach breast self-examination monthly from age 18; 2. clinical breast exam every 6 months from age 25; 3. mammogram yearly from age 25 or 10 years before the youngest relative with breast cancer; 4. nowadays breast MRI together with mammogram, as a package, for both.
- Prevention, 2 methods: 
  1. Chemoprevention: tamoxifen tends to be given, reducing breast cancer risk about 50%; AIs are not used for prevention.
  2. Prophylactic bilateral mastectomy: the most effective prevention, on the true principle "no breast, no cancer"; total or skin-sparing mastectomy must be discussed well with patient and relatives, especially cases that cannot follow a screening program or with FCC with hard nodular breasts that are hard to examine; remove both breasts at once; and tell that bilateral mastectomy does not eliminate breast cancer risk 100% (reduces it 90-95%). Subcutaneous mastectomy does not work.
  3. Prophylactic bilateral oophorectomy: because ovarian cancer risk in BRCA1 and BRCA2 carriers is 10 times that of the normal population; in those with enough children or past menopause, recommend removing both ovaries; reduces breast cancer risk by 50% and ovarian cancer risk by 90%.

## Inflammatory breast cancer (IBC) (p.293-295)
- 1-5% of breast cancers; looks like mastitis, so sometimes the patient is mistakenly given antibiotic for months.
- Features: breast grows fast, painful, within 3 months skin is red, swollen and thick like orange skin, and often already spread to nodes.
- Note: patients are often old, not young; the nipple is swollen, red and hot over >=1/3 of the breast; the skin looks like orange peel (peau d'orange) from skin edema with dimpling of hair follicles; dermal lymphatic obstruction. Criteria for diagnosis of IBC: (1) rapid onset of erythema, edema and peau d'orange appearance or abnormal breast warmth, with or without a palpable lump; (2) symptoms present <6 months; (3) erythema covers at least a third of the breast; and finally (4) pathologic confirmation that it is cancer.
- Suspected IBC: send mammogram and US even if a mass is palpable or not; Greenfield advises breast MRI in every case to assess extent of disease.
- Mammogram may only show skin thickening; MRI shows more; still must biopsy. Figure: IBC mammogram, US, MRI.
- Tissue diagnosis: 1. if skin shows inflammation or a mass is palpable or imaging abnormal: incisional biopsy of the mass together with the skin attached/involved skin; 2. if the mass is palpable or imaging abnormal but overlying skin erythema is minimal: percutaneous core biopsy.
- Pathology: the classic is malignant cells within dermal lymphatic channels, but this is found in only 75%. Nowadays diagnosis of IBC is mainly clinical; pathology confirms; molecular subtype is often triple negative.
- IBC often has nodes and distant metastasis; at diagnosis axillary node metastasis 60-85% and distant metastasis 20-35%.
- Staging: T must be T4 (T4d); overall stage at least IIIB; work-up like advanced breast cancer (CT and bone scan).
- Treatment: 
  1. If no distant metastasis: start with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (induction), anthracycline at least, usually combined with taxane; (that is the standard).
  2. If HER2 +ve, add trastuzumab.
  3. Then assess response: 3.1 no response: give new chemo and consider RT to breast and regional lymph nodes, or irradiate first; if operable, do MRM + ALND level 1, 2, finished with chemo, with endocrine therapy if ER+; 3.2 partial or complete response: go to OR, usually MRM (NCCN does not recommend BCS because of high local recurrence, including distant metastasis); then post-op RT to breast and regional lymph nodes; check ER, PR status; if ER+ or PR+ continue endocrine therapy.
  4. As before, if HER2 +ve continue trastuzumab. 5. PORT to chest wall covering supra- and infraclavicular nodes and internal mammary nodes. 6. If distant metastasis (stage 4): systemic therapy; choice of chemo by tumor features and drug toxicity.
- 5-year survival 7-50%.

## Male breast cancer (p.295-296)
- Not more than 1% of breast cancer. Age at diagnosis about 65, older than females. Risk factors as in women, except menses and parity; also Klinefelter syndrome; about 30% have family history.
- Presentation: mostly palpable breast mass, often found under the nipple, harder and craggier than gynecomastia; peau d'orange of skin often; nipple retraction; chest wall invasion easy; hence when diagnosed it is usually late/advanced; nipple discharge is uncommon in male breast cancer.
- When a lump is palpable do core biopsy; histology is infiltrating ductal carcinoma.
- In men DCIS is little, and LCIS is not found at all; male breast cancer is ER+ in 75-94% while HER2 is mostly negative.
- Treatment is no different from female; 3 options: 1. total mastectomy + ALND or SLN biopsy is the most used, because the male breast is small; indications for systemic treatment are the same as for women; 2. if chest wall invasion, surgery must be radical mastectomy; 3. BCS is not popular except in a patient who is very old or with many comorbidities, requiring RT afterwards. (Figure: MRM in male breast cancer.)
- Post-op RT in T3 or N+, or R1 mastectomy.
- Principles for ET, CT and targeted therapy as in females; for ET St. Gallen 2015 advises tamoxifen, since AIs raise testosterone levels.
- For advanced-stage ER+ patients, besides tamoxifen consider orchidectomy to reduce estrogen.
- At the same stage prognosis is no different from female breast cancer.

## Breast cancer during pregnancy (p.296-)
- Although a breast mass found in pregnancy has <25% chance of being cancer, nowadays breast cancer in pregnancy is found more often; since in pregnancy the breast enlarges, is thick and firm, examination is usually delayed; diagnosis is delayed about 5-15 months, so tumor masses of pregnant patients are larger and more likely to have positive nodes (75%) than in non-pregnant women.
- No report shows cancer cells passing across the placenta to the fetus.
- If suspected, start with US to see whether cyst or solid; for a solid mass do core biopsy, which is possible in pregnancy. Mammogram can be done in pregnancy, better if using a lead shield for the child, or use MRI instead.
- MRI that is safe for the child: done from the 2nd trimester onward, without gadolinium contrast.
- Chest x-ray can be done, but CT chest should not (radiation); if needed send MRI chest without contrast. Liver: US; brain: MRI brain without contrast. Bone scan can be done since radiation is low.
- Tissue should be taken by CNB; histology is mostly poorly differentiated; ER and PgR are mostly negative (chance of positive no more than 25%), while HER2 positive is about 30%.
- In pregnancy stage 3, 4 are found often; however at the same stage prognosis of pregnant and non-pregnant women is the same.
- Treatment (NCCN 2019): 1. First trimester: if the patient still wants the baby, do mastectomy + axillary staging (RT cannot wait); leave reconstruction until after delivery.
  2. Second trimester up to early third trimester: 2 options: 2.1 mastectomy or BCS followed by axillary staging; 2.2 neoadjuvant chemotherapy first, then 2.1, with axillary staging after delivery.
  3. Late third trimester: treat as in 2.1.
- Axillary management: if the node is enlarged, take a needle biopsy; if negative node and SLNB is planned, it can be done using radioisotope (Tc sulfur colloid) in pregnancy; do not use isosulfan because of reports of fetal anomalies in animals (St. Gallen 2015), but the majority of opinion advises doing ALND.
- RT and endocrine therapy (especially tamoxifen, with thromboembolic events), and anti-HER2 (causes oligohydramnios) should be given after delivery.
- Chemotherapy: do not use in the first trimester (spontaneous abortion and birth defects); can give in the second and third trimester, but stop chemo after 35 weeks or 3 weeks before delivery because hematologic complications may occur during delivery. Do not give methotrexate in pregnancy. For pregnant patients FAC (anthracycline based) is recommended.
- In pregnancy do not give Herceptin (target) because it causes oligohydramnios, possibly fetal death at birth. Endocrine therapy also should not be used because reports show birth defects and the baby dying at delivery.
- Suppression of lactation and therapeutic abortion do not improve prognosis, so there is no need to terminate pregnancy.
- Breast feeding after BCS is not contraindicated, except in patients on active chemotherapy, targeted therapy or endocrine therapy: stop breastfeeding.
- Normally pregnancy should be avoided during chemo, RT and endocrine therapy; if wanting a child, wait about 2 years, except cases with high chance of recurrence: wait 3-5 years.

## Unilateral axillary adenopathy (p.297-298)
- Needs complete evaluation to find the cause.
- Most causes are reactive hyperplasia from nonspecific inflammatory change, systemic infection or granuloma; a minority (<25%) are due to malignancy.
- Most common malignancy: lymphoma (14%), then metastatic carcinoma (10%) usually from breast and skin melanoma of the back and arm.
- Examine breast and look at skin for pigmented lesion.
- Imaging: for breast: age <35 choose US, age >35 choose mammogram; for lymph node: US as aid: 1. asymptomatic patient with normal-appearing lymph node: follow up; 2. abnormal US morphology: US-directed needle biopsy.

## Axillary metastases from unknown primary (p.298)
- Generally enlarged axillary lymph nodes are mostly benign; a minority are cancer, of which mostly lymphoma, then secondary.
- If suspecting cancer, do FNA or CNB of the node; if the result is metastatic adenocarcinoma, the primary may come from breast, lung, stomach, intestine, thyroid, liver, ovary, uterus, bladder, etc. immunohistochemistry + CT chest/abdomen may help find the primary.
- But if the patient is a woman, the primary comes from the breast about 90%.
- Breast cancer has about 3% chance of presenting as occult cancer, i.e. no breast mass found on examination, only an enlarged lymph node in the axilla.
- Start examining and investigating the breast first with mammogram and breast US; if a suspicious spot is found, take a needle biopsy; if no abnormality is found, do contrast-enhanced MRI next.
- Breast MRI helps detect lesions that are likely to be occult breast cancer in about 70%.
- If breast MRI finds no breast lesion, do chest and abdominal CT, which might show adenocarcinoma from GI or lung.
- MRI positive breast disease: do US or MRI guided biopsy; if the result is breast cancer, treat by stage.
- MRI negative breast disease: 2 options: 1. mastectomy + ALND; 2. ALND + whole breast RT +/- axillary RT.

## Oncoplastic surgery in breast cancer (p.298-299)
- A surgical approach increasingly used: besides hoping to cure the disease, hope for beauty, without disturbing the cancer operation plan; not delaying treatment, and with no negative effect on adjuvant treatment and follow-up.
- Oncoplastic surgery comprises 2 main parts: surgical oncology and plastic reconstructive surgery. After BCS, the breast may deform 20-30%; this can be fixed with a volume displacement technique = removing breast tissue, dissecting the front under the skin and the back, then displacing the tissue from its original place to sew the sides together; may be done on one or both sides. In mastectomy cases use a volume replacement technique with a myocutaneous flap from muscle, or medical materials such as silicone-filled implant to add fullness.
- Principles of oncoplastic surgery: 1. the patient must understand purpose, method and complications well; may need to adjust the other breast to the same size; 2. choose patients with favorable prognosis, but unfavorable cases are not a contraindication; 3. advantages: still female shape, good body image, less psychological loss, no external prosthesis (silicone or paper tissue stuffed in); 4. does not delay detection of recurrence or follow-up; 5. immediate reconstruction can be done at the time of mastectomy, causing less psychological loss to the patient; 6. must not delay adjuvant treatment; if delayed reconstruction, usually do after complete chemotherapy and RT; 7. age alone, even if old, is not an absolute contraindication; 8. currently oncoplastic surgery is used in BCS to make both sides equal, considered when removing more than 20% of breast tissue.
- Reconstruction can be at mastectomy (immediate) or later (delayed).
- Immediate reconstruction should not be used in inflammatory breast cancer, because of aggressiveness, frequent recurrence, and RT after surgery.
- Delayed reconstruction is used in cases needing staged procedures: 1. surgery on the opposite breast to improve symmetry; 2. revise the breast that was removed; 3. nipple and areolar reconstruction and tattoo pigmentation.
- Breast reconstruction with volume replacement by silicone implant or flap: immediate or delay as stated; contraindications to immediate reconstruction: 1. non-resectable chest wall disease; 2. rapidly progressive systemic disease; 3. serious comorbidity; 4. psychological problems.
- Surgical implantation: 1. implant inserted under pectoralis muscle, e.g. silicone gel; 2. autologous tissue: 2.1 from rectus abdominis = TRAM flap; 2.2 from latissimus dorsi = LD flap; 2.3 from gluteus muscle = gluteus flap; 3. composite: 1 + 2 together; 4. volume displacement or mastopexy by mobilizing normal breast tissue to close, in BCS; 5. skin-sparing mastectomy and nipple-sparing mastectomy (see MRM section).
- A problem arises in cases needing post-op RT: if silicone is used contracture may occur; autologous tissue is better, but beware the breast may become hard, twisted or shrunken.
- Figure: silicone implant tissue expansion before and after.

### Summary of options for breast reconstruction (p.300)
- Many, but the points to know: latissimus dorsi flap and TRAM flap.
- List: Autogenous: abdominal-based flaps: TRAM (single pedicle, double pedicle, free flap*, deep inferior epigastric perforator flap*); upper abdominal horizontal flap; vertical abdominal flap; tubed abdominal flap. Latissimus dorsi musculocutaneous flap. Gluteal flap* (superiorly based, inferiorly based). Rubens flap*; thoracoepigastric flap; lateral thigh flap*; breast-splitting procedure (historical). Alloplastic: silicone gel implant; silicone implant with saline fill (smooth wall, textured wall, round, anatomic shaped); silicone injection (historical). Combination procedures: latissimus dorsi flap with implant; TRAM flap with implant. (* requires microsurgical procedure.)

### Latissimus dorsi (LD) myocutaneous flap (p.300-301)
- Popular; donor wound is toward the back, no scar at the front, so cosmetic.
- Blood supply: thoracodorsal artery via the subscapular artery.
- After an LD flap it is not necessary to add silicone.
- Technique: open wounds at the back and side to reach the LD muscle; dissect the muscle off from serratus anterior and trapezius; take care not to cut the thoracodorsal artery; the lower part is dissected down to the iliac crest. Problem: long visible scar, not beautiful. (Figure: skin, fat, muscle and blood vessels moved to the chest.)
- Endoscopic LD flap: open small wound, insert a scope to dissect the LD muscle; the donor wound is small and very cosmetic.

### Transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous (TRAM) flap (p.302-304)
- The abdomen is an excellent source of autologous tissue for breast reconstruction.
- Rectus abdominis gets blood supply from the superficial epigastric artery (as printed; figure labels superior epigastric artery and vein, and deep inferior epigastric artery and vein; flap zones I-IV).
- Flap may be a free flap or a pedicle flap.
- Advantage: no extra prosthesis needed (the flap gives fullness already). If the surgeon is experienced, complications are very low.
- Indications and contraindications of TRAM flap (figure): Indications: breasts of all sizes; breast ptosis. Relative contraindications: smoking; abdominal liposuction; previous abdominal surgery; pulmonary disease; obesity. Contraindications: previous abdominoplasty; patient unable to tolerate a 4-6 week recovery period; patient unable to tolerate a longer procedure.
- Notes: 1. obesity = BMI >30; 2. smoking: if planning TRAM, must stop at least 6 weeks; in heavy smokers (more than 10 packs or about 100 packs per year as printed) beware skin flap necrosis.
- Even a cancer in the center, close to the nipple, which formerly required total mastectomy, can now have BCS by central quadrantectomy removing breast and skin including nipple with ~1 cm margin and closing the defect with Grisotti's inferior dermoglandular flap (use the tissue below to rotate and close the defect).
- Oncoplastic surgery of breast cancer is a field of interest for breast fellows, or those in big hospitals with a breast center; for many provincial hospitals that have no one doing it, prepare answers when patients and relatives ask how to have beautiful breasts after cancer surgery (refer).

## Complications of surgery of breast cancer (p.304-)
- Breast cancer surgery now removes less and less; radiation therapy uses many specialists; besides longer survival, the patient can still have both breasts, as a balloon-like implant from latissimus dorsi or rectus abdominis flap ("a breast is better than none").
- BCS is the procedure of choice, and if beauty is wanted add oncoplastic surgery; keeping skin = skin-sparing, or keeping the nipple = nipple-sparing.
- MRM is still a standard treatment particularly in developing countries like Thailand where provincial surgeons are overloaded (no resident to prepare, radiation centers few, drugs hard to get).
- After breast surgery, especially MRM, advise the patient to: not have blood draws, BP measurement or injections or lift heavy things with the arm on that side. Care: keep skin healthy, avoid trauma such as bites, use gentle soap, do not let the skin dry; if the arm is operated, beware infection: going to sea, sun, gardening; use sun block; exercise too.

### Wound infection (p.305)
- Found in 3.8%; most common organism staph.
- Etiology: 1. extensive tissue dissection; 2. previous open excisional biopsy; 3. prolonged catheter drainage (a drain reduces SSI, but leaving it too long is bad); 4. malnutrition; 5. medical comorbidity.
- Treatment as for any abscess.
- Prophylactic antibiotic: do not overuse; not routine, though reports say antibiotic can reduce SSI, only for cases having ALND.

### Tissue necrosis (p.305-306)
- Seen at the margin or flap; incidence 8%; if it occurs the wound will be inflamed 21%.
- Etiology: 1. flap too thin; 2. flap under too much tension; 3. previous RT of chest wall.
- Treatment: 1. minor necrosis, defect <2 cm2: dress the wound regularly, wait, debride, healing by secondary intention. 2. Major necrosis: consider; the wound heals itself in most; if the patient is fit, take to OR and close with graft or flap.
- Prevention: 1. flap thickness about 5 mm; 2. no tension; 3. if previous chest wall RT, wait more than 4 weeks before MRM.

### Hematoma (p.306)
- Found 2-10%. Symptoms: swollen flap, ecchymosis; a large hematoma may be painful. Figure: bulge under flaps with discoloration (red, blue, violaceous) suggests blood accumulation; needle aspiration - blood.
- Treatment: 1. mild: 1.1 if a drain is still in, may observe; 1.2 if the drain is off or none placed, aspirate; if hemolyzed blood, it can be sucked out, then apply an elastic bandage. 2. Moderate to severe: flap swells rapidly: urgently explore the wound, evacuate clot, then control the bleeding point and place a new drain.
- Prevention: 1. in MRM beware perforator branches of the internal mammary artery arising from the intercostal muscle when cutting pectoralis muscle; they may retract when cut; if the bleeder cannot be found, take a figure-of-eight stitch (ligation) rather than cautery when found; 2. before closing, irrigate clean and make sure it is dry; 3. large or small breast until sagging: use brassiere or support.
  4. 1-2 weeks before surgery, if possible stop drugs with antiplatelet activity, e.g. ASA, NSAIDs, and herbs such as garlic and ginger-type (กระเทียม, โสม = garlic, ginseng).

### Pneumothorax (p.307)
- In the Halsted mastectomy era it occurred when trying to clamp bleeding perforator branches in the intercostal space beside the sternum; a clamp pushed in deeply could pass into the pleural space.
- Nowadays seen with wire localization when the needle is pushed in too deep, penetrating the pleura.
- Treatment: chest tube (ICD) when needed.

### Seroma (p.307-308)
- The most common complication; can cause wound dehiscence or wound infection.
- Causes: 1. lymphatic vessel injury; 2. extensive flap dissection leaving a large raw surface; 3. remaining dead space.
- Location: in the cavity from lumpectomy, or at the anterior chest wall after mastectomy, or at the axilla after ALND.
- It is unclear whether it is a true complication, since the fluid collected has more lymph fluid than bloody fluid.
- Treatment: 1. if a drain is still in, wait until fluid is <20 cc per day for at least 2 consecutive days, then remove the drain. 2. If the drain is already out, no need to place a new drain: aspirate about every 2-3 days; but if aspirated more than 3-4 times and it does not stop, or is infected, place a new redivac drain.
- Prevention: 1. closed suction drain size 12 F in the axilla and at the lateral border of the sternum under the mastectomy flap; 2. closure of dead space helps but risks stiff joint; seems not worthwhile; 3. octreotide to reduce seroma and lymphatic fluid seems excessive; 4. shoulder immobilization: RCTs show it does not reduce seroma, but most surgeons advise it; 5. methods that did not help reduce seroma: operating with laser, sclerosant injection such as tetracycline, fibrin glue, 4 drains, compression dressing or pad pressing the axilla.

### Lymphedema (p.308-309)
- Accumulation of protein-rich fluid in soft tissue from obstruction of lymphatic flow, usually sparing the muscle layer, so the arm on that side swells; problems: discomfort, pain, easy infection, and can become sarcoma.
- Incidence 25-30% after ALND; even with SLNB alone it is found in 6.9%, especially when the SLN lies right at the axillary vein; and in cases with ALND followed by axillary RT, 50-60% certain (as printed).
- Clinical: a swollen arm that is not exacerbated by physical activity; arm heaviness and pain; infection is frequent; early swelling pits on pressure; later it becomes firm and non-pitting; also frozen shoulder may occur.
- How to confirm arm swelling: 1. measure circumference compared with the other arm at the same position, standard point ulnar styloid or tip of olecranon; difference >10 cm (as printed) means definite swelling; 2. or put the swollen arm in water to above the epicondyle about 15 cm; if the overflow is >200 cc, the arm is swollen.
- Treatment: 1. mainly conservative: weight loss, exercise, arm elevation, PT, massage, elastic bandage, or intermittent pneumatic compression; if lymphangitis occurs, give antibiotics; 2. low-level laser therapy increases lymphatic vessel pumping and stimulates regeneration of lymph vessels; 3. surgery: excise thick swollen tissue and cover with graft, or lymphatic venous shunt: not very effective.
- Prevention: 1. good surgical technique, adequate hemostasis; 2. consider SLN study first if no contraindication; 3. do only ALND level 1, 2; 4. do not irradiate the axilla if not indicated; 5. from level 1 data, control weight; avoid weight gain to help prevent lymphedema.

### Lymphangiosarcoma (p.309)
- Very rare, about 0.07%; believed to result from chronic lymphedema more than 10 years after MRM.
- Some call it angiosarcoma; classic name Stewart-Treves syndrome (named from a report of 50 cases after Halsted mastectomy).
- Clinical: satellite subcutaneous nodules on the swollen arm, red-purple to bluish (ecchymosis-like), at the inner and anterior aspect.
- Distant metastasis very fast, especially lung and brain.
- Very poor prognosis: untreated survival about 6 months; treated about 19 months.
- Treatment: 1. WLE or interscapulothoracic amputation; 2. RT + chemo.

### Nerve injury (p.309-312)
- Principle: preserve every nerve that can be preserved.
- Beware especially of the long thoracic nerve; if cut it is a big problem.
- During ALND do not use electrocautery; the resident holding the retractor should use a small retractor and not pull too long, 15-30 minutes, so that the person operating does not take long.
- Figure: brachial plexus and axillary nerves (dorsal scapular, suprascapular, lateral and medial pectoral, long thoracic, thoracodorsal, upper and lower subscapular, axillary, radial, median, ulnar, intercostobrachial).

#### Long thoracic nerve injury (p.310-311)
- Anatomy: lies behind (dorsal to) the axillary vein, anterolateral to... [as printed: located at the back of the axillary vein, anterolateral], a motor nerve to serratus anterior, whose function is to hold the scapula against the chest wall and help push things forward.
- If cut: winged scapula; the patient cannot lift, push, or raise the arm; shoulder falls; loss of function. Figure: paralysis of serratus anterior; winging of the scapula (medial border projects backward when pressing against a wall); inability to raise the arm above the head (inability to rotate the scapula during abduction above a right angle); mechanism of shoulder abduction (supraspinatus 1, deltoid 2, trapezius and serratus anterior 3).
- So it is a nerve to be preserved carefully; if cut, repair.

#### Thoracodorsal nerve injury (p.311)
- Anatomy: at the midpoint of the axillary vein, anterolateral to the long thoracic nerve; a motor nerve to latissimus dorsi, whose function is extension, rotation and adduction of the arm.
- If cut: internal rotation and abduction of the shoulder are weaker, but not much of a problem because neighboring muscles can compensate.
- Injury occurs often because lymph nodes lying close to it; the surgeon is tempted to cut the nerve.
- Try to preserve it, especially in cases planning oncoplastic mastectomy with a latissimus dorsi flap.

#### Medial pectoral nerve injury (p.311)
- Anatomy: lies right along the upper lateral border of pectoralis minor.
- A motor nerve to pectoralis major and minor.
- If cut: atrophy of the pectoralis muscles, so the breast looks flat and tight and lacking cosmetic result, and internal rotation and abduction are difficult.

#### Intercostobrachial nerve injury (p.311-)
- Anatomy: comes from the 2nd intercostal space, runs transversely across the axilla below the axillary vein about 2 cm (continues on next page).
- It is nearly always cut, because it runs through the node group that must be removed.
- A sensory nerve: if cut there is numbness of the upper lateral chest wall and the inner upper arm; numbness usually improves within 6 months, except when a neuroma forms and causes pain = chronic pain syndrome.

#### Chronic pain syndrome (p.312)
- Also called postmastectomy pain syndrome; cause presumed neuropathic.
- Found in 20-30%, especially in young patients; may come with anxiety, depression of various kinds.
- Burning-type pain that greatly reduces quality of life.
- Exclude tumor recurrence.
- Treatment: 1. amitriptyline or venlafaxine (serotonin uptake inhibitor, reported effective); if not better, add analgesics, NSAIDs, some need morphine group; if pain persists, try local anesthetic injection, or injection into the painful point. 2. Re-explore to find the neuroma and remove it, though it is hard to find, like looking for a needle in a haystack.

### Axillary vein injury (p.312)
- Usually a surgical error from the surgeon pulling, tearing or cutting into it.
- If direct injury, the vein is cut and bleeds; easy to see; but if thrombosis occurs there is pain and arm swelling after surgery.
- Beware in ALND: axillary vein injury may occur if: 1. bulky axillary nodes; 2. previous operation; 3. previous RT.
- Treatment: repair with Prolene 4/0 or 5/0; even though ligation is possible, do not do it because arm swelling would occur.

### Axillary artery injury (p.312)
- Usually not injured because it lies deep, behind and above the axillary vein.
- If injured, must repair.

### Complications associated with SLNB (p.312)
- Compared with ALND, SLNB has fewer complications such as seroma, lymphedema, numbness, pain.
- Not to be used in pregnancy [as printed]; the surgeon should have a learning curve of about 30 cases.
- Beware at injection: blue dye has 2% allergic reaction, some with shock; stop and stop the case first; if a lumpectomy was planned, change to MRM.
- (The author adds that if an allergic reaction occurs and the patient is stable, some surgeons continue; other remarks there are non-medical.)

### Frozen shoulder (p.313)
- Uncommon, but if it occurs quality of life is poor.
- Treatment: physiotherapy.
- Prevention: early mobilization when wound pain has settled, about day 3-5 after surgery, start PT.

### Postmastectomy exercises (p.313)
- Figure: 4 exercises from a textbook: 1. hair dressing: combing hair yourself (not others doing it); 2. wall climbing (spiderman): climb fingers up the wall as high as possible, marking maximum height; 3. rope turning: tie a rope to a door knob (about 5 feet) and turn in a circle; 4. pulley motion: rope over a door/high bar, hold both ends and pull up and down with both arms.
- Local alternative: Thai traditional exercises (rhythmic, e.g. tai chi, bamboo-type); the author thinks they seem to work better.

## Points to remember before finishing (book p.314)
1. Risk factors for breast cancer: many are only theoretical; some do not add risk, e.g. breast size, smoking.
2. Triple assessment = CBE, imaging (mammogram, US), histopathology (CNB); all 3 tests must be done together; do not do mastectomy because CNB is positive alone; if CNB is unclear do open biopsy.
3. Mammogram:
   3.1 Sending a mammogram in age <35 may lead to wrong answers, since young age = low risk, dense breast with much glandular tissue is hard to read; send US instead.
   3.2 If a mammogram is needed in age <35, it is a case of real suspicion or concern, e.g. family history of cancer, especially young-onset.
   3.3 BI-RADS 3: follow up with mammogram every 6 months 4 times; if still unchanged, follow up yearly; but if the exam or palpable mass makes the surgeon uneasy, do core biopsy even if BI-RADS 3.
   3.4 Compare films of both sides side by side, not one film at a time; look at both CC and MLO views.
   3.5 Add US to tell whether the mammographic mass is cystic or solid.
4. Role of MRI: 4.1 axillary lymph node positive, unknown primary, and problematic mammogram; 4.2 rupture of silicone implant.
5. LCIS is not cancer but only a marker of high risk of breast cancer; risk of cancer in both breasts equally; the subsequent cancer is ductal cancer, not lobular cancer.
6. Subsequent cancer of DCIS is usually on the same side and the same quadrant; if pathology of DCIS shows nuclear grade high with necrosis or comedo, it is very severe, so mastectomy may be done.
7. Currently BCS is the main treatment, MRM the second choice; for oncoplastic with TRAM flap, may do immediate or delayed; if no pre-op mammogram, never do BCS.
8. Halsted radical mastectomy (removes pectoralis major and minor with ALND 1, 2, 3) is out of date; remember it for history. If thinking of BCS, avoid excisional biopsy since the skin at the biopsy site must be removed too, making more excision to get free margin.
9. SLN: if axillary nodes are palpable, do FNA instead and do not do SLN (even if FNA is -ve, as above).
10. Previous therapeutic RT to the chest wall of the cancer side: do not give RT again.
11. Clinical features to separate Paget's disease from eczema: Paget is usually unilateral, the lesion starts at the nipple first then spreads to the areola, the wound does not heal, sharp margin, little itching, and a mass may be palpable; eczema is usually bilateral, comes and goes, very itchy, no mass, margin not clear, and the lesion usually starts at the areola before the nipple.
