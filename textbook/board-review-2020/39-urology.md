## Introduction
- This is roughly the urology a general surgery resident should know before the board; remembering it should get you > 80% of the questions.
- Pay special attention to KUB tumors. KUB injury, including injury to the penis, testis and urethra, is summarized in Trauma 2018. Stone disease is not written up (as printed: "too boring").

## Basic Anatomy
- Only what has appeared or may appear in MCQ.
- **Renal artery**
  1. Arises from the aorta at L2
  2. Right renal artery lies behind the IVC and renal vein
- **Renal vein**
  1. Lt. renal vein passes in front of the aorta
  2. Lt. renal vein has many collaterals: gonadal vein, adrenal vein and lumbar vein
  3. So it can be ligated, but the tie must be close to the IVC
- **Ureter**
  1. Crosses the iliac vessels at the pelvic brim near the iliac bifurcation
  2. Blood supply of the ureter:
     - 2.1 Proximal ureter: branches of the aorta and renal artery
     - 2.2 Distal ureter: iliac artery
- **Bladder**
  1. Lies in the retropubic space — extraperitoneal portion
  2. No intraperitoneal portion (as printed)
  3. Arterial supply from branches of the internal iliac artery:
     - 3.1 Superior vesical artery
     - 3.2 Inferior vesical artery
     - 3.3 Obturator artery
     - 3.4 Uterine artery
  4. Bladder transitional epithelium has ≤ 7 cell layers; when the bladder is full, it thins to about 3 layers.
- **Prostate gland**
  - Located posterior to the pubic symphysis, superior to the perineal membrane, inferior to the bladder, and anterior to the rectum.
  - Normal prostate ≈ 20 g in volume, 3 cm long, 4 cm wide, 2 cm deep.
  - Size varies with age, secondary to benign prostatic hyperplasia.
- **Testis** — paired, wrapped in 2 layers: inner tough fibrous tunica albuginea, outer tunica vaginalis. Seminiferous tubules contain cells that make sperm, which leave via the epididymis to the vas deferens.
  1. **Blood supply** — mainly:
     - 1.1 Testicular (internal spermatic) artery — branch of the aorta just below the renal artery
     - 1.2 Deferential artery — branch of the superior vesicular artery (as printed)
     - 1.3 Collaterals from the cremasteric (external spermatic) artery, a branch of the inferior epigastric artery, plus vasal and epididymal arteries — so even if the testicular artery is ligated, the testis does not necrose
  2. Remember: no blood supply comes from the arteries supplying the scrotum.
  3. Lt. spermatic vein drains into the Lt. renal vein; Rt. spermatic vein drains directly into the IVC.

## Hematuria
- Visible red urine = gross hematuria; found only on microscopy = microhematuria. Abnormal when urine RBC > 5 cells/HPF.
- Hematuria from medical (renal) disease usually has casts and much protein; surgical disease leaks little protein.
- Timing of blood in the stream helps localize disease in the KUB system:
  1. **Initial hematuria** (start of stream) → urethra, usually urethritis
  2. **Terminal hematuria** (end of stream) → around the bladder neck, prostate and prostatic urethra
  3. **Total hematuria** (throughout) → usually upper tract or bladder; beware cancer

## Acute Urinary Retention
- Elderly > 50 years: No. 1 cause is BPH; prostatic cancer is No. 3.
- Young adults: No. 1 is urethral stricture.
- Treatment: urgent bladder decompression.
  1. Foley urethral catheter first; in BPH it may be hard → use a coudé curved catheter (printed "corde") to pass the angulation of the prostatic urethra.
  2. If 1 fails → suprapubic cystostomy.
- Suprapubic catheterization (from figure text): done under local anesthesia, with steady aspiration until urine is retrieved; a trocar-type suprapubic tube is passed through a 1 cm skin incision, the catheter advanced over the trocar and sutured in place.

## Urethral Stricture
- Common in young men presenting with AUR; causes: infection (sexually transmitted disease), trauma, or catheterization.
- Treatment: dilate; if that fails → urethroplasty.

## Urinary Incontinence
- Involuntary leakage of urine — dribbling or wetting without intent.
- **Continuous incontinence**: leaks all the time, day and night; may be from a fistula, e.g. vesico-vaginal fistula, uretero-vaginal fistula.
- **Stress incontinence**: leaks on coughing, sneezing, laughing, straining, or any rise in intra-abdominal pressure, without bladder contraction. Common in women of reproductive age with many children — everything lax (loss of anterior vaginal support); in men usually after prostate surgery.
- **Urgency incontinence**: urge to void but cannot reach the toilet in time and leaks first; usually from cystitis, bladder contracting too easily (detrusor overactivity), or neurogenic bladder.
- **Overflow (paradoxical) incontinence**: dribbling after urinary retention until the bladder is full and urine leaks out.
- General treatment steps — 4:
  1. Rule out transient causes, e.g. drugs
  2. Record leakage in a bladder diary
  3. Directed history and physical examination
  4. Make a diagnosis
- History: leakage with cough, sneeze, straining, or continuous. Exam in women includes pelvic exam for laxity of pelvic organs; neuro exam focused on S2-4 — perineal sensation, digital rectal examination for sphincter tone. Then investigations: U/A, FBS, electrolytes.
- Slide points: history — night-time (better or worse?), volume, stress, urgency, bladder diary; physical — neuro exam, mental status, mobility; men: prostate, AUA; women: pad test, bimanual with "Kegel test".
- Voiding diary: how many voids per day, volumes, fluid intake, over 24 h for at least 3 consecutive days.
- Further full-option investigations: urine examination, cystoscopy; urodynamics only when full treatment has failed.
- Treatment (not emphasized): start with bladder training — timed voiding practice, adjust fluid intake, stop smoking, cut coffee — then drugs; which drug is not needed for general surgeons.
- Figure: Incontinence management guideline, as steps —
  1. History/symptom assessment: incontinence on physical activity; with mixed symptoms; or with urgency/frequency → clinical assessment: assess pelvic organ mobility/prolapse, consider imaging of UT/pelvic floor, urodynamics.
     - "Complicated" incontinence (recurrent incontinence; incontinence with pain, hematuria, recurrent infection, voiding symptoms, pelvic irradiation, radical pelvic surgery, suspected fistula) → consider urethrocystoscopy, further imaging, urodynamics → lower urinary tract anomaly/pathology → correct anomaly, treat pathology.
  2. Diagnosis and treatment:
     - Urodynamic stress incontinence (USI) → if initial therapy fails: stress incontinence surgery (injections, low tension slings, colposuspension, AUS).
     - Mixed incontinence (USI/DOI) → either pathway.
     - Detrusor overactivity incontinence (DOI) → if initial therapy fails: neuromodulation, bladder augmentation, urinary diversion.
     - Incontinence with poor bladder emptying: bladder outlet obstruction → correct anatomic bladder outlet obstruction (e.g. iatrogenic), intermittent catheterization; underactive detrusor → intermittent catheterisation.

## Neurogenic Bladder
- Inability to hold or to pass urine due to the nerves controlling voiding, anywhere from the brain down to sacrum 2-4.
- General surgeons need only a little on peripheral nerves:
  1. Parasympathetic (via sacral plexus): contracts the bladder while bladder neck and prostatic urethra relax
  2. Sympathetic (via lumbar plexus): relaxes bladder muscle while bladder neck and prostatic urethra contract
  3. Somatic (via pudendal nerve): contracts and relaxes the external urinary sphincter
- **Symptoms**: mostly frequency, especially at night, and urge leakage; fewer present with retention; may have UTI.
- **Investigation**: urodynamic study is common — the patient voids while the doctor watches (as printed, likened to defecography): uroflowmetry, and pressure in filling cystometry and during voiding.
- **Treatment**
  1. Low muscle tone: parasympathomimetic, e.g. betanechal chloride (as printed) to stimulate contraction; or intravesical electrical stimulation, intermittent catheterization
  2. High tone or spastic: alpha-blocker, botox injection at the urethra with catheterization, or TUR to cut the bladder neck

## Vesicoureteral Reflux (VUR)
- Urine flows back from the bladder up the ureter → chronic UTI, renal scarring, eventually chronic renal failure. Common in children: poor growth, frequency, low weight, chronic cystitis.
- In boys, one cause is posterior urethral valve.
- **Symptoms**: UTI, poor growth, low weight.
- Urine exam shows infection.
- Next: US shows hydronephrosis and hydroureter.
- Then voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) under fluoroscopy — the standard test: Foley into the bladder, contrast in as for cystography, remove Foley, film while voiding to look for posterior urethral valve and reflux on straining.
  - Low grade: reflux up to ureter and renal pelvis, mild dilatation of ureter and pelvis.
  - High grade: reflux to ureter and pelvis with marked dilatation, tortuous ureter and thinned renal parenchyma.
- After VCUG, DMSA renal scan (technetium 99m dimercaptosuccinic acid, taken up by renal cortex) to look for renal scarring and compare function of both kidneys.
- **Treatment**
  1. Low grade: observe, check urine, treat UTI, follow-up US; if abnormal, repeat VCUG
  2. Surgery for high grade, frequent infection, or worsening kidney; several operations, e.g. ureter reimplantation

## Phimosis
- Foreskin too tight to retract for cleaning. Normal at birth; by about 3-5 years, as the child plays with it or reaches adolescence, the glans emerges by itself (as printed, joke wording). Secondary phimosis: the foreskin hardens until it cannot open, usually from chronic skin inflammation — balanitis xerotica obliterans.
- **Indication for circumcision**
  1. Infected foreskin (posthitis)
  2. Inflamed glans (balanitis)
  3. Difficult voiding, frequent UTI, or high grade VUR
  4. Balanitis xerotica obliterans — foreskin too hard to retract
  5. Age > 5 years and the glans still not exposed
  6. Religious reasons
- Do not circumcise if the urethral opening is low (hypospadias), because the foreskin is needed for urethral reconstruction.
- No indication for circumcision → try steroid cream on the foreskin; it reduces inflammation and loosens it.

## Paraphimosis
- Tight foreskin retracted behind the coronal sulcus and cannot be pulled back; it constricts the glans → venous and lymphatic congestion → very swollen foreskin, severe pain, retention, infection and ulcer.
- Residents who play with the foreskin should remember to pull it back, or end up in the ER embarrassed in front of the whole hospital (as printed, a joke).
- **Treatment**: urgent manual reduction using lubricant jelly ("K-Y or xylocaine jelly" as printed); if it fails → dorsal slit, but not circumcision, since the wound is inflamed and easily infected.

## Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)
- **Uncomplicated UTI**: infection without complications in otherwise healthy people, especially women (short urethra); oral antibiotic for 3 days is enough.
- **Complicated UTI**: in children, elderly, men, pregnant women, diabetics, HIV, or indwelling Foley; needs antibiotic, may need IV for 10-14 days.
- **Unresolved UTI**: resistant organism, no response to the antibiotic given.
- **Recurrent UTI**: cured, then infected again.
- **Reinfected UTI**: new infection with a new organism.
- **UTI in pregnancy** must be treated, or the mother gets sepsis and the baby premature labor. Safe drugs: penicillin and cephalosporin; if allergic, nitrofurantoin instead. Avoid: quinolone, trimethoprim, tetra, chloram, erythromycin.
- Nitrofurantoin does not alter gut flora.

## Pyelonephritis
- Acute: high fever, chills, flank pain, frequency, dysuria; chronic is often asymptomatic.
- Pregnant women have a higher risk of pyelonephritis than non-pregnant women.
- Uncomplicated acute pyelonephritis: OPD case, oral quinolone about 10-14 days.
- Complicated acute pyelonephritis: admit, IV quinolone 10-14 days.
- **Emphysematous pyelonephritis**: gas-forming bacteria — No. 1 E.coli (70%), then Klebsiella, Proteus; in debilitated diabetics. Gas entering the collecting system → pneumaturia. CT: gas in and around the kidney (mottled gas shadow). Nephrectomy chance 30%; mortality 65%.
  - Slide captions: abdominal radiograph — gas throughout right kidney; CT — extensive destruction of renal parenchyma with associated gas, gas in retroperitoneal tissues.
  - **Treatment**
    1. Kidney still has some function → IV ATB plus percutaneous drainage first
    2. Nephrectomy if no improvement after 2-3 days of drugs, or the kidney no longer works

## Cystitis
- Uncomplicated cystitis: spontaneous bacterial colonization; common in healthy women who hold urine. Treatment: ATB about 3 days.
- Complicated cystitis: in children, men, elderly, pregnant women and compromised hosts. Treatment: ATB 10-14 days.

## GC Urethritis
- Caused by N. Gonorrhea.
- Urethritis in males; cervicitis and PID in females. GS of urethral discharge shows PMNs with intracellular gram-negative diplococci.
- **Treatment**: GC — ceftriazone (as printed) 250 mg IM single injection, plus doxycycline 7-14 days for non-specific urethritis from chlamydia, which almost always coexists. Do not forget to treat the partner (กิ๊ก) too.

## Acute Suppurative Prostatitis
- Organisms ascend from the urethra into the prostate → fever, dysuria, sometimes hematuria; hot, swollen prostate; touching it gently on PR is extremely painful.
- Absolutely no prostatic massage → bacteremia.
- **Treatment**
  1. IV antibiotic — quinolone first (penetrates prostate well), then adjust to culture
  2. Avoid catheter urine sampling and prostatic massage
  3. If retention needs bladder decompression, avoid retained Foley cath; do SP-cystostomy

## Acute Epididymitis & Orchitis
- Usually GC or chlamydia trachomatis ascending from the urethra, sexually transmitted. Inflammation starts in the epididymis and spreads to the testis → orchitis.
- Scrotum swollen, red, painful; hard to tell epididymitis from orchitis.
- **Treatment**: cover GC and NGC — ceftriaxone 250 mg IM plus oral doxycycline (100 mg) 1 tab bid for 10 days.

## Fournier Gangrene
- Necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum and genitalia from polymicrobial aerobic and anaerobic bacteria; spreads fast with necrosis of skin, subcutaneous tissue, fascia. Usually in debilitated diabetics, alcoholics, cirrhotics.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Systemic sepsis: high fever, delirium (change in mental status)
  2. Scrotal pain, subcutaneous crepitus, foul-smelling necrotic tissue
  3. Swollen, red, hot scrotum, but little scrotal discharge
- **Diagnosis**: clinical.
- **Treatment**
  1. Urgent wide debridement — cut away all necrotic scrotum. The testes can be kept because their separate blood supply comes from aortic branches and they do not necrose; bury them in the thigh (thigh pouch).
  2. IV broad spectrum antibiotics & fluid resuscitation
  3. Heavy fecal contamination of the perineum → colostomy

## Urolithiasis
- **Upper tract stone**: renal stone, ureteric stone.
- **Lower tract stone**: lower ureteric, bladder, urethral, prostatic stone.
- Focus is on ureteric stone, asked more than other stones.
- **Composition**
  1. Calcium oxate (as printed) stone — most common 75%, radiopaque. Common cause: terminal ileal resection → fat not bound by bile acid reaches the colon and binds calcium, leaving less calcium to bind oxalate → more oxalate absorbed from the colon.
  2. Calcium phosphate stone — next most common
  3. Uric acid stone — radiolucent; dissolves in alkaline urine
  4. Cysteine (as printed) stone — reduced reabsorption of amino acids, especially cysteine, raises urinary cysteine; hard stone, ESWL rarely breaks it
  5. Magnesium ammonium phosphate (struvite) stone — related to urinary tract infection, especially urease-producing proteus mirabilis; forms staghorn stone filling the pelvis
- **Symptoms**: colicky pain, costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness, hematuria, infection; some asymptomatic, found incidentally on x-ray.
- **Investigation**
  1. Imaging: CT is best now — non-contrast; diagnoses all stone types and gives stone density and skin-to-stone distance to judge suitability for lithotripsy
  2. US: initial study for pregnant patients with suspected stone
  3. IVP: now a functional study for level of obstruction
  4. Plain KUB: easy, cheap; misses radiolucent stones, e.g. uric, xanthine
- Finding a kidney or ureter stone is not itself an indication for surgery or intervention — usually start with medical treatment.
- **Management**
  1. **Medical treatment**: analgesic NSAIDs, alpha-blockers, plenty of fluids ("antibiotic" crossed out by hand on the page). Uric stone: oral alkali to alkalinize urine and dissolve stone, keep urine pH about 6.5-7.2; urine pH > 7.5 (too alkaline) → calcium phosphate stones.
  2. Ureteric stone (UC) — chance of passage:
     - 2.1 ≤ 3 mm: be patient, 90% pass
     - 2.2 ≤ 5 mm: 50% pass
     - 2.3 6-10 mm: 20% pass
     - 2.4 > 10 mm: 10% pass
     - 2.5 1-2 cm: hard to pass, usually needs ESWL
     - 2.6 > 2 cm: usually needs surgery
     - 2.7 Staghorn stone (antler-shaped): usually needs surgery
  3. **Treatment for removal stone** — several methods:
     - 3.1 Open — now done less; laparoscopic surgery preferred
     - 3.2 Percutaneous nephrolithotomy — renal stone > 2 cm or staghorn stone
     - 3.3 Ureteroscopic manipulation — basket the stone out from below
     - 3.4 ESWL
- **Indication for removal stone**
  1. Persistent or progressive obstruction
  2. Intractable pain or infection
  3. Impaired renal function or progressive renal damage
  4. Solitary kidney
- **ESWL** — before choosing it, know:
  1. No bleeding disorder and no active UTI
  2. Non-pregnant patient
  3. Suits stones above the pelvic brim; low stones do poorly. Renal stone ≤ 2 cm; ureteric stone ≤ 1 cm.
  4. Skin-to-stone distance ≤ 10 cm; very obese patients are unsuitable
  5. No urinary obstruction (lower pole stones pass with difficulty)
  6. High-density stones (> 1,000 HU) are hard and fragment poorly:
     - 6.1 Cystine stone
     - 6.2 Calcium hydrogen phosphate stone (brushite stone)
     - 6.3 Calcium oxalate monohydrate (not dihydrate) stone
- **Ureter stone with infection** (fever)
  1. Urgent admit, hydration, urine culture, IV antibiotic
  2. Worsening, or fever on and off → urgent drainage of infected urine:
     - 2.1 Start with percutaneous nephrostomy under CT guide
     - 2.2 If 2.1 fails → cystoscopy and pass a ureter stent past the obstruction
     - 2.3 Treat the stone electively

## Renal Tumors
- Benign tumors to know (no need to study deeply): 3, plus 1 more important one — AML.
- **Renal cyst**: most common benign renal lesion, mostly benign. Typical: thin smooth wall, no nodule, no calcification, clear fluid; treat only if symptomatic (usually pressure symptoms). A few have thick walls, nodules, calcification or turbid fluid — operate, as they may be malignant.
- **Renal adenoma**: 7%; treat by surgery since it is hard to tell from RCC, especially > 3 cm.
- **Oncytoma** (as printed): 3%; CT shows central scar; treat by surgery since it cannot be told from RCC.
- Mesenchymal tumors, e.g. hemangioma, fibroma, lipoma: very rare.

## Angiomyolipoma (AML)
- Benign tumor of blood vessel, smooth muscle and adipocyte.
- May be bilateral; rare reports of turning into RCC.
- Associated with tuberous sclerosis in 20% (brain involvement → seizures).
- CT: fat density < -20 HU and no calcification in the mass.
- **Treatment**
  1. Asymptomatic, < 4 cm → observation & follow up with US every 6-12 months
  2. Hematuria → embolization
  3. Flank pain, > 4 cm, fast growing, or with tuberous sclerosis → nephron sparing procedure, e.g. upper pole nephrectomy if in the upper pole
  4. Indication for nephrectomy:
     - 4.1 Multifocal
     - 4.2 Large tumor where nephron sparing is not possible
     - 4.3 Traumatic rupture

## Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC)
- M:F about 2:1; usually age 50-70.
- 50% found incidentally on imaging, with better prognosis than symptomatic cases. A vascular renal mass has an 80% chance of RCC; most are solid, cystic lesions are uncommon.
- Accepted risk factor: smoking.
- Mostly sporadic; hereditary is uncommon.
- Among hereditary forms, RCC occurs in Von Hippel Lindau syndrome (VHL), which also has pheochromocytoma, pancreatic cystic tumor and CNS tumor (hemangioblastoma); 2-3 other hereditary syndromes with RCC are very rare.
- Histologic subtype with worst prognosis: unclassified.
- **Classic triad of RCC** (5 items listed, as printed)
  1. Hematuria 60% — usually a late symptom, as the tumor has been there long enough to invade the renal pelvis
  2. Abdominal mass 10% — usually > 5 cm
  3. Back pain
  4. 20-30% present with metastatic disease
  5. Cancer symptoms: fever, weight loss, anorexia, pallor
- Paraneoplastic syndrome in 20%, from hormone-like substances:
  1. Hypercalcemia — PTH-like substance
  2. Erythrocytosis — tumor erythropoietin
  3. ACTH & insulin release
  4. Non-metastatic hepatic dysfunction or Stauffer's syndrome — high alkaline phosphatase, low albumin, prolonged prothrombin time
- RCC is uncommon in the population, so screening is not recommended.
- **Metastasis**: lung No. 1, then bone and liver; often presents as pathological fracture from a lytic (not blastic) lesion.
- **Imaging study**: CT. MRI is not better than CT except to look for renal vein or IVC invasion — MRI shows intraluminal tumor thrombus in the IVC, which may extend to the right atrium (IVC distended but not filled with contrast) and cause dyspnea, syncope or arrhythmias.
- FNA or core cut is not needed, as most renal masses (especially enhancing ones) are malignant — treat as cancer; biopsy risks bleeding and tumor seeding.
- FNA when suspecting:
  1. Renal metastasis, or unresectable RCC needing tissue for chemo
  2. Lymphoma
  3. Renal abscess or infected cyst
- Prognosis depends on tumor stage, especially lymph node metastasis.
- **Management of RCC**
  1. Surgery is the best treatment; chemo and RT do little; RFA works poorly because the kidney has urine flow.
  2. **Nephron sparing**, e.g. partial nephrectomy, indications:
     - 2.1 Size < 4 cm
     - 2.2 Solitary remaining kidney
     - 2.3 Bilateral RCC, often in VHL
     - 2.4 Peripheral location, not at the hilum, no renal capsule invasion, no lymph node spread
     - 2.5 Free margin > 3 mm achievable
  3. **Open radical nephrectomy** — best, standard treatment; when unsure, answer radical (usually correct). Suits tumors > 4 cm still confined within Gerota's fascia. Laparoscopic surgery suits small tumors without local invasion or renal vein spread, with results comparable to open.
     - Radical nephregtomy (as printed) procedure:
       1. Nephrectomy
       2. Adrenalectomy
       3. Removal of perinephric fat, Gerota's fascia and proximal ureter
       4. Regional lymphadenectomy (hilar lymph node)
  4. Tumor thrombus in the IVC is not a contraindication; do radical nephrectomy with complete removal of IVC tumor thrombus.
  5. Very large RCC → embolization to shrink it before surgery.
  6. Metastasis:
     - 6.1 Isolated metastasis, e.g. single lung lesion → lung resection
     - 6.2 Multiple metastasis → may try interferon or chemo such as bevacizumab
  7. Cancer in the renal pelvis is usually transitional cell → remove the whole ureter: radical nephroureterectomy with resection of bladder cuff at the ureteral insertion site.

## Renal Pelvis or Ureter Carcinoma
- Transitional cell carcinoma > 90%, from the transitional epithelial layer. Squamous about 5%, usually in patients with stones or chronic pyelonephritis.
- Symptoms: hematuria 80%, flank pain 20%, weakness, weight loss.
- Age about 70; men more than women; 60% already invasive at diagnosis.
- Risk factors as for bladder cancer; an odd one is the herb aristolochia fangchi, which raises cancer risk.
- IVP: radiolucent intraluminal filling defect.
- CT urography now gives the most information: shows the mass, spread to lymph nodes, and distinguishes tumor from stone.
- Ureteropyeloscopy helps diagnosis and treatment of early cancer.
- **Treatment**: radical or total nephroureterectomy + excision of the bladder cuff around the ureteric orifice.

## Bladder Cancer
- **Risk factor**
  1. Aromatic amines in cigarettes — highest risk; male sex, white skin
  2. Carcinogens from car exhaust or industrial solvents in rubber, leather tanning, textile and printing industries raise risk of TCC
  3. Prior RT or chemotherapy
  4. Chronic irritation from Foley catheter, bladder stone, and the parasite schistosomiasis raise risk of SCC
  5. Urachal remnants or bladder exstrophy raise risk of adeno
- **Cell type**
  1. 90% transitional cell carcinoma (TCC)
  2. The rest squamous cell (SCC) and adenocarcinoma
- **Symptoms**: painless hematuria 90%; when cancer invades muscle, bladder irritability — dysuria, frequency, painful voiding — in 20%.
- Diagnosis: cystoscopy with biopsy; cytology has low sensitivity (31-70%).
- By pathology, 2 main groups:
  1. Superficial cancer — invasion not reaching muscle
  2. Invasive cancer — invasion into muscle
- Contrast enhanced CT is the best workup to assess depth of penetration of bladder cancer.
- **Treatment** based on stage and grade:
  1. Confined to mucosa (metastasis very rare) → TUR-BT (one best), or with intra-vesical BCG or chemo; if only one choice, answer TUR-BT
  2. Confined to submucosa, or recurrent tumor → TUR-BT + intravesical BCG or TUR-BT + intravesical chemo (either); wait 2 weeks after TUR before intravesical therapy so the bladder wound heals
  3. Invading muscle = invasive cancer → radical cystectomy + ileal conduit + chemo
  4. Metastatic disease → chemo + palliative treatment, e.g. urinary diversion

## Benign Prostatic Hypertrophy (BPH)
- The prostate makes fluid that nourishes sperm; it surrounds the urethra.
- Prostate cells contain 5-alpha reductase, which converts testosterone (from the testis) to DHT; DHT stimulates prostate cells to divide faster → hypertrophy.
- The prostate also has smooth muscle controlled by alpha-1 adrenergic receptors; contraction squeezes the prostatic urethra → retention.
- Men > 50 years: BPH 50%; > 80 years: BPH 80%. So those awaiting TUR who then cannot void — do not let a (female) resident put in the Foley (as printed, joke wording).
- BPH arises from the transitional zone of the prostate.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Early symptom: diminished urinary stream
  2. Later: nocturia, frequency, dysuria and urinary retention
- BPH is one of the diseases under lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS); patients with voiding difficulty are first given a working diagnosis of LUTS.
- **Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)**: symptoms of the lower urinary tract from bladder or urethral disease. 3 groups:
  1. **Storage symptoms** — before voiding: frequency, incontinence, nocturia, urgency
  2. **Voiding symptoms** — during voiding: slow stream, intermittency, hesitancy, straining, terminal dribble
  3. **Post-micturition symptoms** — after voiding: feeling of incomplete emptying, post-micturition dribble
- LUTS severity graded with AUA or IPSS score: 7 questions, each scored 0-5 (as printed: "mild symptom 1 point, severe 5 points"), total 35.

| AUA Symptom Score — over the past month | Not at all | < 1 time in 5 | < half the time | About half | > half the time | Almost always |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Sensation of not emptying the bladder completely after urinating | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 2. Had to urinate again < 2 hours after finishing | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 3. Stopped and started again several times when urinating | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 4. Difficult to postpone urination | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 5. Weak urinary stream | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 6. Had to push or strain to begin urination | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 7. Times typically got up at night to urinate (none / 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 or more) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |

- Score 0-7 mild; 8-19 moderate; 20-35 severe.
- **Investigations**: always start with urine exam to screen for infection causing frequency/difficult voiding, plus PSA.
- Figure: LUTS causes (slide) — overactive bladder/detrusor overactivity, benign prostatic obstruction, nocturnal polyuria, underactive bladder/detrusor underactivity, chronic pelvic pain syndrome, neurogenic bladder dysfunction, urinary tract infection/inflammation, foreign body, urethral stricture, bladder tumour, distal ureteric stone, others.
- **Management** by symptom severity:
  1. **Watchful** — mild LUTS score < 7 not disturbing daily life: advice, e.g. less fluid before bed, stop alcohol, beer, caffeine; periodic follow-up.
  2. Symptomatic → **Initial Rx** with drugs:
     - 2.1 **Alpha blocker**, e.g. terazosin or doxazosin — relaxes prostate smooth muscle, less urethral compression; within 24 h the muscle relaxes and patients often void. Side effect (also muscle relaxing): hypotension, dizziness.
     - 2.2 **5-alpha reductase inhibitor** — suppresses androgen: finasteride (5 mg) once daily; blocks testosterone → dihydrotestosterone (DHT) in prostate cells, stopping growth; prostate shrinks 20-30%, usually takes > 3 months.
  3. **Surgical treatment**: transurethral resection of the prostate (TUR-P) under spinal. Laser, microwave or RFA needle — general surgeons need not know.
     - Indication for TUR-P:
       - 3.1 Recurrent UTI
       - 3.2 Recurrent gross hematuria
       - 3.3 Worsening renal function
       - 3.4 Failure medical treatment
       - 3.5 Presence of bladder stone
- After TUR-P some prostate tissue remains, so prostatic cancer can still occur.
- **Post TUR syndrome**: TUR needs continuous irrigation to prevent clot. Hypotonic irrigating solution is absorbed → volume overload and hyponatremia → brain swelling: headache, slow HR, rising BP, then seizure. Rx: Lasix plus IV fluid as NSS or hypertonic saline.
- Laser, microwave, RFA via the urethra — not needed for general surgeons.

## Prostate Cancer
- 95% adenocarcinoma from acinar structure. No link between prostate cancer and BPH is currently found; thought to be a DNA growth abnormality — cells divide well and die poorly.
- Usually starts in the peripheral zone at the posterior lobe — most common site (70%); transitional zone around the urethra only 20%. So early cancer is usually silent until urinary obstruction or hematuria brings the patient in.
- Best screening: digital rectal examination (DRE) + serum PSA.
- DRE finds a hard, irregular prostatic nodule, or PSA > 4 and not falling or rising → transrectal US guided prostate biopsy (TRUS). If cancer: staging with CT pelvis or MRI (beyond capsule, nodes, adjacent organs) and bone scan for bone spread.
- Prostate cancer likes bone; film shows osteoblastic (hyperdensity) lesions.
- PSA is organ specific for prostate, but can rise in benign conditions such as prostatitis, trauma, or Foley insertion. Check PSA from age 50 yearly.
- PSA after redical (as printed) prostatectomy should normalize within 6 weeks; persistently high post-op PSA suggests residual tumor.
- Biopsy is now done when PSA is high or keeps rising on follow-up: biopsy the hard area or nodule plus random biopsy of the peripheral zone, 5 cores left and 5 right.
- Besides TNM staging, cell characteristics by Gleason score matter for prognosis.
- **Gleason score** — the pathologist's grade of aggressiveness:
  1. Score 2-6: well diff (low grade cancer)
  2. Score 7: mod. diff (intermediate grade cancer)
  3. Score 8-10: poorly diff (high grade cancer); poorly diff usually needs PO.RT
- **Management**
  1. **Localized cancer** — confined to the prostate, spread not beyond seminal vesicles. Rx 3 options: radical prostatectomy, 3D-RT, or interstitial implanted RT, decided by urologist and patient; one best answer = surgery.
     - 1.1 Radical prostatectomy — aggressive, curative; for tumor in both lobes, Gleason score ≥ 7, or PSA > 10; removes prostate and seminal vesicles. Post-op problems: incontinence 20% (Schwartz 10th says in expert hands < 1%) and impotence 25%.
     - 1.2 RT — for poor surgical candidates or +ve margin after surgery.
  2. **Locally advanced cancer** (beyond capsule, or N+ve but M0) → radical prostatectomy + pelvic LND + PO.RT
  3. **Metastatic disease** → hormonal ablation with LHRH agonist (testosterone blocking agent) or bilateral orchidectomy; hormone Rx reduces bone pain and obstructive uropathy.
- Chemo does little for prostate cancer.
- Stage 1A found after TUR-P is a very early case — nothing more needed.
- Watchful waiting: very elderly patients with many comorbidities and no symptoms — just follow up.

## Penile Cancer
- Risk factors: phimosis without circumcision → smegma build-up → chronic inflammation of the glans; or circumcision after age 14 (late doesn't help); HPV infection; smoking.
- Beware a chronic ulcer on the glans penis — not very painful, foul smelling, or not healing after treatment as fungal infection — may be cancer; biopsy.
- Spreads to groin nodes; palpable node → FNA. Sentinel lymph node biopsy (borrowed from breast cancer) is now used, but SLNB is still under research with false negative 10-15%. Groin node positive → CT abdomen/pelvis.
- **Treatment**
  1. Penectomy with 2 cm margin; node positive but ≤ 4 cm → bilateral ilioinguinal dissection; node > 4 cm → chemotherapy recommended
  2. RT usually needs a high dose
  3. Chemo for advanced disease

## Primary Testicular Tumor
- Mostly primary; a few secondary from prostate, lung, GI, melanoma.
- Primary testicular tumors are the most common urological cancer in men aged 15-34.
- 2 main types:
  1. **Germ cell tumor** 95% — seminoma and non-seminoma
  2. **Non germ cell tumor** 5% — sex cord stromal tumors, e.g. sertoli and leydic (as printed) cell tumor
- Germ cell tumors: seminoma 35%, non-seminoma 65%.
- Non-seminoma: mixed cell type most common, then embryonal cell carcinoma, choliocarcinoma (as printed), teratoma, yolk sac tumor.
- Testicular cancer spreads to nodes, except choriocarcinoma, which spreads by blood.
- **Symptoms**: mostly painless hard scrotal mass — a young man with a painless testicular mass is tumor until proven otherwise.
- **Cautions** (ข้อควรระวัง)
  1. Some present with gynecomastia — usually leydig or setoli (as printed) cell tumor, age about 20-35, with breasts still enlarged beyond physiologic change; palpate the testes.
  2. Scrotal swelling, e.g. acute hydrocele, where the testis cannot be felt → US to see the testis clearly.
  3. Cryptorchism should be operated before age 2; untreated, seminoma risk at age 10-35 is 20 times normal.
  4. Some present with metastases, e.g. palpable nodes, lung or bone symptoms.
- **Approach to scrotal mass**: ask about trauma (kicked, squeezed); trauma → suspect hematoma or ruptured testis. Without trauma, ask whether it hurts: painful → hernia, torsion, infection, tumor; painless → usually hydrocele.
- Palpate the testis gently; squeezing hard may get you punched by the patient. Warm your hands first, or the testis retracts and cannot be felt (as printed, joke wording).
- History, exam and US suggest testicular mass → tumor markers AFP and beta HCG, then CT chest, abdomen, pelvis for spread, then OR for radical orchidectomy.
- Lactic acid dehydrogenase (LDH), a cellular enzyme, correlates with tumor volume; higher LDH is worse; raised in both seminoma and non-seminoma.
- **Radical orchidectomy**
  1. Inguinal incision (จำ) — never cut through the scrotum
  2. Early clamping of the spermatic cord, then remove the testis
  3. Await pathology
- Again: never do scrotal biopsy before definitive surgery — cancer spreads via the skin to the groin (as printed: "then you are in trouble").
- If pathology shows cancer → CT chest, abdomen and pelvis for staging, focusing on retroperitoneal lymph nodes from the common iliac vessels up to the renal vein, where testicular cancer spreads.

## Seminoma
- Most common testicular cancer; age about 30-40.
- **Tumor markers not raised** — only a few (10%) raise them; a case with raised tumor markers → think non-seminoma first.
- **Treatment** — radiosensitive tumor:
  1. Confined to the testis or only regional LN → radical orchidectomy then postop. RT
  2. Retroperitoneal node +ve or lung met. → chemotherapy; retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (RPLND) not needed
  3. Residual disease after RT or chemo → resect

## Non-Seminoma
- Several types:
  1. Embryonal cell — usually in children (as printed)
  2. Yolk sac — infancy and childhood
  3. Choriocarcinoma
  4. Teratoma — usually in children
- Tumor markers (AFP and beta HCG) raised (จำ).
- Spreads to retroperitoneal LN; choriocarcinoma spreads by blood, mostly to lung.
- **Treatment** — radioresistant but, luckily, chemosensitive:
  1. Confined to the testis (N0), after orchidectomy → postop RPLND or chemo
  2. Work-up shows +ve retroperitoneal LN → start chemo, then RPLND + PO chemo
  3. Metastatic disease → chemo

## Hydrocele
- Fluid sac between the visceral and parietal layers of the tunica vaginalis.
- Types:
  1. Idiopathic
  2. Secondary — from a hernia with peritoneal fluid running down, infection (e.g. mump, TB), trauma, and tumor
- Beware: 20% of hydroceles are due to tumor — if the testis cannot be felt, send US.
- Key diagnosis: child < 12 years with dragging pain and a constant mass, except communicating hydrocele, which comes and goes; can get above the mass; translumination test +ve.
- **Treatment**
  1. Child < 1 year: hydrocele may resolve; if not resolved after 1 year, consider surgery via inguinal approach (hydrocelectomy). Communicating hydrocele with hernia → operate without waiting, once the child is fit.
  2. Adults: operate when painful or dragging. Sclerosing agents (e.g. 95% alcohol or tetracycline) for those unfit for surgery; problem is frequent recurrence, poor results even after repeated injections.

## Vericocele
- Dilatation of the pampiniform plexus around the spermatic cord; usually left-sided, because the Lt. gonadal (spermatic) vein drains into the Lt. renal vein — longer, enters at a right angle, and runs between the aorta and SMA — giving higher pressure on the left; the right vein drains into the IVC at an acute angle, so blood returns to the systemic circulation more easily.
- Looks like a bag of worms on the scrotum.
- Right-sided or bilateral varicocele, or veins that do not collapse lying down → beware tumor, especially right retroperitoneal tumor; send abdominal US.
- Symptoms: small ones usually silent; large ones cause dull dragging ache of that testis, worse with heavy exercise or long standing/walking, relieved lying down.
- Varicole (as printed) is associated with infertility in about 33% — venous congestion raises testicular temperature → less sperm production.
- **Indication for surgery**
  1. Infertility
  2. Left testis atrophy (ไข่ใบซ้ายฝ่อ)
  3. Testicular pain
- **Surgical treatment**: inguinal or microscopic subinguinal varicocelectomy.

## Testicular Torsion
- Acute scrotal pain in the pre-puberty period from 540° twisting. Exact cause still unknown; hypotheses: cold weather triggering the cremasteric reflex lifting and rotating the testis, or puberty with testicular enlargement, or undescended testis.
- 2 types:
  1. Intravaginal torsion — twist within the tunica vaginalis; usually adolescents; the most common type
  2. Extravaginal torsion — twist outside the tunica vaginalis; usually newborns; thought to be promoted by the testis not yet descended into the scrotal sac
- Testis rides high and lies horizontally (normally vertical axis).
- Differential diagnosis of painful testis:
  1. **Epididymitis and orchitis** — history of eating out (sex) without protection (as printed, "you understand"); gradual onset over > 1 day, not sudden like torsion; dysuria; urine shows WBC and bacteria; testis swollen, red, hot, painful but still in vertical axis. Lifting the testis: torsion → more pain (negative Prehn's sign); inflamed testis → less pain (positive Prehn's sign).
  2. **Incaerated (as printed) hernia** — swelling that comes and goes, history of hernia; one day the content descends and cannot be pushed back.
  3. **Torsion of epididymal appendage** — twisting of a small remnant of the degenerated wolffian duct at the upper testis; sudden onset but less pain than testicular torsion; testis still vertical; doppler shows normal flow. Usually resolves; treat pain and support the testis; if torsion cannot be excluded → explore.
- If the clinical picture fits torsion, doppler US confirmation is not needed (operator dependent); use doppler only if unsure or cannot exclude orchitis: torsion → decreased flow; inflamed testis → increased flow.
- Nuclear scan for testicular blood flow does not work well — not available and slow, by which time the testis is dead. Treat fast, within 4-6 h; > 12 h, salvage < 50%; > 24 h usually orchidectomy.
- **Treatment**
  1. Emergency exploration — incision in the middle of the scrotal sac (median raphy); detorsion of the twisted testis first; wrap the untwisted testis with warm wet swabs and assess viability.
  2. Testis viable → bilateral orchidopexy, fixing both testes to the scrotal sac, since the other side may twist too.
  3. Testis black and necrotic → orchidectomy.
  4. Manual detorsion is not recommended — may not fully untwist, and twisting the wrong way may kill the testis (as printed, "turn it into a horse's testis").
  - Note (หมายเหตุ): torsion of the various appendages, e.g. appendix epididymis, does not threaten the testis, so it can be observed and treated symptomatically.

## Priapism
- Penile erection lasting abnormally long, > 4 hours, without sexual stimulation; patients say the penis is as hard as wood (woody feeling) (as printed, a joke about older readers envying it).
- **Cause**: mostly idiopathic.
- **Risk factor**
  1. Trauma
  2. Hypercoagulable states
  3. Sickle cell anemia
  4. Some substances, e.g. cocaine or antidepreesants (as printed)
- **Priapism** — 2 types:
  1. Low flow or ischemic type — very painful; confirm with penile blood gas from the cavernosal bodies showing hypoxia and acidic blood; must be treated urgently or the penis necroses
  2. High flow or non ischemic type — not very painful; can be treated conservatively first
- **Management** — the core is to make it go down, the faster the better:
  1. Insert IV catheter or biopsy needle into the corpus cavernosum and aspirate blood
  2. If 1 fails → irrigate with diluted epinephrine or norepinephrine to constrict vessels and reduce arterial inflow (remember: never use papaverine)
  3. If 2 fails → OR:
     - 3.1 Create a communication between the glans penis and corpus cavernosum
     - 3.2 Shunt between cavernosum and spongiosum
  4. Treat underlying disease or risk factors
- Figure: percutaneous shunt techniques for priapism — Winter (biopsy needle cores tissue through glans into corpora cavernosa), Ebbehoj (No. 11 scalpel), T-shunt (No. 10 scalpel with scalpel rotation).
