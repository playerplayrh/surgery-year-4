# Spine and Spinal Cord Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 111-154 (PDF pages 2245-2288) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated ๑๘ กุมภาพันธ์ ๒๕๕๙)

## Introduction
- Severely injured patients, especially unconscious ones or those with maxillofacial injury, may also have spine and spinal cord injury.
- About 5% of TBI patients have spine injury, while 25% of spine injury patients also have TBI.
- 20% of spine injuries involve more than 1 level.

## Patients who should be suspected of spine and spinal cord injury (คนไข้ที่ควรสงสัย)
1. Unconscious patient
2. Complaint of neck or back pain
3. Maxillofacial injury
4. Numb arms or legs, no sensation, cannot move
5. PR: no sphincter tone

## Sites of spine injury
1. Cervical spine 55%, and 10-20% also have injury at another spinal level
2. Thoracic spine 15%
3. Thoracolumbar spine 15%
4. Lumbosacral spine 15%

## Spinal injury
- = injury to bone, disc and ligament.
- Two types (in terms of treatment):
  1. **Stable fracture** — posterior ligamentous complex intact, not torn; bony alignment normal; no cord compression → no spinal fixation needed
  2. **Unstable fracture** — usually has:
     - 2.1 Tear of the posterior ligamentous complex
     - 2.2 Vertebral body collapse > 25%
     - 2.3 Dislocation of the vertebral body out of normal alignment
- Unstable fractures usually need stabilization, e.g. collar, halo, brace, or open fixation of the bone.
- Fall from height with heel pain and calcaneus fracture on film → beware an associated TL fracture.
- Diving accident → beware compression fracture of the C spine or teardrop fracture.
- Most common C spine fracture: C5.
- Most common C spine dislocation or subluxation: C5-C6.
- Figure: Fracture patterns (ลักษณะของกระดูกหัก) — burst, extension, wedge compression, dislocation, rotational fracture-dislocation.

## Basic Anatomy
- Spine: 7C, 12T, 5L, 5S, 1 coccyx (text as printed; the figure labels coccyx × 4).
- **Ligaments of the spine** that provide stability:
  1. Anterior ligamentous complex — anterior and posterior longitudinal ligaments
  2. Posterior ligamentous complex — spinous ligament holding the articular and spinous processes
- (Figure caption) Stabilizing elements of the subaxial cervical spine fall into anterior and posterior columns: A anterior longitudinal ligament; B intervertebral disc; C intertransverse ligament; D posterior longitudinal ligament; E capsular ligament; F facet joint; G ligamentum flavum; H interspinous ligament.

## Physical Examination in Spinal Cord Injury
- Level of cord injury: the spinal cord ends at L1; from L2 down there are only nerve roots, the cauda equina.
- So injury above L1 involves both cord and nerve roots; injury from L2 down involves nerve roots only.
- Breathing: abdominal respiration with quadriplegia → probably lower cervical cord injury from C4 down — injuries from C3 up are all fatal.
- Neurogenic shock has a slow heart rate, whereas hypovolemic shock with BP drop has a fast heart rate — heart rate helps tell the two apart.
- Spinal cord injury drops BP through loss of sympathetic tone → vasodilatation; except that with injury below T6, BP remains normal. So with a lesion below T6 and shock, first think of hypovolemia from bleeding somewhere before blaming neurogenic shock.
- Patient moves the arms but not the legs → cord injury must be at T level or below.
- **Priapism** — from ANS dysfunction: loss of sympathetic tone lets parasympathetic tone dominate → suggests cord injury above T7; this kind of erection is bad — indicates likely poor prognosis.
- **Neurological examination** (การตรวจทางระบบประสาท)
  1. Sensory, by dermatome
     - 1.1 Pain and temperature — assess the anterior column
     - 1.2 Position and sensation — assess the posterior column
     - 1.3 Sensation at the perineum around the anus; while there, put a finger in the rectum to check sphincter tone and see whether there is sacral sparing
  2. Muscle power, with grading
  3. DTR

## Deep Tendon Reflexes
- Normal = 2+
- Hyporeflexia = 0 & 1+
- Hyporeflexia = 3+ & 4+ (as printed — same word used for both)
- Knee jerk L3-L4
- Angle jerk (as printed) L5-S1
- Weak gastrocnemius with loss of ankle reflex → level of injury at L5-S1 (จำ).

## Severity of Spinal Cord Injury
- Roughly 3 patterns (4 listed as printed):
  1. **Complete cord injury** (transection) — permanent loss of motor and sensory function below the injured level; usually cord transection at C and T levels, with no chance of recovery — except cauda equina injury, which can still partly recover. Early on, complete cord injury has no spinal reflex — this distinguishes it from spinal shock, in which some reflexes remain.
  2. **Incomplete cord injury** — partial loss of motor and sensory function; the patient shows a spinal cord syndrome.
  3. **Spinal shock** — temporary shutdown of the spinal cord → loss of motor, sensory and reflexes below the injured level.
  4. **Neurogenic shock** — syndrome of hypotension and bradycardia from spinal cord injury above T6: sympathetic tone is interrupted and vagal tone dominates → bradycardia and peripheral vasodilatation; skin stays warm and pink.

## Spinal Cord Syndromes
- Cord syndrome patterns by neuroanatomy — 4 types:
  1. Central cord syndrome
  2. Anterior cord syndrome
  3. Posterior cord syndrome
  4. Brown-Sequard syndrome
- Figure: Cord cross-section tracts — dorsal column (touch, vibration); corticospinal tract (upper motor); anterior horn (lower motor); spinothalamic tract (pain, temperature).
- Pay special attention to central cord.
- **Central cord syndrome** — from hyperextension injury compressing the cord from the front. 50% of patients recover.
  - Motor: paralysis of the hands and arms while the legs are still fine (จำ) — due to how somatic fibers are arranged in the corticospinal tract (as printed: how they are arranged — a clever surgeon should not care); also sphincter dysfunction.
  - Sensory: loss of pain and temperature.
  - Prognosis: fair — there is a chance (มีลุ้น).
- **Anterior cord syndrome** — injury to the anterior 2/3, leaving only the posterior column. Mostly from anterior spinal artery injury. Very poor prognosis — about 10% chance of recovery.
  - Motor: legs paralysed more than arms.
  - Sensory: loss of pain, temperature and touch, but vibration and position remain normal.
  - Prognosis: worst.
- **Posterior cord syndrome** — only crude touch sensation remains; prognosis fair (มีลุ้น), like central cord.
- **Brown-Sequard syndrome** — cord hemitransection: half of the cord is destroyed.
  - Motor: paralysis of arm and leg on the same side.
  - Sensory: loss of position and proprioception on the same side, loss of pain and temperature on the opposite side.
  - Prognosis: best.

## Summary of causes of cord syndrome (สรุปสาเหตุของ cord syndrome)

| Syndrome | Aetiology |
|---|---|
| Complete transection | Trauma, infarction, transverse myelitis, abscess, tumour |
| Cord hemisection | Trauma, multiple sclerosis, tumour, abscess |
| Central cord | Neck hyperextension, syringomyelia, tumour |
| Anterior cord | Hyperflexion, disc protusion (as printed), anterior spinal artery occlusion, post AAA |

## Spinal Shock
- Sudden, temporary loss of spinal cord neurological function: flaccid paralysis and loss of spinal reflexes below the cord injury level. During this phase you cannot yet tell whether the cord injury is complete or incomplete.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Early on, the neurological deficit may rise 1-2 levels from vascular change at that segment
  2. Bradycardia, SBP about 80-100
- Spinal shock usually improves within 24 h; about 99% recover within 48 h.
- On recovery from spinal shock, muscles come back first; test simply with the bulbocavernosus reflex.
- **Bulbocavernosus reflex** — reflex arc at S2-3: the examiner squeezes the glans penis (the patient's, not the examiner's — as printed) → stimulates afferent fibers through the spinal cord, out via efferent fibers. If the cord has recovered, the anal sphincter contracts.
- In general, if after 24-48 h there is still no sensation in arms/legs and around the anus → likely structural disruption; chance of recovery is small.
- Those who do not recover are complete cord injury: motor and sensory dead, only reflexes remain; flaccid paralysis becomes spastic paralysis, and the urinary bladder changes from flaccid to reflex neurogenic bladder.

## Investigation for Spine and Spinal Cord Trauma
- Aim: is there fracture & dislocation, and if fractured, is it stable or unstable.
- ATLS 9th ed recommends CT C-spine — more accurate than plain film.
- **Plain film spine**
  - If plain film is chosen, take 3 views, aiming at the bones and their alignment; the normal neck has a lordotic curve.
  - AP/lateral view — should show C7 and T1; may need to pull the patient's arms down, or take a swimmer view.
  - Oblique view — for pedicle and articular process.
  - Open mouth or odontoid view — for the odontoid (C2). Exam tip: an x-ray of the skull in open-mouth position = fracture dens or odontoid.
  - If ligament injury is suspected → flexion and extension views.
- **CT spine**
  - 3 mm cuts help diagnose fracture dislocation that plain film may miss, especially C1-C2 and C7-T1.
  - Large US centers now use CT instead of plain film (axial, coronal and sagittal views) — much better sensitivity and specificity. As printed, in Thailand do not copy them blindly (อย่าไปอุจจาระตามช้าง) — it is wasteful.
  - Limitation of CT: ligament, disc and cord injury less clearly seen than with MRI.
- **MRI spine**
  - Suited to patients with neurological deficit whose plain film or CT is normal.
  - Advantages (ข้อดีของ MRI):
    1. Shows the cord clearly — cord swelling, cord compression or spinal epidural hematoma
    2. Shows the disc clearly
    3. Shows soft tissue and ligament clearly — order when ligamentous injury is suspected
  - Limitations: expensive and slow → unsuitable for unstable patients.

## Initial Assessment and Management
- Prehospital phase and transport: lay the patient supine, look after breathing, move carefully. Always assume every severely injured patient may have C-spine injury: rigid cervical collar plus supportive block on backboard, strapped across the forehead.
- Beware children: about 20% — the ligaments supporting the spine are still weak, so cord injury happens easily while X-ray shows nothing abnormal = spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality (SCIWORA).
- Position children on a backboard with thoracic elevation and an occipital recess — lying flat like an adult increases C-spine flexion.
- Move the patient with 3 helpers, rolling like a log (log roll).
- Immobilization: head, neck, chest, trunk and legs kept in a straight line.
- Protect C-spine with a hard collar, or sandbags beside the neck with sticky plaster taped across the forehead to the bed.
- Shock with fast heart rate → look for a cause of hypovolemic shock, possibly in chest or abdomen.
- C-spine film: lateral view first.
- Cord injury with low BP: once blood loss in abdomen and chest is excluded, give an IV load first; if BP does not rise, add vasopressor.
- Physical exam is usually in the secondary survey: slide a hand under the back and palpate the spinous processes along the whole length, looking for the most tender point and whether the interspinous gaps are abnormally wide or narrow.
- The most tender point is probably the injured site.
- Films in the secondary survey: AP, lateral and open-mouth view for the odontoid; if plain film is unclear, add CT at C1-C2 and C7-T1.
- Conscious patient with no neck pain → probably no C-spine X-ray needed; a collar already on can be taken off.
- The National Emergency Radiography Utilization Study set 5 clinical criteria; if all 5 are met, C-spine injury can be ruled out:
  1. No midline cervical tenderness (printed "tendernrss")
  2. No focal neurological deficit
  3. Normal alertness
  4. No intoxication
  5. No painful distracting injury
- Taking off a hard collar — 5 principles (6 listed as printed):
  1. No neck pain
  2. No posterior midline neck tenderness
  3. No focal neurological deficit
  4. No alcohol or drugs
  5. No distracting injury
  6. CT C-spine normal
- Remember: **when in doubt, leave the collar on.**

## Management of Spine and Spinal Cord Injury
- Order of treatment:
  1. Treat cord injury
  2. Treat bony fracture
  3. Treat complication
  4. PT
  5. Treat mental illness and stress (โรคจิต โรคเครียด)
- After the early crisis, further care:
  1. NPO and leave the NG tube in — ileus usually lasts about 48 h, then start feeding
  2. Foley
  3. DVT prophylaxis — DVT is common in spinal cord injury; usually start with mechanical prophylaxis such as pneumatic compression device; LMWH may also be needed for about 3-6 months
  4. Constipation → laxatives, enema, or manual evacuation
  5. Beware pressure sore
- **High dose methylprednisolone** — no data yet that it improves cord recovery, while it adds infection and GI bleeding. Supporters believe steroid given within 8 h, especially within 3 h, may help, and is useless after 8 h. From the author's review: for the theory exam, answer "do not give".
- **Indication for surgery**
  1. Fracture or dislocation that cannot be corrected without surgery
  2. Open fracture
  3. Soft tissue or bony compression of the cord
  4. Acute anterior spinal cord syndrome
  5. Progressive neurological deficit from cord swelling or bone/disc fragments pressing
- **Surgical treatment**
  1. Realignment of the bones — for C spine, skull traction may be applied first; TL spine has no traction option — open only
  2. Cord compression (usually from disc or bone fragments), especially incomplete cord injury with increasing neurological deficit → urgent decompression
  3. Long-term stabilization by spinal fusion — C spine usually wiring or bone graft; TL spine usually Harrington rod

## Atlas Fracture (C1)
- The atlas is a thin ring with no body, only a hole for the odontoid (C2) to fit into.
- Jefferson fracture = fracture of the C1 ring with breaks in the anterior and posterior arch; about 46% also have a C2 (axis) fracture.
- C1 breaks from severe axial or vertical loading on the C spine.
- **Symptoms**: if not killed by cord injury, the patient complains of severe neck pain, cannot move the head at all, but can still sit or stand. Fortunately C1 fractures are usually stable, so cord injury is uncommon.
- If suspected → lateral and submentovertex view films, confirm with CT.
- **Treatment**
  1. Initial treatment: rigid collar first
  2. Then halo apparatus about 4-6 weeks, followed by collar another 1-2 months
  3. Unstable fracture → skull traction or spinal fusion

## Axis Fracture (C2)
- C2 fractures are of 2 kinds:
  1. Odontoid fracture
  2. Hangman fracture
- Non-odontoid, non-hangman fractures make up about 20% — various breaks of the body.

## Odontoid Fracture (C2)
- Fracture of the odontoid (dens) part of C2.
- The odontoid has a fairly poor blood supply → risk of nonunion.
- An odontoid fracture that compresses the cord usually stops breathing and dies at once; the survivors we see are those without cord injury.
- Diagnose with open-mouth and lateral films; CT to show the fracture line clearly.
- (Figure caption) Two reformatted CT images: transverse fracture of the base of the dens; the dens displaced slightly posteriorly on the C2 body.
- **3 types**
  1. **Type 1** — fracture above the base of the odontoid; luckily usually stable. Treatment: hard collar about 4-6 weeks.
  2. **Type 2** — fracture right at the base; mostly unstable, a few stable. Type 2 is the most common. Treatment:
     1. Unstable → spinal fusion
     2. Stable → skull traction 6 weeks, then halo apparatus another 6 weeks
  3. **Type 3** — fracture extending into the vertebral body. Treatment: skull traction about 4 weeks, then halo another 8 weeks.

## Hangman Fracture
- Fracture through the bilateral pars interarticularis or pedicles at the back of C2.
- From hyperextension of the neck.
- Fortunately hangman fracture rarely causes cord injury.
- **Treatment**
  1. Mostly halo vest, kept on longer than for Jefferson fracture
  2. Severe displacement → spinal fusion

## Dislocation of Cervical Spine
- Most common at C5 and C6.
- From flexion and rotation force.
- **Early treatment**: usually traction first, then decide whether to operate.
- **Indication for surgery**
  1. Unstable fracture or dislocation
  2. Progressive neurological deficit
  3. Persistent bony fragments in the spinal canal

## Lower Cervical Spine Fracture (C3-C7)
- Common at C5 and C6 (most flexion and extension); least common at C3.
- **Compression fracture of vertebral body** — only collapse, no displacement → stable fracture. Treatment:
  1. Bed rest until pain resolves, then hard collar another 2 months
  2. Neurological deficit (nerve root compression) → remove the bone pressing on the nerve, then spinal fusion
- **Burst fracture of vertebral body** — usually many fragments → unstable fracture. Treatment:
  1. Skull traction first
  2. Then operate to remove bone fragments, fix with plate and screw plus bone graft

## Thoracolumbar Spine Fracture
- Usually from a fall from height (axial loading injury) or hyperflexion & rotation.
- Common site: T10-T12.
- Fracture above L1 may include cord injury since the cord ends at L1; fracture below L1 gives only cauda equina injury.
- Fracture with more than 1 column disrupted = unstable fracture.
- TL fractures classified by fracture pattern:
  1. **Wedge compression** — collapse of the front of the bone; rarely neurological deficit; usually stable. Treatment (การรักษา):
     1. Collapse < 25% → bed rest until pain resolves
     2. Collapse > 25% but not > 50% → body jacket or Taylor (hyperextension) brace; severe pain → inject bone cement into the bone; as the bone hardens the pain eases
     3. Collapse > 50% or progressive kyphosis → consider surgery
     4. With cord injury → surgical decompression plus fixation with Harrington rod
  2. **Burst fracture** — from axial loading; the bone breaks and collapses both front and back. Treatment:
     1. No cord injury or neurological deficit → NPO about 1-2 days (ileus is common early) with bed rest until pain resolves, then Taylor brace another 2-3 months
     2. Neurological deficit → the posterior column is not intact → unstable fracture: operate to remove fragments, then fix with bone graft or Harrington rod (printed "Harrinton")
  3. **Fracture dislocation of TL spine** — very severe; usually complete neurological deficit
  4. **Flexion distraction injury** — type 3 combined with Chance fracture (type 5)
  - Treatment for both 3 and 4:
    1. Open reduction with Harrington rod to fix the bone
    2. After surgery, bed rest 4-6 weeks, then Taylor brace another 3-4 months
  5. **Seat belt type injury** or **Chance fracture** — transverse fracture of the vertebral body; usually no neurological deficit, but may come with retroperitoneal organ injury or small bowel tear
- Fractures of transverse process, spinous process, sacrum and coccyx usually cause only pain without neurological deficit. Treatment: symptomatic — analgesics with bed rest is enough.

## Peripheral Nerve Injury
- **Seddon classification** — nerve injury in 3 groups:
  1. **Neurapraxia** — no nerve disruption, only a bruised nerve with conduction block; usually recovers within 12 weeks
  2. **Axonotmesis** — only the axon is torn; endoneurium and perineurium intact. First wallerian degeneration (printed "wallerrian") of the distal end, then the axon regenerates from the proximal end to replace it. Our job: observation and follow-up.
  3. **Neurotmesis** — both axon and endoneurium torn; the nerve is separated. Treatment: repair
     - 3.1 Primary repair — join the nerve at once; suits sharp laceration, patient in good condition with stable vital signs and no severe soft tissue injury; early repair gives better results than delayed repair
     - 3.2 Delayed repair — suits crush injury with severe soft tissue injury, or unstable vital signs needing a quick finish. While waiting, EMG at about 3-4 weeks post-injury; after 3 months consider re-exploring the nerve
- Figure: Seddon diagram — neuropraxia: mild injury, recovery; axonotmesis: severe injury, regeneration (1 mm/day), recovery; neurotmesis: injury, degeneration, neuroma formation.

## Summary of peripheral nerve injury in the arm (สรุป peripheral nerve injury ที่แขน)
- **Median nerve (C6-T1)**
  1. Motor: thumb apposition, thumb abduction, finger flexion
  2. Sensory: 3½ fingers on the palmar side
  3. Median nerve palsy: loss of thumb abduction and apposition (figure labels: unopposed thumb, thenar atrophy)
- **Ulnar nerve (C8-T1)**
  1. Motor: intrinsic muscles, finger abduction and wrist flexion (printed "wrisf")
  2. Sensory: half of the ring finger and the little finger, dorsal side (as printed)
  3. Ulnar nerve palsy: claw deformity of the ring and little finger
- **Radial nerve (C5-C8)**
  1. Motor: wrist extension, finger extension, no hand muscle
  2. Sensory: 3½ fingers (thumb, index, middle, ring — โป้ง ชี้ กลาง นาง) on the dorsal surface
  3. Radial nerve palsy: loss of wrist extension
