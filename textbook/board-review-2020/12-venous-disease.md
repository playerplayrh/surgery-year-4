# Venous Diseases 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 628-685 (PDF pages 632-689)

- Intro: the venous diseases a resident should know are probably CVD (chronic venous disorder), DVT and PE; Schwartz does not write much about them; the reference book most worth reading is probably Rutherford, whose volume of knowledge is too much to remember; the author's guide here should answer more than 90% of questions.

## Venous Anatomy
- Systemic veins hold about 2/3 of the circulating blood volume: low flow, low pressure, low resistance.
- Blood returns to the heart mainly through the contraction of the calf muscle pumping blood up from the deep veins, together with the pumping of the heart and venous tone.
- Veins in the leg have valves, which are bicuspid, dividing the column of blood so it can flow only toward the heart, and from the superficial system into the deep system; the common iliac vein, portal vein and IVC have no valves.
- Veins have 3 systems:
  1. Superficial system: veins in the subcutaneous layer lying above the femoral (deep) fascia, divided into 2 main trunks:
     - 1.1 Greater saphenous vein (GSV): starts by receiving venous blood at the dorsum of the foot, passes the medial malleolus of the ankle and the anteromedial aspect of the leg, runs up to the upper thigh, then drains into the common femoral vein at the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ), entering from the lateral side of the SFJ; the common femoral artery lies lateral to the SFJ; beside the GSV is the saphenous nerve, a sensory nerve, lying very close to it below the knee.
     - 1.2 Lesser or small saphenous vein (SSV): receives venous blood from the posterolateral side and back of the leg below the knee, drains into the popliteal vein at the saphenopopliteal junction (SPJ) in the popliteal fossa; beside the SSV is the sural nerve, a sensory nerve, lying very close in the lower half of the calf.
  2. Deep system: runs alongside the major artery between muscles, under the deep fascia, the route for venous blood from the leg to the vena cava and then the right heart.
  3. Perforator system: veins connecting the superficial and deep systems, with valves that force blood in one direction only, from superficial to deep; perforators lie at many positions, e.g. Boyd perforator, Cockett perforator, Dodd group or Hunterian perforator vein. Figures: leg veins with valve at saphenofemoral junction, mid-thigh perforator, ankle perforators; Dodd's group, Boyd's communicating, Cockett's communicating veins, gastrocnemius point, soleus point, lateral communicating vein, 12 cm and 5 cm communicating veins (skip).
- Remember: perforator veins have valves that drain blood from the superficial vein to the deep vein, not from deep to superficial.
- Venous valve: a bicuspid valve preventing blood from flowing back down to the foot; blood is lifted step by step at the calf; the GSV and SSV have about 6-10 valves, holding the blood in sections, while the subclavian, common iliac and common femoral veins usually have no valves.

## Chronic Venous Disorder (CVD)
- Means a chronic abnormality of the veins, both structure (morphology) and function, causing chronic venous disease (CEAP C1-C3) as symptoms and signs arising from abnormal veins, e.g. varicose vein (CEAP class 2).
- Or the severe symptoms arising from raised pressure in the veins until ulcers form are called chronic venous insufficiency (CVI), which is in CEAP class 4-6.
- Venous pathology found most often: valve incompetence:
  1. Primary incompetence: arises from weakness of the venous wall itself; often the cause of superficial venous insufficiency.
  2. Secondary incompetence: often follows DVT or superficial thrombophlebitis with destruction of the venous valves.
- Clinical manifestations of CVD found most often: a heavy leg, tired, cramps at night, leg pain that is often worse on standing for long periods, improving when walking, or lying with the leg raised; some have pain while walking called venous claudication, caused by venous hypertension and blood being congested; the patient says the leg pain is like it will burst (bursting pain).
  - Separate this pain from peripheral arterial disease (intermittent claudication) and from pain due to spinal nerve root compression (neurogenic claudication), where the pain occurs while walking, but when sitting or bending forward the spinal canal widens and the pain decreases.
- Classification of chronic venous disorder: systematised with the CEAP classification, which has 4 assessments, with the aim of making the grouping of disease one standard and helping doctors choose treatment:
  1. C = Clinical classification. 2. E = Etiological classification. 3. A = Anatomical classification. 4. P = Pathophysiological classification.
- Clinical classification: by presenting signs, C0-C6:
  - C0: no visible signs of venous disease.
  - C1: small dilated subcutaneous veins in the skin: if under 1 mm called telangiectasia; if 1-3 mm called reticular vein. Figure: telangiectasia, reticular vein (skip).
  - C2: varicose vein, size over 3 mm; the vein is dilated and tortuous; cause mostly from reflux.
  - C3: starts to have leg swelling: in the early period it is pitting edema from protein leaking out of the veins, but without skin change; if long standing the tissue becomes hard, and pressing then gives no pit.
  - C4: skin change: C4a: hyperpigmentation: blackish skin from leaking of hemosiderin pigment of red cells; C4b: skin looks hard from lipodermatosclerosis or fibrosis of the subcutaneous tissue arising from inflammation and fibrosis of subcutaneous tissue; if not treated well it enters C5 and begins to form an ulcer.
  - C5: healed ulcer + C4 features.
  - C6: active ulcer + C4.
- Table (CEAP clinical classification of chronic venous disease): 0 no visible or palpable signs of venous disease; 1 telangiectases and/or reticular veins; 2 varicose veins; 3 edema; 4a skin changes (pigmentation and/or eczema); 4b skin changes (lipodermatosclerosis and/or atrophie blanche); 5 healed venous ulcer; 6 active venous ulcer; S (in addition to the class) symptoms including ache, pain, tightness, skin irritation, heaviness, muscle cramps as well as other complaints attributable to venous dysfunction; A (in addition to the class) asymptomatic.
- Etiology classification (E): 4 groups: 1. Ec = Congenital: cause present from birth, e.g. Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome and vascular malformation; these diseases have high pressure in the veins; 2. Ep = Primary: cause from abnormal weakness of the vein or the venous wall itself in some places; 3. Es = Secondary: cause arises after venous obstruction (post-thrombotic syndrome); 4. En = no cause identified, or cause not yet found.
- Anatomical classification (A): tells the location of venous disease, often needing physical examination with Doppler or duplex as helpers; 4 groups: 1. As = superficial vein; 2. Ap = perforator vein; 3. Ad = deep vein; 4. An = no venous location identified.
- Pathophysiological classification (P): 4 groups: 1. Pr = reflux, arising from backward flow of blood in the veins; 2. Po = obstruction, arising from blockage, e.g. DVT; 3. Pr&o = reflux and obstruction together; 4. Pn = none identified.
- There are more details about the recording form, but it is not necessary to know.

## Investigation of CVD
- Imaging studies are used to help diagnosis and plan treatment; the author gives a short summary: only what is clearly needed.
- Duplex US: have the patient stand straight, use a linear array transducer probe of frequency 4-7 MHz; start by looking whether there is a clot in the vein, and whether it is in a superficial or deep vein; then press the probe on the vein: if it does not compress (non-compressible vein), it shows a clot is truly in the vein; if the duplex is not enough, it can also see venous flow or the flow signal, to tell whether there is venous obstruction or reflux (backflow of blood to the foot).
  - Advantage: a non-invasive investigation that can give the diagnosis and tell the location of disease clearly, correctly and accurately, so there is no need to examine with plethysmography to measure the volume of venous blood in the veins.
  - Limitation: seeing the veins in the inguinal area or the abdominal cavity, which needs CTV or MRV.
- CT venography (CTV): inject contrast into a peripheral vein of the leg then scan to see the veins of the leg, pelvis and abdomen, especially in cases with a pelvic mass that may externally compress the veins.
- MR venography (MRV): no problem of contrast nephropathy, and since it can assess soft tissue well it suits cases suspected of vascular malformation.
- Intravascular US (IVUS): a small US probe passed through a central line; popularly used to examine iliac vein compression, where all other imaging cannot give an answer, and IVUS is used to help assess dilatation together with placing a venous stent.
- Venography or phlebography: besides being used for diagnosing disease, it is used in cases with intervention such as dilating and placing a stent in a vein; venography has 2 types:
  1. Ascending venography: inject dye into a vein at the back of the foot together with a tourniquet above the injection site, to force the contrast to run from the superficial system into the deep system, then take films to see the deep veins of the whole leg.
  2. Descending venography: inject dye into the common femoral vein with the patient standing, to look for reflux or backflow down the veins.

## Varicose Vein
- A bulging and tortuous dilatation of the veins in the subcutaneous tissue of the leg caused by loss of valvular function of the veins, whether superficial, perforator or deep system, so blood flows back (reflux). Figure: mechanism of varicose vein (saphenous vein, superficial compartment, deep (pump) compartment, deep outflow tract) (skip).
- By CEAP classification of CVI, varicose veins lie in groups C1 and C2: C1: small dilated veins under 1 mm like dilated skin capillaries are called telangiectasia; small dilated subcutaneous veins 1-3 mm are called reticular vein; C2: tortuous veins bulging, dilated, size over 3 mm from the centre, called varicose vein.
- Patients' age: usually working age, often in jobs where they sit or stand at a desk with little movement; people with high abdominal pressure, e.g. obese people, pregnant women, are found often.
- We divide varicose veins by cause:
  1. Primary varicose vein: a group with unknown cause; believed that most of the problem comes from incompetence of the superficial vein, from weakening of the venous wall or valves that do not close tightly; incompetence of the deep vein and perforator is found less.
  2. Secondary varicose vein: the cause of the dilated veins is disease of the deep vein, most often the late complication of deep vein thrombosis with lysis of the clot followed by fibrosis, making the valves not close tightly, which we call post phlebitic syndrome, found in 60% of proximal DVT; a minority arise from a mass in the abdominal cavity or a mass in the pelvis compressing the vein, or occur after an AV fistula.
- Symptoms:
  1. Aching pain, dull, annoying when the leg is tired, or at cramp; the pain is worse when sitting with the leg hanging down or standing; the pain decreases when the leg is raised, or when wearing an elastic bandage for a week the patient feels better.
  2. Some have pain from superficial thrombophlebitis.
  3. Some come with cramps.
  4. Itching and skin colour change.
  5. Some come with symptoms of CVI, CEAP classification C3 and above: swollen leg, or an ulcer at the medial ankle (venous ulcer). Figure: presentation (unsightly veins, leg heaviness, dull achy leg, swelling, asymptomatic) and progression (hyperpigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis, induration, ulceration, bleeding) (skip).
- Points to note:
  1. If CEAP is only C1-C2, it is usually incompetence of the superficial vein.
  2. If C3 and above, there is usually a problem of incompetence of the perforator or deep vein.

## Physical Examination
1. Have the patient stand, look at both legs, front and back; the dilated veins show clearly; palpate a little: if they are painful, red and swollen it may be thrombophlebitis; tap lightly (Schwartz test) and feel the vibration along the column of the vein; if the valves of the vein leak, the vibration from the tap travels far; then use Doppler to listen for valve incompetence or not; nowadays Schwartz test and other tests are done less, because Doppler and duplex are far more reliable.
2. Brodie-Trendelenburg test: a test for incompetence of the superficial vein and perforator, divided into 2 phases: the first phase tests the perforator; the second phase tests the superficial vein.
   - Method and interpretation (for medical students; the author last examined this around his first year of residency):
     1. Have the patient lie flat, raise the leg to about 45 degrees (the patient's leg) so the varicose veins collapse.
     2. Put a rubber tourniquet around the upper thigh below the SFJ (some use a finger to press).
     3. Have the patient stand up, then observe the refilling of the superficial veins below the tourniquet.
     3.1 If on standing the veins bulge quickly, it shows the perforator is incompetent (+); or the problem may be at the short saphenous vein; to separate them, put another rubber tourniquet below the knee.
     3.2 If on standing the veins do not bulge, or take more than 30 seconds to bulge, it shows both perforators and short saphenous are still good; the incompetence must come from the superficial vein.
     3.3 The next step tests whether the SFJ is incompetent: do the steps above again; when the thigh is tied, have the patient stand and release the tourniquet: if the veins bulge quickly at once, it shows there must be SFJ incompetence.
     3.4 Testing the lesser saphenous vein is done like the SFJ test, but we use a tourniquet placed below the knee to control the refilling of the varicose veins below the knee.
   - Interpretation: -ve/-ve: both perforator and superficial vein are good; -ve/+ve: perforator good but superficial vein incompetent; +ve/-ve: perforator incompetent but superficial vein good; +ve/+ve: all incompetent.
   - From the most classic study the Brodie test has sensitivity 50% and specificity only 25%, so it is a test suited to medical students and first-year residents only.
- As for Perthes test, Linton test, cough impulse test and many more: if we are not going to be vascular fellows, do not bother to know them, a waste of time.
- Moreover, the European Society of Vascular Surgery 2015 and a study from the USA say not to recommend doing all the tests mentioned above, because the accuracy is low: go straight to duplex, which is far better and more reliable than all of them put together, including the vascular surgeons at the large centres, who say that in a patient suspected of having CVI, do a duplex scan right away, without worrying about severity and duration of symptoms.
- For the vascular lab, duplex US in the standing position, and venography, which may be necessary in some cases, but not done routinely: in a case that needs investigation choose duplex first because it tells the location of the reflux segment (venous valvular incompetence) well.

### Indication for investigation
1. History of DVT, or examination of the leg finds, besides varicose veins, signs of CVI such as ulcer or hyperpigmentation.
2. Recurrence after proper treatment.
3. Cannot locate the site of the perforator or lesser saphenous incompetence.
- For patients with only C1 located far from the GSV area, e.g. a reticular vein at the lateral aspect of the upper leg, there is no need to send an imaging study.
- In practice, when meeting a varicose vein case at OPD, nobody will sit and do all the various tests, because it takes long and is not very reliable; just take a history, examine, look, palpate the veins, then take a Doppler to listen; the way to do it in steps:
  1. Have the patient stand, then put the probe at the position we suspect has valve incompetence.
  2. Then use the hand to squeeze the patient's calf and listen to the sound of venous blood flow.
  3. Emphasise listening when the calf squeeze is released: if we hear the sound of venous flow flowing back, it shows true valve incompetence.
- If the Doppler is abnormal, send for duplex, done in the standing position: it helps tell what the cause of the venous disease is:
  1. Reflux: leaking, at the superficial vein and perforator vein we find blood flowing back for longer than 0.5 sec; but if deep vein, blood flows back for longer than 1 sec.
  2. Obstruction: helps locate where, tells which vein is blocked, and helps tell the exact position of valvular incompetence.

## Management of Varicose Vein
- 3 main treatments: 1. conservative treatment; 2. interventional treatment; 3. surgical treatment.

### Conservative Treatment
1. Lifestyle modification: 1.1 do not sit with legs hanging down or stand still until the legs swell; walk often; when sleeping raise the legs higher than heart level about 30 degrees; 1.2 do physical exercise, dorsiflex the ankles often; calf muscle contraction helps pump blood back well; 1.3 lose weight, do not overeat.
2. Compression therapy: use an elastic bandage wrapped from the toes upward, or wear compressive stockings with pressure about 20-30 mmHg; the purpose is to flatten the veins, reducing swelling and venous leakage; wrap the elastic bandage from when waking up, then take it off when going to bed.
   - Contraindication to remember: do not use in patients who also have arterial occlusion, especially a leg that is black and decayed (remember): so if ABI is under 0.6, or ankle BP under 60 mmHg, remember to forbid wrapping; check the pulse of the diseased leg before wrapping; besides, patients with severe skin inflammation should also not use.
   - Caution: compression stockings have a lifetime of only 3-6 months, so one should change to a new one every 3-6 months (remember).
3. Venoactive drug: those who cheer it believe it helps increase venous tone of the venous wall, reducing venous reflux, leakage, pain and swelling; at present there are several drugs, e.g. daflon, aescin (horse chestnut); given in patients with leg pain together with swelling; but a Cochrane review of 44 papers found this group of drugs has no data supporting clear improvement of CVI, so they should not be used as first line treatment.

### Interventional Treatment
- 3 methods used (listed as): 1. sclerotherapy; 2. endovenous treatment with laser or RFA.

### Sclerotherapy
- A chemical ablation by injecting a sclerosant into the vein to destroy the endothelium, so the vein becomes inflamed, blocked and fibrosed; in the old days sclerosant was injected; nowadays the popular method is US guided foam sclerotherapy (UGFS).
- Patients for sclerotherapy must have varicose veins without incompetence of the SFJ or SPJ (popliteal).
- Indication: 1. telangiectasia and reticular vein (not suitable for sclerotherapy in large dilated veins); 2. isolated varicosities; 3. below-knee varicosities, positions unsuitable for venous stripping or endothermal ablation; 4. recurrent varicosities.
- Contraindication: 1. hypersensitivity to sclerosant; 2. acute thrombophlebitis, with an inflamed wound still present, DVT or PAD together; 3. large varicosities with communication to the deep vein; 4. after injection, must wrap a bandage on the leg for 3-5 days, then change to compression stocking for 3-6 weeks, so patients who do not understand or cannot maintain good external compression should not have this method; 5. pregnant or breastfeeding patients; 6. hyperthyroidism; 7. bed-ridden status, or those who cannot move the legs: DVT will occur easily.
- Success usually occurs with veins not over 3 mm in size; if the vein is larger than this, must use a more concentrated sclerosant or use foam sclerotherapy; what injection method and which sclerosant to use is a point to know.
- US guided foam sclerotherapy (UGFS): take sclerosant 3% polidocanol mixed with air at a ratio of 1:4 to make a foam (micro-bubbles); then inject the foam into the varicose vein; the foam helps push out the blood remaining in the vein, so the sclerosant contacts the venous wall better; results are better than injecting sclerosant alone; after injection wrap with an elastic bandage for about 3 days.
- After the vein is blocked there is a chance of re-canalization, and may need re-injection.
- In general foam sclerotherapy gives good results, even though it is a secondary option to HL/S, laser, RFA, but it is a procedure with good safety.
- Beware neurological complications from foam bubbles that we inject, which then slip into the brain via a patent foramen ovale causing TIA or CVA.
- Complications found: 1. hyperpigmentation at the injection site, luckily the stain usually fades within 1 year; 2. leakage causing skin necrosis; 3. the needle hits a nerve or artery; 4. anaphylaxis.

### Endovenous Therapy
- With laser or RFA: the heat produced (thermal ablation) makes the varicose vein collapse and block.
- Thermal ablation has 2 methods: endovenous laser ablation and endovenous RFA ablation.
- The varicose vein to be treated by this method should be a straight vein, not very tortuous, and lying more than 1 cm from the skin.
- Advantages of endovenous therapy: 1. minimally invasive procedure; 2. ambulatory procedure; 3. avoids GA or spinal block; 4. quick method; 5. no scarring.
- This method is not suitable for: 1. large varicose vein (over 3 cm) and very tortuous; 2. varicose vein that is too short; 3. venous inflammation, because the catheter is hard to insert into an inflamed vein; 4. a vein lying less than 1 cm from the skin, because the heat will burn the skin; 5. GSV below the knee and SSV lower than the middle of the calf, because the heat will burn the saphenous nerve and sural nerve lying close to the vein.
- Beware endovenous heat induced thrombosis (EHIT), where the venous clot from the GSV extends into the common femoral vein: if the clot extends more than 50% or causes common femoral vein occlusion, give anticoagulation for 3 months.
- Method: inject xylocaine with adrenaline mixed with NSS + sodium bicarbonate by the usual formula around the GSV, called tumescent anesthesia; the fluid injected stays around the vein and besides reducing pain, tumescent anesthesia helps press the varicose vein flat so the heat touches the venous wall more and prevents the heat from burning tissue next to the vein.
- If a good case is chosen: RCT comparing RFA with open conventional ligation + stripping found early success, less pain, return to work sooner, while the vein occlusion rate at 5 years is still as high as 85%.
- At present endovenous therapy should be the first line treatment of chronic venous disease.
- And when comparing endovenous treatment with sclerotherapy, thermal ablation has clearly better occlusion rate and recurrence.

#### Endovenous Laser Ablation (EVLA)
- Insert a laser fiber into the GSV and release heat energy so that the blood is heated, which makes the venous wall hot too, damaging the endothelium of the venous wall followed by occlusion of that varicose vein. Figure: laser in the vein (skip).
- After finishing, wrap the leg for 24 hours, then wear a stocking continuously, except at bedtime when it can be taken off.

#### Endovenous RFA Ablation
- Insert an RFA catheter into the GSV, using heat from radio waves to damage the venous wall like the laser; the difference is that RFA delivers heat to the venous wall directly. Figure: RFA catheter (skip).

#### Endovenous Vena Seal
- A new toy replacing chemical ablation with sclerosant by using cyanoacrylate glue injected to block the vein; results of this treatment: success rate in the early period is very good, but still must wait for long term results.

#### Endovenous Mechanochemical Ablation (MOCA)
- Inject foam, then insert a catheter whose tip can spin round and round in the varicose vein, making the inside of the vein injured, followed by occlusion; it is said the results are fairly good, not as painful as heat, but still must wait for long term results like glue sealing.

### Surgical Treatment
- Even though the vascular associations both in England and America recommend, if there is no contraindication and the case is suitable, choosing endovenous thermal ablation first, making operations fewer, because endovenous ablation hurts less, recovers faster, returns to work sooner, and has effects not much different.
- However, surgery remains the standard treatment of superficial varicose vein, especially varicose veins with a cause from saphenous reflux, or whose character is not suitable for a catheter.
- Before surgery be sure there is no occlusion of the deep vein (secondary varicose vein).
- Open procedure: High ligation + venous stripping (HL/S):
  1. High great saphenous vein ligation: to disconnect the SFJ from the common femoral vein so there is no reflux of blood; the issue is whether it is necessary to ligate all proximal tributaries (6 branches) within 2 cm before the SFJ or not: some begin to argue that it may not be necessary, but for the author, according to Rutherford, ligate all proximal tributaries, for the reason that we fear the branch we did not ligate will connect to the saphenous stump and neovascularization will arise. Figure: 3-4 cm groin incision to tie off the main saphenofemoral junction; stripper passed through the vein segment to be removed; 1 cm incision below the knee (skip).
  2. Venous stripping: pull the vein out only from groin to knee; do not strip below the knee (remember), because there is a risk of damaging the saphenous nerve, a sensory nerve running next to the GSV below the knee; if hit, the patient has numbness down the medial side of the leg to the ankle. Figure: vein ligation and stripping (stripper enters and exits through groin incision; stripper head attached at calf incision; ligated venous branches) (skip).
  3. Phlebectomy or stab avulsion: managing the GSV below the knee and the SSV, which is not suitable for venous stripping: the method is to make a small skin incision about 3 mm with a knife and use a small-tipped clamp to grab and pull that varicose vein out. Figure: steps A-E (skip).
- Aftercare: walk right away, but wrap an elastic bandage day and night for about 1 week.
- For short saphenous reflux: surgery for short saphenous varicose vein is standard treatment, by ligating the short saphenous vein 3-5 cm before it reaches the sapheno-popliteal junction; venous stripping is not necessary because the short saphenous vein lies close to the sural nerve, a very sensitive sensory nerve: if the vein is pulled and the sural nerve injured, the patient has numbness along the lateral side of the leg; endovenous ablation still awaits long term results.
- If a varicose vein of the leg has ruptured and bleeds: initial treatment: wrap with an elastic bandage then elevate the leg first; if still not controlled, do suture ligation first, then later treat the varicose vein as surgical Rx.
- Newer technology, e.g. transilluminated powered phlebectomy to find the position of the group of varicose veins to be removed, or a new surgical method, selective varices ablation by cutting only the segment with reflux: left to those in the field; no need to know in detail.
- Summary: choosing the ablation method of the greater saphenous vein: from many data, results from studies agree that endovenous ablation, laser or RFA, has efficacy and safety not different from conventional surgery (HL/S); guidelines from NICE, SVS (Society of Vascular Surgery) and American Venous Forum agree that:
  1. Choose endovenous ablation, laser or RFA, first.
  2. A patient unsuitable for endovenous ablation: do UGFS.
  3. A patient unsuitable for UGFS: do HL/S.
- Summary again, choosing the ablation method of the greater saphenous vein by looking at the GSV character:
  1. GSV fairly straight, not tortuous: choose HL/S, laser or RFA; but if one can choose, choose endovenous ablation first.
  2. GSV lying close to the skin, with history of superficial thrombophlebitis (the vein is often occluded and cannot take the catheter), or size over 1.5 mm [as printed]: choose HL/S.
- For Thailand: where endovenous treatment cannot be done, there must be HL/S only.
- Guideline for treatment of varicose vein from Rutherford, Decision Making in Vascular Surgery. Figure (algorithm), as steps: patient with varicose veins -> clinical evaluation -> significant edema, skin changes, ulcer? No: other valid indications for intervention? No: follow, consider stockings; Yes: physical exam, Trendelenburg test indicate isolated greater saphenous vein incompetence? -> noninvasive studies/duplex scan. ## Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)
- A disease arising from abnormal veins with venous hypertension, making the spaces between endothelial cells widen, RBCs and WBCs leak out around the vessel; the red cells then break down and release hemosiderin, which stains the skin and is the target of cutaneous abnormalities: swelling, a dark colour, thickening, hardening; the WBCs release necrotizing lysosomal enzymes causing tissue destruction, ending as an ulcer.
- Leg swelling is a pitting type, or goes down on raising the leg; besides CVI, it is also found in those with heart disease causes, but those with heart causes swell on both sides; the typical finding of venous origin is swelling with hyperpigmentation and cicatricial change: the skin looks thick and hard (subcutaneous fibrosis) around the ankle, while the toes and dorsum of the foot in CVI patients do not swell much, differing from lymphedema, where the dorsum of the foot usually swells.
- Causes of CVI:
  1. Congenital, e.g. valve aplasia followed by reflux.
  2. Venous obstruction.
  3. Post-thrombotic: following DVT (venous obstruction), making the valves destroyed until they cannot close: both reflux with obstruction; in the USA obstruction is found more than reflux, but in Thailand we find reflux more than obstruction.
- Clinical classification: from C4-C6.
- Diagnosis: relies on clinical findings, comprising hyperpigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis, venous ulcer; and it is necessary to confirm the diagnosis with duplex US in every case.
- Remember: hyperpigmentation in CVI arises from hemosiderin deposition.
- Venous ulcer: arises from prolonged venous hypertension stimulating macrophages and monocytes to release many cytokines, causing inflammation of the subcutaneous tissue and finally an ulcer; the location of the ulcer is above and posterior to the medial malleolus about 3 cm; the lateral malleolus is possible but rare; we see that the cause of a chronic venous ulcer can be diagnosed from physical examination.
- Features of a venous ulcer:
  1. A moist wound with much granulation, a shallow wound not reaching fascia, with browny edema from hemosiderin deposition.
  2. Has features of CVI, e.g. varicose veins, subcutaneous fibrosis around the wound.
  3. The wound is usually at the gaiter area, which is the position of the 1st perforator connecting the superficial and deep systems.
- Investigation: should be done in every case from CEAP C3 upward, or with a history of DVT; we send to confirm whether reflux is real or CVI arises from obstruction, and from the investigation we can assess the severity of the reflux or obstruction:
  1. Duplex Doppler US: the standard diagnostic study: have the patient sit, not bearing weight; put a high frequency B-mode ultrasound probe together with Doppler US pressing along the length of the vein; duplex US will show the anatomy, the size of the superficial, deep and perforator veins, the amount and direction of blood flow in the vein; it can examine reflux and obstruction of superficial and deep veins; besides, the advantage of duplex US is that it is a noninvasive study, so it suits screening in patients we suspect to have CVI.
     - At present duplex is regarded as the most appropriate test for examining CVI; duplex works so much better that venography is hardly needed any more.
     - Limitations of duplex: 1. operator dependent; 2. obese patients (difficult to read); 3. cannot examine deep-lying veins such as the iliac vein or veins in the abdominal cavity: if one wants to see them, must use CTV or MRV.
  2. Venography: used in cases where duplex US gives no answer:
     - 2.1 Ascending venography: tie a tourniquet at the ankle, inject contrast at the foot below the tourniquet, force the contrast to run into the deep vein, to look at deep vein obstruction but cannot tell reflux.
     - 2.2 Descending venography: inject contrast into the common femoral vein below the groin in a patient lying head up, or standing: if there is reflux, blood will flow back down toward the foot.
     - Summary: ascending looks at obstruction; descending looks at reflux.
  3. For other investigations, e.g. venous pressure and foot volumetry measurement, which the residents rarely meet: not needed (the author's aside).
- The role of CTV and intravenous US is little used.
- For CEAP C1 no investigation: just look and touch.

## Management of CVI
- 2 main treatments: nonoperative and operative.

### Nonoperative Treatment
1. Lifestyle modification: do the same as for varicose vein, by avoiding standing or sitting with the legs hanging down for long periods; when sleeping, raise or prop up the legs high; tell the patient their life from now on will need compression stockings for life (stockings, not rubber bags).
2. Compression therapy: the main treatment: makes the blood not stagnate in the leg, and so reduces the pressure of the veins, speeding wound healing, using gradual compressive stocking, elastic bandage, paste gauze boot or Unna boot and pneumatic compression device; at present it is recommended to start compression treatment from C1 with symptoms, or C2 and above.
   - Levels of pressure used, divided into 4 levels:
     1. Mild or light compression: pressure 10-20 mmHg: suits C1 with symptoms such as pain or swollen leg, and used to prevent DVT in people who must sit a long time, or move little, bedridden, or after surgery.
     2. Moderate compression: pressure 21-30 mmHg: suits C2-C3 with leg pain or swelling, or after injecting sclerosant in C1.
     3. Strong compression: pressure 31-40 mmHg: suits people at risk of venous ulcer after DVT, or after open superficial venous surgery, or after endovenous treatment.
     4. Very strong compression: pressure over 40 mmHg: suits C6 with an ulcer, to speed ulcer healing rate.
   - If CVI is only swelling without an ulcer yet, start with medical grade elastic compression stocking 30-40 mmHg first; besides reducing pressure, reducing swelling and adding blood to feed the wound, it helps reduce pain and helps venous ulcers heal 70-90%; but recurrence is high (50%) because people cannot wear it tightly all the time.
   - Remember: before compression Rx there must be no evidence of arterial occlusion, especially a patient with leg pain, wound pain, palpable but faint pulse, ABI under 0.6: fix with CTA together with solving the arterial occlusion problem first, then deal with the venous ulcer afterward.
   - Wrapping: use elastic bandage or wrap with 2-4 kinds of material layered (2 or 4 layers): innermost layer is the layer that lays gauze over the venous ulcer, then wrap with roll gauze that is slightly stretchy so the cover does not slide; first layer with webril to absorb wound drainage like a sanitary pad; second layer with elastic bandage; third layer with coband bandage or elastic bandage; wrap from the tip of the foot upward, to achieve pressure of 30-40 mmHg. Figure: wrapped leg (skip); figure: compression stocking pressure gradient (ankle 18 mmHg, calf 14 mmHg, popliteal 8 mmHg, lower thigh 10 mmHg, upper thigh 8 mmHg) (skip).
   - Use of compression stocking:
     1. Suits C2 and above: to maintain pressure for results, wear it day and night, except at bedtime; the important thing is the stocking must be changed every 3 months (remember); the reason it does not work is that people have only one stocking, but use it for a year; however it is, it loosens and wears out, not good.
     2. But if it is already a wound, must wear the stocking with tight pressure all 24 hours.
     3. C5 use stocking with pressure 20-30 mmHg; C6 use stocking with pressure 35-40 mmHg.
   - But if the pressure needed is more than 40 mmHg (very strong compression), should use an Unna boot, which is like a slab with a bandage, a popular effective compression; the person wrapping the paste bandage must be professional, changing the bandage weekly; Unna boot suits old people who cannot dress the wound or change the compression stocking themselves; from RCT, paste gauze boot makes wounds heal better than elastic compression stocking; remember: we use Unna boot from C5 upward, i.e. there must be a wound; if there is no wound yet, do not use Unna boot. Figure: Unna-Flex box and application (skip).
3. Wound care: method of treating the venous ulcer:
   1. Use NSS to clean the wound so that the wound is moist enough; if very wet, cover the wound with foam to reduce wetness, then compression Rx.
   2. Avoid antiseptic or topical antibiotic, because it often causes irritation or allergy, making the wound heal even more slowly.
   3. If there is eczema around the wound, apply topical steroid.
   4. Debridement: only the part with dead tissue.
   5. If there is infection (the wound discharges a lot, pain, swelling, redness, heat), culture together with giving antibiotic (normally not needed); apply zinc paste or povidone to the wound to kill germs, or cover with a modern dressing with silver; but forbidden to use antibiotic cream or apply at the wound.
4. Drugs commonly used:
   - 4.1 Pentoxifylline: helps reduce platelet activation, so reduces inflammation of veins; but its role in treatment is still not clear; it is believed that using this drug together with wrapping helps wounds heal faster.
   - 4.2 Phlebotropic drug: the flavonoid group (daflon) acts directly at the wall and venous valve, reducing pain and swelling well.
   - 4.3 Diuretic: only adjunctive Rx.
   - 4.4 Aspirin.
   - Note: drug use is only adjunctive Rx, not the main treatment; the key point: if meeting an MCQ and asked to choose one best, one should not go for the answer with the drug.
5. Surgical treatment:
   - Indication: 1. conservative treatment full and still not better (over 3-6 months); 2. the patient cannot do full conservative treatment, usually compression Rx, wrapping all day and night, cannot tolerate the hot sweaty feeling; 3. young patient who wants to return to work soon; 4. CVI class 4 and above.

### Surgical Treatment of CVI
- Before surgery, should do duplex Doppler US first to see where the location of the failed valve is: 1. is it at the superficial, perforator or deep vein; 2. is the failed valve partly failed or totally failed.
- Superficial vein reflux:
  1. Conventional Rx: comprising high ligation, phlebectomy and stab avulsion like treating varicose veins.
  2. Minimal invasive Rx: endovenous treatment with laser, RFA giving heat to destroy the venous wall; results also look good but reports are still few, so surgery is better.
- Perforator vein reflux: treat in a case of pathological reflux: looking at duplex finds venous reflux longer than 0.5 sec, perforator size larger than 3.5 mm, and the perforator is the one lying under the venous ulcer; treatment helps wounds heal faster and prevents recurrence of the wound:
  1. Minimal invasive surgery: with an endoscope inserted via the upper lateral side of the leg at skin that is not thick or inflamed, to cut the perforator vein in the subfascial layer, a method called subfascial endoscopic perforator vein surgery or SEPS.
  2. Open perforator ligation or Linton procedure: an open operation used 30-40 years ago, making a long incision on the medial side through thick, inflamed skin and soft tissue to reach and ligate the perforator (done in the author's young staff days); but open surgery often has wound healing problems, so it is not popular now.
  3. Percutaneous ablation of perforators (PAPS): inserting a needle through the skin with duplex US as a guide; when the failed perforator is found, use RFA, laser, or US guided foam sclerotherapy to make the perforator occlude; this method is popular nowadays because it is the most minimally invasive.
  4. In a case where both superficial and perforator fail, there is still argument about what to do, but generally:
     - 4.1 If CEAP C5: recommended to ligate or ablate only the superficial venous system, then assess results whether it is necessary to deal with the perforator problem, because in most, after surgery of the superficial system, the perforator reflux disappears by itself; if not, one must come to a 2nd round.
     - 4.2 But if CEAP C6: should treat them together because the wound heals faster.
- Deep vein: the deep vein problem is more complicated than superficial and perforator vein problems: besides choosing the operation between open or conventional surgery and minimally invasive surgery, one must still look at whether the lesion at the deep vein has a cause of obstruction or from loss of the deep vein valve causing reflux; because deep vein surgery is something the teacher should know some surgical methods of, the author writes here.

### Deep Vein Surgery
- A difficult operation for people not familiar with it; choosing the method depends on whether the problem arises from obstruction or reflux.
- Obstruction problem:
  1. Conventional surgery
  - How to fix deep vein obstruction: open venous bypass with the opposite-side GSV or PTFE crossing the pubis to the opposite leg, e.g. fem-fem venous bypass, called the Palma-Dale procedure, or by venous transposition; the problem often found after surgery is blockage of the venous graft, because venous blood has low pressure and flows slowly, so it clots easily.
  - Some recommend adding an adjunctive AV fistula at the lower side, e.g. using the posterior tibial artery joined with the posterior tibial vein, to increase blood flow in the venous graft, so that it does not clot easily.
  - Opening the vein to remove the thrombus then closing with a venous patch: this method is used less than open bypass.
  2. Minimal invasive surgery: at present percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and stenting are done more, because endovascular access is more available with good tools, and the operative risk is low.
     - Some reports recommend using it as the first choice, by placing an endovenous stent, suitable for obstruction of veins lying above the groin, e.g. the iliac vein, starting by inserting a catheter into the vein then dilating at the obstruction; because veins are thin-walled they collapse easily, so it is advised to leave a stent in place.
     - For veins lying below the groin toward the upper leg, e.g. the femoral vein, one should choose open surgery, because at the groin and upper leg there is much movement, which makes the stent fold or break easily.
- Reflux problem:
  1. Conventional surgery:
     - 1.1 Valvuloplasty or repair of the valve: suits valves that are still not badly damaged, still able to move a little; the repair has both internal repair and external repair.
     - 1.2 Valve transplantation: used with a deep vein whose valves are completely damaged.
  2. Minimal invasive surgery: implanting a bioprosthetic valve like changing a leaking heart valve, by placing a valve in the prepared vein either via a catheter (endovenous treatment) or by open surgery. Figure: bioprosthetic valve (skip).
- Valvuloplasty:
  1. Internal repair: used for venous valves that are not badly damaged, by opening the vein and going in to suture the valve with prolene 7-0 to make the loose part tighter, also called commissuroplasty; this kind of repair has ulcer healing 70%, a better result than external repair. Figure: internal repair steps (skip).
  2. External repair: by suturing the vein valve from the outer side at the commissure; this method suits small veins. Figure (skip).
- External banding: open in, then use a Dacron or PTFE artificial vessel to tie a venous segment that is dilated at the position near the valve; tying makes the lumen narrower.
- Valve transposition: transposing a deep vein whose valve is damaged beyond repair to attach to a normal vein lying beside it, at a position below the valve of that usable vein, e.g. attaching the SFV below the valve of the deep femoral vein or great saphenous vein. Figure (skip).
- Valve transplantation: used in a case where the valve is damaged until it cannot be repaired and a neighboring vein cannot be used: by transplantation, we cut out a segment of axillary vein that still has a good valve, and move that cut axillary vein to connect at the leg in place of the original diseased one. Figure (skip).

Obstruction: venography +/- IVUS -> percutaneous venoplasty +/- stenting -> venous bypass for failure.
- In summary: for the classification of CVI use CEAP, but for assessing the clinical response to treatment use the venous severity score, which Rutherford and colleagues reported in J Vasc 2000, looking at symptoms, leg swelling, wound and others to assess how the result is after treatment, dividing severity into grades 0 to 3.

## Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)
- Means the formation of a thrombus in a deep vein.
- Deep vein system means veins at the leg, arm, pelvic vein, portal and mesenteric vein, hepatic vein, renal vein and pulmonary vein.
- What will be written in this chapter focuses on the leg, then the arm.
- The severe problem of DVT, to the point of death, is when the thrombus dislodges to block the pulmonary artery, called pulmonary emboli (PE), or massive thrombus occludes the iliofemoral vein causing phlegmasia cerulea dolens, where the leg swells tight until it obstructs venous blood flow, followed by a blue leg (venous gangrene).
- Venous thromboembolism (VTE) means DVT together with PE.
- Distal DVT means DVT at the calf vein (below the knee).
- Proximal DVT means DVT from the popliteal vein and going upward.
- Mechanism of thrombosis: 3 factors, or Virchow triad:
  1. Stasis: those who lie in bed a long time, especially cervical spine fracture with cord compression, have the highest risk, more than fracture pelvis, which is next; besides, CHF, pelvic tumor, large masses.
  2. Endothelial injury: makes platelets aggregate with activation of the coagulation mechanism; the cause may be trauma to the vein, sepsis or vasculitis, followed by release of inflammatory substances.
  3. Hypercoagulability: arising from blood that clots faster than normal, from old age, cancer, hormone or thrombophilia.
- Thrombophilia: arises from abnormal proteins, deficiency of protein C, protein S and antithrombin III, which have the duty to inhibit thrombus formation and help lyse clots; in this group we find factor V Leiden deficiency [as printed] is the commonest cause.
- Risk factors for VTE (figure):

| Strong | Moderate | Weak |
|---|---|---|
| Fracture of pelvis, hip, or long bones of leg; hip or knee arthroplasty; major general surgery; major trauma; spinal cord injury | arthroscopic knee surgery; central venous lines; congestive heart failure; estrogen therapy; malignancy; paralytic stroke; pregnancy/postpartum; genetic thrombophilia | bed rest >3 days; prolonged immobility; age; laparoscopic surgery; obesity; varicose veins |
- Notes on risk factors:
  1. Obesity alone is not a risk factor unless very obese and with other risk factors together.
  2. The pill that adds risk is the 3rd generation progestin desogestrel group.
  3. Economy class syndrome has a 2-4 times higher risk of VTE.
- Pathophysiology of DVT: thrombosis in deep veins often starts at the small pockets of the vein called the sinus of calf vein (soleus sinusoid), then blood flow is interrupted causing venous stasis; if there is fibrinolysis the clot is lysed; but if clot lysis is not in time the clot extends, becoming larger and longer (propagation of thrombus); the specific problems are leg pain, leg swelling, or the clot dislodging to cause PE.
- Paradoxical emboli: DVT at the leg dislodges up to the heart, and when the clot passes a patent foramen ovale to the left heart and leaves the heart, it blocks various places, becoming arterial emboli; the clot in the vein that passes through an ASD hole to block an artery is called paradoxical emboli.
- Clinical manifestation of DVT:
  1. Limb swelling of the whole leg is the important symptom (unilateral pitting edema).
  2. Calf muscle pain on squeezing or dorsiflexion of the foot (positive Homan's sign), found 40%.
  3. The temperature of the leg is warmer than the other side.
  4. If DVT occurs at the iliac vein, the leg swells, turns milky white (milk leg) with pain, called phlegmasia alba dolens; the leg turning white is believed to arise from vasospasm of the artery.
  5. If left alone, the pressure of the leg continues to rise because blood can go in but cannot return, the leg swells tightly until venous gangrene. Figure: patient photo (skip).
- Diagnosis: diagnosing DVT by clinical symptoms: OPD cases are diagnosed more easily than IPD cases; however, the problem found is that the clinical symptoms of DVT such as pain and swelling are not specific, with accuracy of only about 40%.
- So to make the diagnosis more correct, the modified Well's score is used and gives points; from the ACCP meeting in 2012 it was recommended to use the modified Well's score to assess every patient suspected of DVT.
- Table (Modified Wells Criteria: clinical evaluation table for predicting the probability of a DVT): active cancer +1; paralysis, paresis, or recent plaster immobilization of the lower extremities +1; recently bedridden for three days or major surgery within the last 12 weeks +1; localized tenderness along the deep venous system +1; entire leg swollen +1; calf swelling 3 cm or more larger than asymptomatic side +1; pitting edema confined to symptomatic leg +1; collateral superficial veins +1; previously documented DVT +1; alternative diagnosis at least as likely as a DVT -2.
  - Clinical probability of DVT as printed in the figure: "Likely <2; Unlikely >=2" (the figure's labels appear reversed; the text below gives the intended reading).
  - By the text: 2 points or less (low probability): not likely DVT; more than 2 points (high probability): likely DVT.
  - Note: if after examining one thinks another disease is more likely than VTE, subtract 2 points from the total score.
  - In the low risk group of the modified Wells criteria, and the intermediate risk of the original Wells criteria, there is a low chance of DVT: send D-dimer: if D-dimer is less than 500 ng/mL there is a very low chance of DVT, no need for further investigation; but if D-dimer is more than 500 ng/mL the chance of DVT is moderate, needing further investigation, e.g. sending duplex or compression US; the high risk group does not need D-dimer: send compression US straight away.
- Siriraj DVT score: worth knowing (from Siriraj teachers): uses symptoms and signs; the author thinks it is interesting because in practice it is easy to assess, with sensitivity and specificity above 70%.
  - Siriraj criteria: confinement to bed, unilateral limb pain, previous DVT, calf enlargement over 3 cm compared with the other side: 1 point each.
  - If score under 2: low probability group; if score 2 or more: high probability group: send duplex to confirm the diagnosis.
- Figure (algorithm), as steps: suspect DVT -> Well score: low/moderate risk: D-dimer: negative: no DVT; positive: compression US. High risk: compression US: positive: Rx DVT; negative: repeat US at 7 days: negative: no DVT; positive: Rx DVT.
- In the case of high probability but duplex US cannot be sent at once: if no contraindication, give an anticoagulant by injection first, then run to ask the radiologist for a queue jump to do duplex as fast as possible.
- If the radiologist is not free, stuck shopping, or in a hurry to pick up their child at school: the point is, if there is a vein to be found, use US which is not expensive, pressing the vein, called compression US; or if a vascular fellow is there, use the fresh strength of the fellow, help by asking them to do it.
- D-dimer: a substance arising from the breakdown of fibrin clot in the thrombolysis process; finding D-dimer shows thrombosis must have occurred, so sensitivity is over 90%; but D-dimer can also be found in other conditions such as trauma, cancer, inflammation, so specificity is only 50%.
  - Cut point at 500 ng/mL: if under 500 count D-dimer as negative; if equal to or over 500 count it as positive; so D-dimer is only a screening test; if high risk by Well score, send US straight away, no need for D-dimer.
  - D-dimer negative: can rule out DVT; D-dimer positive: send compression US next.
- Duplex US: the most objective criterion in diagnosing DVT: if there is no DVT, pressing the probe on the vein flattens the vein and shows blood flow in the vessel; but if there is a thrombus in that vein, pressing the probe does not flatten it, and echo is seen in the vein; when color flow is turned on, no blood flow is seen: that is definitely DVT. Figure: compression ultrasound diagram (artery/vein, normal compressible vein, thrombus) (skip).
  - Limitations of compression US: iliac vein or veins in the abdomen, in an obese patient, a muscular person, athlete: cannot be pressed to the vein, or a patient with much bowel gas; or a distal DVT in the calf: seen unclearly because the clot is too small; and another limitation: cannot separate old from new thrombus.
  - Caution: before pressing one must see the venous wall clearly; if not clear and pressing down, it may push the clot loose.
- Ascending venogram: invasive, so it is not used much now, because non-invasive investigation, especially duplex US, gives almost all the answers. Figure: venogram (skip).
- Duplex scan: done together with compression US to see color flow: sensitivity and specificity over 95%; so it is the one best investigation of DVT of the leg: the answer is duplex, the diagnostic test of choice. Figure: color duplex of common femoral thrombus (skip).
- MRV or CTV: also see clearly but are a bit expensive; should be a secondary choice, or when we need to see the veins in the abdominal cavity.
- Isotope scan of fibrinogen has many limits for leg DVT because it cannot separate active thrombosis from an inflammatory process, so it is not popular.
- Investigation of thrombophilia, e.g. protein C, protein S, antithrombin III and factor V Leiden: not to be sent routinely; send only for cases that are recurrent VTE.

## Anticoagulant
- Residents should know about 4: 1. unfractionated heparin (UFH); 2. low molecular weight heparin (LMWH); 3. oral anticoagulant (warfarin); 4. new drugs, e.g. fondaparinux sodium (Arixta) [Arixtra].

### Unfractionated heparin (UFH)
- Mechanism: enhances the function of antithrombin III to inhibit the function of thrombin and inactivate factor X, thus blocking the clotting factors; while LMWH inhibits factor Xa.
- Usually given IV or SC; UFH has a short half life of only 60-90 minutes.
- Monitor with activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) to keep in the therapeutic level, aPTT ratio 1.5-2.5 times the normal; start checking aPTT 6 hours after giving UFH until the wanted value is reached, then continue once a day.
- Dose of the drug: "eighty eighteen" (the author's mnemonic): 1. initial dose IV 80 unit/kg/hour [as printed; the table below gives it as the bolus]; 2. maintenance dose 18 unit/kg/hr.
- If aPTT is over 2.5 or the effect of UFH needs reversing, stop the heparin, and follow with: 1. titration with protamine SO4 1 mg for every 1,000 units of UFH, i.e. about 10 mg of protamine; 2. give FFP.

### Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH)
- Has a molecular weight about 3 times lower than UFH.
- Mechanism: inhibits factor Xa and thrombin.
- Given by subcutaneous injection; the drug has a long half life, so injecting 1-2 times a day is OK.
- No need to monitor with aPTT like UFH, while LMWH gives effect about the same as UFH and has fewer complications such as HIT and osteoporosis than UFH.
- In cases where we want to monitor LMWH, draw blood to check active factor Xa; since LMWH is excreted by the kidney, use with care in patients with renal impairment: adjust the dose reduction by creatinine clearance, whereas UFH can be used in renal failure patients.
- In a patient on LMWH who needs surgery, wait about 12 hours for the drug to wear off, unless urgent, then use protamine SO4 1 mg/100 units of LMWH.
- In a patient having surgery with epidural or spinal anesthesia who needs LMWH after surgery, the drug can be given 24 hours after the block.
- Dose of the drug: the one used often is enoxaparin sodium (Clexane):
  1. Rx VTE: 1 mg/kg bid SC for 10 days, or 1.5 mg/kg SC for 10 days (as printed).
  2. Prophylaxis VTE: 20-40 mg SC injected 2 hours before surgery, then given after surgery about 6-12 hours, OD.

### Complications of UFH & LMWH
1. Thrombocytopenia (written extra because the teacher likes to ask). 2. Arterial thrombosis. 3. Osteoporosis. 4. Skin necrosis can also occur.
- Remember: UFH and LMWH have no teratogenic effect, unlike warfarin.

### Heparin Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)
- Found in 2-5% of patients given UFH and LMWH, but the chance of occurrence with UFH is more.
- Cause believed to be autoimmune, with heparin as the trigger producing antibody against platelet factor-4 (PF4), stimulating aggregation and thrombosis.
- The problem mostly occurs on days 5-7, except in re-exposure, where HIT occurs within 1-2 days.
- Acute HIT has 2 forms: 1. mild type, usually found on day 4 after heparin: platelet level still over 100,000; 2. severe type, usually found on day 4-14 after heparin: platelet under 100,000 or down more than 50% from baseline.
- The problem of thrombocytopenia in HIT is mostly not bleeding, but platelet activation causing thrombosis.
- So when giving UFH or LMWH one should check platelets, starting on day 4: if platelet under 100,000 to 150,000 or down from baseline more than 50%, suspect HIT.
- Confirm the diagnosis by finding HIT antibody PF4, or its full name, platelet factor 4 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.
- Treatment (remember):
  1. Stop giving heparin and avoid heparin from now on (LMWH also forbidden); a heparin lock left in to inject IV drugs: pull it all out.
  2. Change to fondaparinux, which acts directly by inhibiting factor Xa, or a direct thrombin inhibitor group such as lepirudin, bivalirudin; but these are excreted by the kidney, so are forbidden in chronic renal failure; another drug that can substitute is argatroban, which is metabolized in the liver, so suits renal patients, but if the liver is not good, or after hepatectomy, it should not be used.

### Warfarin
- An oral anticoagulant, a vitamin K antagonist (VKA).
- Mechanism: 1. inhibits synthesis of vitamin K dependent factors (2, 7, 9, 10); beware the trap is factor V too [as printed]; 2. inhibits protein C and protein S.
- The drug has a half life of 24-72 hours but the full anticoagulation effect of warfarin takes about 3 days after starting; so in the first part of treating DVT, give warfarin together with UFH or LMWH.
- Monitor with PT to get INR 2-3 and adjust the dose by INR every 2 weeks until the INR is steady, then check INR once a month.
- Dose used: start 5 mg for the first 2-3 days, then adjust the dose by INR.
- Dose adjustment of VKA (figure; measured INR, target 2.0-3.0): INR under 1.5: increase total weekly dose by 10-15%; 1.5-1.9: increase by 5-10%; 2.0-3.0: no change; 3.1-3.5: decrease by 0-15%; 3.6-4.5: decrease by 5-20%.
- Must stop warfarin if INR is over 4.5-10, waiting for INR to return to normal first, then resume.
- INR over 10: stop warfarin and give vitamin K 2.5 mg.
- INR over 20 or serious bleeding: stop warfarin then inject vit. K1 IV together with FFP.
- Drug interaction with warfarin: 1. enhancement effect (PT prolonged): cimetidine, PPI, cephalosporin, flagyl, sulfa; 2. inhibition effect (PT shortened): barbiturate, rifampin, griseofulvin, carbamazepine.
- Complications of warfarin: 1. abnormal bleeding; 2. skin necrosis from occlusion of tiny vessels, usually found in the first 2-3 days; 3. teratogenic effect, so forbidden in pregnant people.
- In pregnant people, the drug used with VTE is LMWH.
- A patient taking warfarin who will have surgery or tooth extraction must stop the drug; but the reversal of the drug takes about 4-5 days for INR to return to normal; if rapid reversal is wanted, in not over 24 hours, counteract with IV vitamin K1 10 mg together with FFP; after surgery slowly restart warfarin; but if still unable to eat, change to heparin first, and when the patient is stable and can eat, slowly restart warfarin.

### New Oral Anticoagulants
- New anticoagulants, classed as direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs); the drugs in this group often end with "aban":
  1. Dabigatran (150 mg): an anti factor IIa (thrombin); Schwartz edition 11 cheers this drug clearly: 1 tablet morning and evening.
  2. And rivaroxaban (15 mg): 1 tablet morning and evening for 3 weeks, then 20 mg OD continuing.
- DOACs differ from warfarin in that DOACs are anti factor Xa (rivaroxaban), while warfarin inhibits factors II, VII, IX, X and protein C and protein S.
- Both new oral drugs have a shorter half life than warfarin because this group acts quickly: after heparin is given a full 5 days, heparin can be stopped and DOACs started the next day.
- Advantages of DOACs: 1. besides acting quickly, the level of effect is steady, fixed oral dosing possible; 2. no need for lab monitoring; 3. treatment results look rather safe, especially bleeding from adverse effect of the drug, found less than warfarin; 4. fewer adverse reactions with other drugs.
- Disadvantages of both drugs: no direct antidote to reverse the drug's effect, must wait for it to be metabolized, or do hemodialysis; no data yet on long term effect; besides, they are excreted via the placenta and breast milk, so forbidden in pregnant people and breastfeeding.
- At present in America it is recommended to use DOACs instead of warfarin, but in our country the price of this drug group is too expensive, so we must keep taking warfarin.

### Fondaparinux Sodium (Arixta)
- A new anticoagulant used to treat DVT and for prophylaxis well.
- Mechanism: 1. factor Xa inhibitor, so less thrombin is produced; 2. increases the activity of antithrombin III.
- Dose: inject 5-10 mg subcutaneous once daily; but the drawback is that the drug is excreted by the kidney, so should not be used in cases with creatinine clearance under 30 mL/min.
- Can be used in HIT.

## Treatment of DVT
- Aim of treatment: 1. reduction of limb swelling; 2. stopping clot propagation; 3. prevention of pulmonary embolism; 4. prevention of venous gangrene; 5. prevention of recurrent DVT; 6. prevention of CVI.
- Early thrombus resolution improves long term outcome (remember).
- Treatment modalities of DVT:
  1. Start with supportive treatment: bed rest, leg elevation, have the patient wear stocking or wrap an elastic bandage.
  2. Anticoagulant therapy.
  3. Thrombolytic therapy.
  4. Venous thrombectomy.
  5. Insertion of caval filter.
- The issue about bed rest: new reports say it is not necessary: if the patient can move, let them move; it is found that early ambulation gives less pain and the leg swelling goes down faster than telling the patient to lie in bed all the time.

### Anticoagulant therapy
- The main treatment consists of injecting heparin and taking warfarin, giving heparin at a dose with aPTT 1.5-2.5 times the normal level (start within 24 hours); at the same time start oral warfarin together with heparin.
- Duration of giving:
  1. Heparin is given at least 5 days to wait for warfarin to work first; once warfarin reaches the therapeutic level, i.e. PT at 2-3 times the normal level for at least 24 hours, heparin can be turned off.
  2. How long warfarin is given depends on the cause and risk factors:
     - 2.1 If there is a risk with a precipitating cause, or provoked VTE, e.g. sitting on an airplane or tour bus a long time, or occurring after a fracture, when the bone has healed and the patient can walk well, the risk is gone: this group is usually given the drug for 3 months.
     - 2.2 First time but no clear risk found (non-provoked VTE): we usually give the drug more than 3-6 months.
     - 2.3 Cancer: should give the drug longer than 3 months or until the course of treatment ends, then at least 6 months more; may give the drug for life, assessing the benefit and the harm of giving it long term.
     - 2.4 Recurrent DVT: should give the drug for life, assessing the benefit and the harm of long term giving, e.g. risk of bleeding.
  3. While injecting LMWH and then recurrent DVT occurs: increase the dose of LMWH by 25-30%.
  4. While taking warfarin at the therapeutic level and recurrent DVT occurs: change to LMWH for at least 1 month.

### Thrombolytic therapy
- The use of thrombolytic drugs; 3 drugs are used often: streptokinase, urokinase, and the one used today, recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (r-TPA), by injecting r-TPA through a catheter into the clot (catheter directed thrombolysis); the drug activates plasminogen into plasmin, which helps dissolve the fibrin clot.
- Advantages of thrombolytic Rx:
  1. Provides rapid thrombus resolution, but must be a case that has been present less than 5 days, with the best result in DVT under 48 hours.
  2. Reduces the occurrence of CVI in the early period, because valve function is still preserved.
  3. Use the drug for not more than 48 hours.
  4. Catheter directed thrombolysis (CDT) suits DVT of the iliofemoral vein; for veins from the femoral vein downward, CDT is not suitable.
- Disadvantages of thrombolysis: 1. bleeding complication; 2. expensive; 3. if present over 72 hours the clot is usually firmly stuck and use does not work much; 4. allergic reaction.
- Giving a thrombolytic drug is given about 48 hours, monitoring the fibrinogen level; after the thrombolytic drug is finished, must treat with anticoagulant afterward.
- Contraindication for thrombolytic Rx:
  1. Still having active bleeding, especially in the brain or having a brain tumor. 2. Head injury, craniotomy, spinal surgery. 3. Recent stroke. 4. Uncontrollable hypertension. 5. Major operation within 10 days. 6. Coagulopathy. 7. Others, e.g. active peptic ulcer, esophageal varices, hypertension, aortic dissection.

### Venous thrombectomy
- A surgery opening the vein and pulling the clot out; indications:
  1. Used with DVT at the femoral and iliac vein that has phlegmasia cerulea dolens.
  2. Or a patient with a contraindication to using anticoagulant.
  3. Or a contraindication to thrombolysis.
- Patients suitable for thrombectomy:
  1. Should be a case not over 72 hours or at most not over 7 days, because if longer than 7 days the clot sticks firmly to the venous wall, so the clot cannot be removed completely and re-thrombosis occurs.
  2. Good functional status and expected life expectancy over 1 year.
- While doing it, beware the clot dislodging to cause PE; so before doing it, give anticoagulant together with an IVC filter placed first to prevent.
- From clinical studies, venous thrombectomy does not reduce pain and swelling better than anticoagulant; so the role of surgery should be obstruction of major veins, namely the subclavian vein at the arm, the iliac and femoral vein at the leg; the purpose is to prevent progress to venous gangrene.
- Contraindication of venous thrombectomy: septic iliofemoral thrombosis following pelvic infection.
- Currently venous thrombectomy is done much less, because catheter directed thrombolysis has come in.

### Caval filter
- Placing inert material into the IVC, fixed to the venous wall, working as a filter for thrombus that dislodges from the leg to help prevent fatal PE.
- Indication:
  1. Contraindication to anticoagulant, e.g. a patient with bleeding in the head who still has iliofemoral DVT to add, or complications from using anticoagulant.
  2. Recurrent pulmonary embolism after receiving anticoagulant in the therapeutic level.
  3. Following pulmonary embolectomy.
- Relative indication for caval filter:
  1. Long floating thrombus over 5 cm in the iliofemoral vein or IVC.
  2. Recurrent septic emboli or infected pulmonary emboli.
  3. Cor pulmonale due to recurrent PE.
  4. Severe pulmonary artery hypertension due to recurrent PE.
  5. RCC with tumor thrombus extending into the renal vein.
- Access site: the femoral vein on the side opposite the DVT leg; puncture, then place the filter under fluoroscopy, positioning the filter just below the renal veins. Figure: steps 1-7 of IVC filter insertion (skip).
- Points of caution:
  1. Craniotomy 4 days earlier and then DVT: Rx: forbidden to give heparin, but should place a caval filter.
  2. A case given anticoagulant who is still at a subtherapeutic level: increase the dose first, do not take him to place a caval filter.
  3. Potential complications of the filter: migration, IVC occlusion, insertion site thrombosis.
  4. A cancer patient with DVT can have a caval filter (not forbidden); septic thromboembolism forbids it.

## Iliofemoral Vein Thrombosis
- Symptoms rather sudden and severe; found in the left leg more than the right, about 2:1.
- From the obstruction the pressure in the leg rises, blood runs to the leg less; at first there is pain, swelling and milky white leg from the groin to the calf, called phlegmasia alba dolens. Figure: patient photo (skip).
- If not treated and left alone, there is propagation of the clot into the iliofemoral vein tributaries until all the venous drainage of that leg is blocked; from the high pressure, arterial inflow to the leg is impaired; the leg swells and turns blue-purple, starting at the toes and spreading up to the groin, called phlegmasia cerulea dolens; if still not treated, venous gangrene occurs, losing the leg or life.
- The patient will be in shock from fluid loss into the interstitial space; if time allows, use US to look.
- Treatment:
  1. Rx shock: hurry to load IV.
  2. Positioning: have the patient lie with the leg raised high.
  3. Best initial Rx is still heparin to prevent clot propagation: IV bolus 5,000 units followed by 500-1,000 units/hour, to keep aPTT about 2.
  4. Catheter directed thrombolysis (CDT): a way that can remove the clot loaded in the iliofemoral vein very quickly; CDT should be for a case present not over 48-72 hours, at most not over 7 days; must be done before venous gangrene occurs; and the patient must have no contraindication to thrombolytic drugs, especially bleeding.
  5. If there is a little time, e.g. only alba dolens: may try percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy first; if unsuccessful, go to the OR for surgical venous thrombectomy.
  6. Venous thrombectomy:
     - 6.1 Make a longitudinal incision at the common femoral vein, then pull the clot out with a venous Fogarty; venous thrombectomy helps reduce pain and swelling if a good case is chosen: 70% of patients get better.
     - 6.2 Patients who should have thrombectomy: those with a contraindication to anticoagulant, or in 6-12 hours the leg looks worse, impending venous gangrene, or proximal iliofemoral vein thrombosis.
- After venous thrombectomy, re-thrombosis occurs often, so it is recommended to make a temporary distal AVF, using a branch of the GSV joined to the superficial femoral artery end-to-side.
- If the patient survives, or the leg is not amputated, wait 6-12 weeks and then close the AVF; early clot removal, whether CDT or thrombectomy, helps prevent post-thrombotic sequelae.
- Figure (algorithm, known iliofemoral DVT), as steps: evaluate activity level, risk, and life expectancy: poor: anticoagulation therapy, leg elevation; progressive symptoms (edema, pain)? No: chronic anticoagulation and compression therapy; Yes: go to iliocavogram. Reasonable: iliocavagram: contraindication for thrombolysis? Yes: venous thrombectomy; No: free-floating caval thrombus? Yes: vena caval filter then catheter-directed thrombolysis; No: catheter-directed thrombolysis: fails: venous thrombectomy; succeeds: treat iliac vein stenosis if present: compartment syndrome? ## Calf Vein Thrombosis
- Means DVT lying below the knee, or below the popliteal vein downward; the chance of PE is low.
- In the past calf vein DVT was treated in every case, but after 2012 it was found that giving anticoagulant gives a benefit of only 15%.
- So the trend is: if there is only isolated calf vein DVT, examine with duplex US once a week, 2 times in a row:
  1. If there is no progression of the clot: conservative Rx.
  2. If there is progression of the clot toward the proximal side first, a thrombus of large size and lying close to the popliteal vein, cancer, or a history of VTE: Rx: give anticoagulant for about 6 weeks.

## Upper Extremity Deep Vein Thrombosis
- 10% of DVT is found at the arm; nowadays it is found more because of C-line punctures or insertion of cardiac devices.
- Primary upper extremity DVT is found 20%, with cause from thoracic outlet syndrome or effort-related thrombosis.
- Secondary upper extremity DVT is found 80%, mostly with cause from C-line punctures or insertion of cardiac devices, e.g. pacemaker, defibrillator, and cancer.
- Diagnosis: from compression US.
- Has fewer complications than leg DVT: PE and post-thrombotic syndrome from arm DVT are less, and recurrence is lower.
- Treatment: DVT with a cause from a line: consider whether the line is still needed: if still necessary, e.g. giving TPN, and the line is not blocked, not infected, it can still be used without needing to take the line off as I used to teach in the past; the most important thing is to give anticoagulant, starting with LMWH 3-5 days together with warfarin 3-6 months; but if the line is blocked, or no need to give IV any more, take the line off.

## Effort Thrombosis (Paget Schroetter Syndrome)
- Thrombosis of the axillary and subclavian vein; found in young people, athletes; male : female = 2:1.
- Cause:
  1. Using the arm or exercising, then compression of the vein against the thoracic outlet; 80% are associated with thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS).
  2. Secondary cause is hypercoagulable state.
  3. DVT of the leg is related to advanced age and obesity, but arm DVT has no relation.
- Symptoms:
  1. Arising quite suddenly from venous obstruction: the arm swells and is tense, pain not very severe, only discomfort, usually; there may be superficial dilated veins.
  2. Chance of PE is about 10%.
  3. Chance of venous gangrene is very small.
- Investigation: 1. the one used often is compression US, sensitivity and specificity 97%, competing with duplex US, which sees thrombus in the axillary and subclavian vein; 2. CTA or MRA for cases with a plan for surgery.
- Treatment:
  1. Start with conservative: rest, arm elevation and give anticoagulant, usually succeeding in the early period.
  2. 80% will have persistent symptoms, the arm still swollen: if no contraindication to thrombolysis, treat with catheter directed thrombolysis.
  3. Catheter clot extraction or surgical thrombectomy are kept for cases with massive clot burden that threatens viability of the arm, or a contraindication to using thrombolysis with anticoagulant.
  4. If there is TOS, do surgical decompression by cutting off the 1st rib and the constricting band.
- Summary of effort thrombosis treatment: IV heparin followed by catheter direct thrombolysis followed by 1st rib resection.

## DVT in Pregnancy
- The drug suitable for pregnant people is LMWH; forbidden: warfarin and DOACs, because of teratogenic effect.
- If DVT is found in the 2nd trimester, start with IV heparin about 5 days then follow with subcutaneous injection of LMWH until delivery.

## Superficial Venous Thrombophlebitis (SVT)
- A disease of thrombus in a superficial vein, associated with DVT, PE, and a possibly hidden cancer.
- Risk factors: varicose veins in the leg, at the site of an IV line; others, e.g. pregnancy, taking the pill, but severity and course of disease differ.

### Post IV SVT
- Superficial thrombophlebitis arising from IV fluids or drug injection through a vein, usually at the arm; the inflammation lies along the line of the IV; low-grade fever, arm pain; the chance of VTE is very small, unlike SVT from varicose veins in the leg. Figure: IV catheter in vein causing redness and inflammation; clot due to trauma to vein; arm photo (skip).
- If there is high fever, discharge, sepsis, high WBC, suspect suppurative thrombophlebitis.
- Treatment:
  1. In the case arising from injection or IV fluids: treat with conservative Rx to reduce pain, swelling, redness with warm compress, NSAIDs, elastic support and ambulation; symptoms often get better within a few weeks; giving antibiotic is not necessary.
  2. But if suppurative thrombophlebitis occurs, showing it has become pus, with discharge, the infected vein must be cut out completely together with draining the pus, and antibiotic must be given.

### SVT with Varicose Vein
- Often at the leg, often following varicose veins; some occur by themselves; associated with both DVT and PE.
- Migratory thrombophlebitis: repeated inflammation moving from place to place, makes one think of underlying disease such as pancreatic cancer, RCC, angiosarcoma, Buerger's disease, periarteritis nodosa, Behcet's disease.
- Diagnosis: from symptoms: pain on the inner side of the leg along the line of the GSV, and examination finds red inflamed patches and a palpable hard cord-like vein.
- The use of duplex US as routine is still debated; the problem is that Rutherford believes it should be done because duplex tells the severity and extent of disease best (the author agrees).
- What everyone agrees on is that if the site of phlebitis lies close to the SFJ, there is a chance the clot will dislodge and go up to cause PE; so on meeting superficial thrombophlebitis near the SFJ, do a duplex.
- Treatment:
  1. In the case occurring in varicose vein, anticoagulant should be given if superficial thrombophlebitis occupies a wide area along the length of the great saphenous vein; some cut off at a clot length over 5 cm, especially thrombus close to the SFJ, which has a chance of PE, giving LMWH for 45 days; but if it is a small spot, short, or only a branch of the superficial vein, this group does not need anticoagulant.
  2. For operation, use it with SVT arising in varicose veins, by doing HL/S.

## Pulmonary Embolism (PE)
- A condition arising from dislodgement of thrombus in a DVT patient up to block the pulmonary artery and its branches.
- Most clots come from leg DVT: 85% from the leg, about 10% from the right atrium, the remaining 5% from the pelvic vein, vena cava or from the arm.
- Clinical manifestations: symptoms depend on the size of the thrombus and the position in the pulmonary artery blocked: if small, or blocking a small branch, there may be no symptoms; medium or large branch: fast breathing, dyspnea, fast pulse, low BP, hypoxia, lung tissue ischemia causing fever, and bloody cough (hemoptysis); but if a large size blocks the trunk, the patient will go into shock and die because blood cannot enter the lung at all. Figure: embolism travels up through IVC to heart and lungs; area of infarct (skip).
- Summary:
  1. Most have no symptoms.
  2. Classic symptoms: dyspnea, chest pain, hypoxia and hemoptysis, found in many diseases, e.g. pneumonia or MI, which reduces specificity.
  3. Symptoms of leg DVT are found little (30%).
- Because patient symptoms are not specific, the same approach as DVT is used, with Well's score to help diagnosis.
- Table (Wells' Criteria for Pulmonary Embolism): clinical signs and symptoms of DVT +3; PE is #1 diagnosis, or equally likely +3; heart rate over 100 +1.5; immobilization at least 3 days, or surgery in the previous 4 weeks +1.5; previous, objectively diagnosed PE or DVT +1.5; hemoptysis +1; malignancy with treatment within 6 months, or palliative +1.
  - 0-1 point: low risk group, PE found 2-4%.
  - 2-6 points: moderate risk, PE found 25-28%.
  - More than 6 points: high risk group, PE found 60-90%.
- Remember the Well score is hard to recall; in practice, if a patient has symptoms and we think of PE as number one together with a history or risk of VTE, the trend is that this patient is probably in the high risk PE group.
- Figure (algorithm), as steps: suspect PE -> clinical scoring: low risk: D-dimer: negative: no PE; positive: CTA. Moderate risk: Rx IV heparin? then CTA. High risk: Rx IV heparin then CTA. CTA negative: no PE; positive: diagnose PE.
- Because PE can kill, high-risk patients are given heparin right away and then investigated with CTA; for moderate risk, giving heparin right away is not wrong; in the low risk group for PE, screen with D-dimer first, and if positive then send CTA.
- The original Wells score is rather complicated, hard to remember, so it was simplified, called the simplified Well score for PE: every item gets 1 point, then patients are classed into only 2 groups: low risk not over 4 points and high risk 4 points or more [as printed in the text; the table below uses 1 point as the cut], and a guideline is then presented that looks practical.
- Table (Wells score, original vs simplified): clinical signs of DVT 3 vs 1; alternative diagnosis less likely than PE 3 vs 1; previous PE or DVT 1.5 vs 1; heart rate over 100 bpm 1.5 vs 1; surgery or immobilization within 4 weeks 1.5 vs 1; hemoptysis 1 vs 1; active cancer 1 vs 1. Clinical probability: PE unlikely: original 4 or less, simplified 1 or less; PE likely: original over 4, simplified over 1.
- Guideline management by simplified Wells score (figure): score 4.0 or less: negative D-dimer: no further testing; positive D-dimer: CT pulmonary angiography or ventilation-perfusion lung scan; score over 4.0: CT pulmonary angiography or V/Q scan directly.

### Investigation (PE)
- D-dimer: like DVT, high sensitivity but low specificity, so it suits screening for the low risk group: if D-dimer negative, PE can be excluded.
- CTA: takes little time; at present the gold standard for PE, with sensitivity and specificity about 97%, helping tell the size and position where the thrombus blocks. Figure: CT with filling defect (skip).
- Isotope lung scan or ventilation perfusion scan: used little now because CTA works better and faster.
- Pulmonary angiogram: used now only in cases that need intervention.
- Echocardiography: suits a patient suspected of PE but not stable enough to send to CTA; even if the clot in the pulmonary artery is not seen, the right ventricle is seen very dilated.
- Cautions about investigation in PE patients:
  1. Biochemical triad of AST, LDH and bilirubin is not reliable.
  2. ABG: low PaO2, and as a result of hyperventilation PaCO2 is low too (not high).
  3. EKG: sinus tachycardia.
  4. CXR: a wedge shaped defect at the infarction area.
  5. Scan: compared with the chest film, the area of infarct shows no perfusion.

### Treatment of PE
- Treatment depends on the patient's state:
  1. If no hypoxia, no shock, still able to talk coherently: give supportive Rx with O2 and IV, then send CTA to look.
  2. Main treatment is to give heparin first: 5,000 unit IV bolus followed by continuous UFH 1,000 unit/hr.
  3. After giving heparin, those with severe symptoms, i.e. severe hypoxia or shock: resuscitate (ABC), give vasopressor, then if the state holds, send CTA.
  4. But if the state cannot hold for CTA, and a cardiologist is in the hospital, ask them to do echo; if echo shows a dilated right heart, it is probably PE; if no contraindication to thrombolysis, give it right away; thrombolysis can be given systemically or by catheter-directed thrombolysis.
  5. But if there is a contraindication to thrombolysis, or the patient's condition cannot wait for the drug to take effect, or thrombolysis was given with no result: must use measures to help remove the clot, such as passing a catheter to suck the clot out, or open the chest and do pulmonary embolectomy (sure to die).
  6. Embolectomy:
     - 6.1 Percutaneous catheter embolectomy: insert a catheter via the internal jugular vein or common femoral vein to suck the clot out; if it does not work, must operate. Figure: catheter embolectomy (skip).
     - 6.2 Pulmonary embolectomy: using cardiopulmonary bypass. Figure: aspirating dissector, patent lumen, obstructed lumen, thromboembolic material removed with forceps; pulmonary thrombus specimen (skip).
  7. Cases where anticoagulant is not appropriate, e.g. just had a craniotomy or active UGI bleeding: place a caval filter.

## Postoperative VTE Prophylaxis
- To assess DVT risk factors in order to consider VTE prophylaxis, the popular one is the Caprini score, revised most recently in 2018, dividing criteria into 4 groups with scores of 1, 2, 3, 5.
- Figure (Caprini risk table, points assigned):
  - 1 point: age 41-60 years; minor surgery; BMI over 25 kg/m2; lower limb edema; varicose veins; pregnancy or postpartum; unexplained or recurrent miscarriage or stillborn infant; OCP or HRT; sepsis (under 1 month); abnormal pulmonary function; acute MI; CHF (under 1 month); IBD; medical patient at bed rest.
  - 2 points: age 61-74 years; arthroscopic surgery; major open or laparoscopic surgery (over 45 min); malignancy; bed rest (over 72 hours); immobilizing plaster cast; central line.
  - 3 points: age 75 years or older; previous VTE; family history of VTE; factor V Leiden; prothrombin 20210A; lupus anticoagulant; cardiolipin antibodies; elevated serum homocysteine; heparin-induced thrombocytopenia; other congenital or acquired thrombophilia.
  - 5 points: stroke (under 1 month); elective arthroplasty; hip, pelvis, or leg fracture; acute spinal cord injury (under 1 month).
  - Evaluation: very low risk 0 points (under 0.5% risk of VTE without prophylaxis); low risk 1-2 points (1.5%); moderate risk 3-4 points (3%); high risk 5 or more points (6%).
- Then assess the VTE risk and divide into 4 groups:
  1. Very low risk: score 0, risk under 0.5%: VTE prophylaxis: early ambulation only.
  2. Low risk: score 1-2, risk 1.5%: VTE prophylaxis: intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC).
  3. Moderate risk: score 3-4, risk 3%: VTE prophylaxis: if not high risk of bleeding, inject LMWH or UFH.
  4. High risk: score 5 or more, risk of VTE 6%: VTE prophylaxis: 4.1 if not high risk of bleeding, inject LMWH or UFH together with IPC or graduated compression stocking; 4.2 but if high risk of bleeding, do IPC only.
- High risk of bleeding includes: 1. active bleeding or previous major bleeding; 2. untreated bleeding disorder; 3. severe renal or liver failure; 4. thrombocytopenia; 5. acute stroke; 6. uncontrollable systemic hypertension; 7. LP or epidural or spinal anesthesia; 8. patients who also receive anticoagulant, antiplatelet or thrombolysis; 9. surgery with dangerous bleeding, e.g. craniotomy, spinal surgery.
- Early ambulation: the method is to elevate the legs together with active flexion and extension of the ankle, helping reduce venous stasis.
- Mechanical prophylaxis: suits those with a contraindication to anticoagulant; 2 ways:
  1. Pneumatic compression device (PCD): a bag wrapped around both legs with a pump pushing air in at intervals (intermittent PCD), squeezing the leg to push blood up, reducing stasis and preventing DVT. Figure: PCD (skip).
     - Contraindication to IPC: the leg with DVT already (pressing makes the clot dislodge and cause PE), and the leg with a wound or infection; CHF.
  2. Graduated elastic compression stocking (GCS): used from ankle to knee with pressure about 20 mmHg; often must be used together with anticoagulant; contraindications are the same as IPC.
- Anticoagulant: medical VTE prophylaxis:
  1. LMWH: the one used often is enoxaparin 40 mg subcutaneous 12 hours before surgery and inject 40 mg after surgery subcutaneous OD; how long to inject, follow this: 1.1 general cases inject 7-14 days; 1.2 cancer cases, history of VTE, or Caprini score over 8: inject for 4 weeks; 1.3 in a patient changing a knee, changing a hip joint, who must lie down a long time: inject 5 weeks, starting about 12-72 hours after surgery, waiting a little to be sure there is no postoperative bleeding.
  2. UFH 5,000 unit subcutaneous q 8 hours, start and duration the same as LMWH.
- Because the Caprini score has so many details that are hard to remember, and Siriraj has not set an easy criterion, here is an easier table to get the idea.
- Figure (VTE risk factors, relative risk weighting): High: thrombophilia; history of VTE; malignancy; drugs, e.g. tuberculosis treatment, steroids, thalidomide; HIV infection. Moderate: advanced age (over 60 yrs = increased VTE risk); chronic cardiac insufficiency; obesity (BMI over 30 kg/m2); oestrogen therapy. - The American College of Chest Physicians 2012 issued guidelines for VTE prophylaxis with a few additional data:
  1. ASA and IVC filter have no role in VTE prophylaxis.
  2. A cancer patient is a high risk patient, especially with additional risk factors such as advanced age, prolonged immobility, obesity, indwelling central venous catheter: when it comes to surgery there is a higher chance of fatal VTE; the prevention is to give LMWH; if the surgery opens the abdomen, major cancer surgery such as hepatectomy in HCC, laparoscopy, thoracotomy, thoracoscopy: besides giving anticoagulant (LMWH) from before surgery, still give prolonged prophylaxis after surgery for another 28-30 days, which reduces VTE by 60%.
