# Cardiac Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 296-321 (PDF pages 2430-2455) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 23 พฤศจิกายน 2558)

- Paper exam questions on this topic are not hard, but in oral exams residents' basics are often weak; a topic where examiners can grill residents badly (as printed).

## Anatomy of The Heart
- **Anterior aspect**: mostly the right heart — about 1/3 right atrium and 2/3 right ventricle; near the apex a small strip of left ventricle shows.
- **Superior aspect**: at the level of the 3rd ICS — the base of the great vessels.
- **Pericardium**: fixed fibrotic sac enclosing the heart and great vessels (aorta, SVC, IVC, pulmonary A and V).
  - 2 layers: visceral (inner) and parietal (outer). Little elasticity → only 50-100 cc of blood in the pericardium can already cause tamponade.
  - Normal content only 30-50 cc of fluid.
- **Phrenic nerve**: runs down the lateral side of the pericardium, anterior to the pulmonary hilum.
- **Box of death**: anterior chest area between the midclavicular lines of both clavicles, running down through the nipples to the costal margin. Penetrating injury in this box may hit the heart.

## Mechanism of Injury
- **Blunt**: about 10%; usually causes myocardial contusion; mortality higher than penetrating injury.
- **Penetrating injury**: 90%; for injuries in the box area, chance of hitting:
  1. Right ventricle 43%
  2. Left ventricle 34%
  3. Right atrium 16%
  4. Left atrium 7%
  5. More than 1 chamber 30%
- AAST cardiac injury scale has 6 grades, mixing blunt and penetrating together; confusing and a waste of time to memorize (as printed).
- Presentation ranges from minor to arrest.

## Investigation
- **FAST**
  - Practical, available 24 h; not hard to learn — with a little training accuracy > 90% (as printed, joke about "our level" being expert).
  - Pericardial blood seen and patient still holding up → take to OR.
- **2D-Echocardiography**
  - Non-invasive like FAST but more detailed: shows wall, septum, valve, chordae; favoured by CVT and vascular surgeons. Now 3D echo is even clearer (wall and valves seen opening and closing).
  - Problems: available only in centers and needs a really skilled reader; false -ve 5-10%. Acceptable as a paper-exam answer, but in oral exams FAST is the better answer.
- **Pericardiocentesis**
  - Spinal needle or angiocath no. 18, inserted left of the xiphoid at 45° to the skin, tip aimed toward the left shoulder.
  - Advance while aspirating; may use US guidance or attach EKG to the needle. Aspirating only 20 cc can already relieve tamponade.
  - Pericardial blood is normally unclotted, but with rapid bleeding it can clot.
  - Now used much less; done only when truly life-saving.
- **Subxyphoid pericardial window**
  - ATLS-type authors favour it over needle aspiration, because the needle may miss or hit the heart or a coronary.
  - Technique:
    1. Upper midline incision from the xiphoid down about 5 cm; in trauma it is done quickly — local anaesthesia is enough.
    2. Excise the xiphoid → bulging pericardiophrenic membrane is seen; push the peritoneum down with a sponge without entering the abdomen.
    3. Place stay sutures, then open the pericardium about 2-4 cm.
- **CT scan**: rarely used for this; usually done for another reason and cardiac injury is found incidentally. Findings suggesting cardiac injury:

| MDCT direct findings | MDCT indirect findings |
|---|---|
| Hemopericardium | Sternal fractures ± pectus deformity |
| Pneumopericardium | Mediastinal hematoma |
| Cardiac tamponade: hemorrhagic pericardial fluid; distended central veins (IVC, SVC, hepatic vein); with or without contrast reflux and periportal edema | |
| Pericardial tear ± cardiac herniation | |

## Cardiac Tamponade
- More often from penetrating than blunt trauma.
- Suspect when shot or stabbed near the nipple with shock, but the neck veins are distended instead of collapsed, and breath sounds are equal on both sides (so not tension pneumothorax).
- **Beck triad** — present in only 10-30%:
  1. Hypotension — SBP falls while DBP rises → narrow pulse pressure
  2. Muffled (distant) heart sound — hard to hear because every hospital ER is as noisy as a beer bar on Pattaya beach (as printed)
  3. Neck vein distension
- **Pulsus paradoxus**: on inspiration SBP in cardiac tamponade falls by a further 10 mmHg.
- **Chest x-ray**: may look normal in acute injury; classic = enlarged "water bottle" heart. If possible take it upright; if the patient cannot sit or stand, head-up is still fine — it may show air or blood in the pleural space, which needs ICD before going to OR.
- **EKG**: usually low voltage.
- If CXR and EKG are suspicious, echo may follow — not very suitable in this emergency setting.
- To satisfy examiners: from history and physical exam alone, think of FAST before any other investigation — quick, easy, available in the ER, and accuracy > 90% if the operator is competent.
- **Management**
  1. Hemodynamically stable → quick history, physical exam, then FAST straight away.
     - 1.1 FAST +ve → to OR, median sternotomy.
     - 1.2 FAST equivocal/suspicious → ask for echo; if echo not available, consider subxyphoid window — pleural blood often obscures the pericardium. Echo is what texts and CVT surgeons like, but in real life in Thailand the window works better (บ้านเรา).
     - 1.3 FAST -ve and no other associated injury needing surgery → admit and observe. At this point residents will send for chest film or run an EKG — guaranteed not to be scolded (รับรองแม่ไม่ด่า).
  2. Hemodynamically unstable → quick primary survey; may need ICD at once without a chest film; then load fluid quickly.
     - 2.1 BP still drops after IV fluid → quick FAST; if FAST +ve or equivocal → rush to OR; incision either median sternotomy or anterolateral thoracotomy. If the OR is far and the patient may arrest in the lift, do pericardiocentesis before going to OR.
     - 2.2 BP returns to normal after IV fluid (patient stable, surgeon less stressed) → FAST while humming quietly, then read the FAST:
       - 2.2.1 FAST +ve → OR, median sternotomy.
       - 2.2.2 FAST -ve → admit and investigate, e.g. chest x-ray upright, EKG, CT, echo.
  3. Arrested on arrival in ER, especially penetrating injury → EDT. If ROSC → to OR; if not, admit to the mortuary (ห้องดับจิต).

## Blunt Cardiac Injury
- Less common than penetrating injury; caused by blunt chest injury — even from climbing on top to do CPR.
- Symptoms depend on the degree of myocardial contusion: from minor (recovers in 2-3 days) to V.tach, MI, or arrest.
- **Suspect when** (ควรสงสัยในรายที่):
  1. Fracture sternum or seat-belt mark on the chest
  2. BP drop not explained by hypovolemic shock or a neurogenic cause
  3. EKG with tachycardia (not bradycardia) or any cardiac arrhythmia — but EKG has no pathognomonic finding
  4. Raised troponin
- Cameron adds cardiac index < 2.5 L/min and echo showing anatomical abnormality of septum, valve or wall — the author says this does not work in general hospitals, except for reasonably stable patients in a fancy 5-star center with staff available all the time.
- Suspected cases: chest x-ray, EKG, troponin stat and again 8 hr later.
- Chest film is usually normal; it is sent to exclude other injuries.
- **Cardiac troponin**: protein not found in normal people; marker for myocardial injury; sensitivity 50% at 4 hr and 100% at 12 hr; troponin stays raised for a further 6-8 hr.
- Troponin is now used instead of creatine kinase, which is not very sensitive for myocardial injury.
- Stable patient: CVT surgeons like echo — shows wall motion, septum, valve, chordae and pericardium clearly.
- Unstable, or FAST shows tamponade (impact strong enough to rupture a ventricle) → no echo; straight to OR.
- **Management**
  1. Stable → EKG and troponin at admission and again 8 hr later.
  2. Admit about 24 hr to monitor for arrhythmia, especially age > 55 years or prior heart disease.
     - 2.1 No arrhythmia, normal EKG and troponin → discharge home; no ICU needed.
     - 2.2 Arrhythmia occurs or is found → treat it, then echo; these patients should be in ICU. Possible EKG findings:

| Table 1 — EKG findings in cardiac contusion | |
|---|---|
| Non-specific abnormalities | Pericarditis-like ST segment elevation or PTa depression; prolonged QT interval |
| Myocardial injury | New Q wave; ST-T segment elevation or depression |
| Conduction disorders | Right bundle branch block; fascicular block; AV nodal conduction disorders (1, 2 and 3 degree AV block) |
| Arrhythmias | Sinus tachycardia; atrial and ventricular extrasystoles; atrial fibrillation; ventricular tachycardia; ventricular fibrillation; sinus bradycardia; atrial tachycardia |

  3. Unstable, persistent shock → FAST to rule out tamponade or intra-abdominal bleeding.
     - 3.1 Pericardial tamponade → treat as tamponade.
     - 3.2 No pericardial blood and no abdominal bleeding → call CVT for help (โบ้ยให้ CVT): cause may be valve, septum or coronary injury; if suspected → echocardiography, inotropes, cardiac cath, or open repair.

Figure: Blunt cardiac injury (BCI) algorithm, as steps —
1. Suspicion of BCI → obtain admission 12-lead ECG.
2. Normal ECG and patient hemodynamically stable, < 55 years old, no history of cardiac disease → no further evaluation for BCI.
3. Abnormal ECG (e.g. arrhythmia, ST changes, ischemia, heart block, unexplained ST), OR patient hemodynamically unstable and ≥ 55 years old, OR history of cardiac disease → initiate cardiac monitoring (24-48 hours).
   - Remains hemodynamically stable → discontinue monitoring.
   - Hemodynamically unstable → trans-thoracic or trans-esophageal echocardiography to evaluate cardiac function (TEE preferred).

## Coronary Artery Injury
- From blunt or penetrating trauma; extremely rare — whoever meets one is unlucky enough to be reborn (as printed).
- **Management**
  1. Intimal tear → thrombosis then MI. Anticoagulant cannot be given; thrombolysis may kill the patient first. Treat as MI; if the patient survives, cath and stent later.
  2. Torn coronary artery found at operation: distal 1/3 → simply ligate. Proximal 2/3 → ligation is certainly fatal; compress with a finger first, then call CVT to take over, since repair or bypass needs the pump.

## Valve Injury
- Usually from penetrating injury; aortic valve most often.
- Early on usually not diagnosed; found post-op when CHF develops and is hard to treat. Most medical students can hear the murmur.
- **Investigation**: 2D echo.
- Early treatment: treat CHF first, then hand over to CVT, since it usually ends with valve replacement.

## Foreign Body
- Lodged in the myocardium → needs cardiopulmonary bypass; ask CVT to remove it.
- (Image caption as printed: "FB. enbedded in the intraventicular septum".)
- **Reasons to remove the foreign body in every case**
  1. Causes endocarditis
  2. May migrate into a cardiac chamber
     - 2.1 In the right ventricle → if it dislodges it lodges in the lung
     - 2.2 In the left ventricle → if it dislodges it is very hard to find, but usually (luckily) it travels down and lodges at the bifurcation of the common femoral artery → remove under local anaesthesia as for embolectomy: arteriotomy and extract the FB.

## Principles of Operative Surgery
- Cardiac injury surgery needs some nerve and a steady hand — you may have to suture a beating heart.
- During the CVT rotation, residents should learn to open and close the chest and to identify the phrenic nerve running down the lateral side of the pericardium.
- **Incision**
  1. **Median sternotomy**: from the manubrium above to the xiphoid below; handles anterior heart injuries — right atrium, right ventricle, atriocaval junction. Choose for reasonably stable patients, or with mild BP drop that recovers well; can be extended into an abdominal midline incision.
  2. **Anterolateral thoracotomy**: from parasternal to posterior axillary line; if needed extend as clamshell to the opposite side. Suits unstable patients needing speed — may need aortic cross-clamp or internal cardiac massage. Do not forget: if the patient survives, ligate the proximal and distal internal mammary artery when closing — otherwise once BP rises it bleeds heavily and may need reopening.
- **Pericardiotomy**
  - The pericardium is thick, so tamponade cannot be ruled out just by looking.
  - Lift the pericardium with Allis, open longitudinally anterior to the phrenic nerve about 1-2 cm, then scoop out all clot.
  - Figure caption: pericardiotomy with toothed pick-ups and curved Mayo scissors, starting at the cardiac apex anterior to the phrenic nerve, extending on the anterior surface of the heart toward the great vessels.
  - Close the pericardium loosely so blood and serum can drain freely into the pleural cavity.
- **Temporary bleeding control of cardiac wounds** (แผลที่หัวใจ)
  1. Press the bleeding point with a finger first (digital control).
  2. Insert a Foley cath, inflate the balloon and pull gently; fluid can be given through it if needed, but beware air embolism. Inflate the balloon with NSS, not air — if the balloon bursts, NSS causes no air embolism. Do not pull the Foley hard — the hole may tear wider.
  3. Place traction (stay) sutures and pull, then quickly suture the remaining gap.
- **Examination and Assessment**
  - After temporary control, inspect the heart wound front, sides and back — it may be through-and-through. Always remember to check the back of the heart.
  - How to check the back of the heart:
    1. Slip the right hand behind and feel; may find a hole.
    2. Lift the heart and look behind — quickly, as lifting the heart lowers cardiac output; load IV before lifting.
- **Surgical technique**
  1. **Ventricle**: atraumatic nonabsorbable prolene 3-0 or 4-0, simple sutures or horizontal mattress, buttressed with Teflon strip or pledgets ("Taflon" as printed). The author likes pericardium — tough and right there; a small piece prevents cut-through when tying. No need to suture all layers of the ventricle — epicardium is enough; sutures should not pass into the endocardium, as this promotes later thrombus.
  2. **Wound near a coronary artery**: horizontal mattress with bites passing beside the artery; when tied the wound closes and the coronary artery is lifted up, so it is not occluded.
  3. **Atrium**: usually does not survive to reach us, unlike the ventricle where bleeding stops with each contraction. Grasp the atrial hole with Allis, partially occlude with a Santinsky clamp (as printed), then run continuous prolene 3-0 or 4-0.
  4. **Coronary artery** laceration: most die before the chest is opened; if one survives to reach a "god-level" surgeon like us (as printed):
     - 4.1 Distal 1/3 injury → ligate.
     - 4.2 Proximal 2/3 injury → ligation is fatal; must repair with prolene 7-0 or GSV bypass, which needs cardiopulmonary bypass; just occlude with a finger and call CVT to help.
  5. **Valve, septum and papillary muscle injury** → pass to CVT.
