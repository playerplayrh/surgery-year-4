# Skin and Subcutaneous Tissue 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 156-192 (PDF pages 160-196)

## Introduction (book p.156)
- Skin is the largest organ of the body. Surgeons need not know every skin disease; the common skin cancers to know are BCC, SCC and melanoma. Melanoma treatment has progressed to gene level with many new targeted drugs (NCCN V.1 2017); those who want depth should go to a fellowship in oncology.

## Skin structure and function (p.156)
- Skin is the largest organ; protects against noxious agents, chemicals, microbes; helps control body temperature through sweat.
- 3 layers: 1. epidermis: 5 strata with different names; 2. basement membrane: keratinocytes migrate upward and are shed from the epidermis, taking about 50 days; 3. dermis: contains skin appendages: sweat glands, hair follicles, nerve endings, blood vessels; deeper is the subcutaneous tissue, mostly fat.
- Sweat glands: 1. eccrine: secrete clear watery sweat as droplets, for thermal regulation; 2. apocrine: secrete thick sticky milk-like substance (axilla etc.); body odor arises when apocrine secretion mixes with bacteria.
- Cutaneous sensation from skin to CNS: Meissner, Ruffini and Pacini corpuscles carry local pressure, vibration and touch.
- Glomus bodies: tortuous AV shunts; when stimulated they increase superficial blood flow; not related to sensation.

## Solar or UV radiation (p.156)
- UV spectrum: UVA 400-315 nm; UVB 315-290 nm; UVC 290-200 nm.
- The ozone layer absorbs UVC; what reaches the earth is UVA and UVB, especially UVB, which causes sunburn, chronic skin damage and impaired function of Langerhan cells (the skin's immune cells), so skin cancer can develop; UVB is associated with 90% of BCC and 65% of SCC and melanoma (as printed: "90% of BCC and SCC and 65% of melanoma").

## Terms for describing skin lesions (p.156-157)
1. Flat spot <5 mm = macule; >5 mm = patch.
2. Raised bump <5 mm = papule; >5 mm = nodule.
3. Red patch = erythema.
- Vesicle: fluid-filled raised lesion 5 mm or less across. Bulla: fluid-filled raised lesion greater than 5 mm across. Blister: common term for vesicle or bulla. Pustule: discrete, pus-filled, raised lesion.

## Infection and abscess (p.157)
- Skin and soft tissue infection: 2 groups: 1. uncomplicated infection: not severe: cellulitis, erysipelas, impetigo; 2. complicated infection: severe, e.g. necrotizing fasciitis.

### Cellulitis (p.157)
- Superficial spreading infection of skin and subcutaneous tissue, at the dermis layer; cardinal signs: pain, swelling, redness, warmth; borders not clear; spreads fast. (Figure: poorly demarcated margin.)
- Organisms: staph and strep. Unlike an abscess, cellulitis still has blood supply and viable tissue so antibiotic alone can treat it; the drug should cover strep and staph, e.g. cefazolin 1 g IV every 6-8 hours, or Augmentin (625 mg) orally bid or 1.2 g IV every 8 hours.
- An abscess has necrotic tissue and loss of blood supply (vessels thrombosed); antibiotic often cannot reach the site of infection, so drainage is needed too.

### Erysipelas (p.157)
- Infection of the dermis like cellulitis but more superficial, caused by group A strep (S. pyogenes); the border of inflammation is sharply demarcated from normal skin. (Figure: sharp margin.)

### Lymphangitis (p.158)
- Infection of lymphatic channels in the subcutaneous layer; red streaks on the skin along the lymphatic channel toward the node group; lymph nodes often swollen and inflamed too.

### Impetigo (p.158)
- Infection of the superficial epidermis by hemolytic strep group A, staph may join; often arises from scratches, insect bites.
- 2 groups by depth: 1. superficial impetigo: from Staph. aureus, epidermal inflammation; starts from small vesicles that become bullae, then rupture and form honey-yellow crusts with a red base; seen on skin around the nose, mouth, arms, especially in children. 2. Deeper impetigo = ecthyma: punched-out ulcer; common on the legs; hard scab, red margin, rough base.
- Treatment: clean the lesion + oral antibiotic.

### Hair follicle infection (p.159)
- Folliculitis, furuncle, carbuncle; caused by staph; common in DM patients, at sites with friction, e.g. the beard area.
- Only one hair follicle inflamed as a red papule to pustule with a hair, <1 mm = folliculitis: antibiotic alone is enough. A pus-filled nodule, if only one = furuncle (boil): must drain plus antibiotic. (Figure: furuncle vs carbuncle.)
- Carbuncle: progression of furuncle sideways: group of follicles infected with many pus-draining openings; subcutaneous pus and necrosis; common at the nape, back and trunk; patients often have risk factors: diabetes, steroid use, immunocompromised; fever, pain; treatment: drain plus antibiotic.

## Surgical site infection (SSI) (p.159-160)
- Infection after surgery within 30 days, or within 1 year if a prosthesis is used. SSI is the most common nosocomial infection in surgical patients, then UTI, URI.
- Factors: 1. degree of microbial contamination of the wound during surgery; 2. duration of the procedure; 3. host factors such as DM, malnutrition, obesity, immune suppression. GA is not a factor.
- Usually appears about day 4-5; organism: Staph. aureus; in a clean-contaminated wound, E. coli is often the one.
- Aggressive form: often on day 2 after surgery; organisms strep pyogenes, pseudomonas, clostridium perfringens; patient has severe sepsis; need radical debridement plus IV antibiotic.
- CDC classifies SSI by depth: superficial incisional, deep incisional, organ/space. (Figure: skin and subcutaneous = superficial incisional; deep soft tissue (fascia, muscle) = deep incisional; organ/space.)

| Type | CDC criteria |
|---|---|
| Superficial incisional | Within 30 days after operation, only skin or subcutaneous tissue of the incision (suture abscess excluded), and at least one of: purulent drainage; organisms from aseptically obtained culture of fluid/tissue from the incision; pain/tenderness, localized swelling, redness or heat with deliberate opening by the surgeon (unless culture-negative); diagnosis by surgeon or attending |
| Deep incisional | Within 30 days (1 year if implant) and appears related to deep soft tissue (fascia, muscle), and at least one of: purulent drainage from the deep incision but not the organ/space; spontaneous dehiscence or deliberately opened by the surgeon with fever, localized pain or tenderness (unless culture-negative); abscess or other evidence of infection of the deep incision on exam, reoperation, histopathology or imaging; diagnosis by surgeon or attending |
| Organ/space | Within 30 days (1 year if implant) and involves any part of the anatomy other than the incision, opened or manipulated during the operation, and at least one of: purulent drainage from a drain through a stab wound into the organ/space; organisms from culture of fluid/tissue in the organ/space; abscess or other evidence on exam, reoperation, histopathology or imaging; diagnosis by surgeon or attending |
(Infections involving both superficial and deep sites and organ/space infections that drain through the incision are reported as deep incisional SSI.)

### Managing SSI and reducing risk (p.161)
- If the SSI is only a localized wound infection, do I&D; antibiotics are kept for cases with severe cellulitis or sepsis (Schwartz's view, which the author thinks is good).
- Ways to reduce SSI risk (RCT evidence in clean-contaminated surgery): skin prep before surgery with chlorhexidine-alcohol reduces SSI better than the povidone-iodine we use. Hair removal with an electronic clipper is no better than not shaving; if hair must be removed, the best way is clippers, removing hair in the operating room just before surgery; the worst way is a razor, which increases SSI risk. Oxygen: giving only 30% during surgery reduces SSI no differently than 80%. Avoid hypothermia during surgery up to the first hour after surgery. Give prophylactic antibiotic when indicated.
- Prophylactic antibiotic: aim to reduce SSI by giving 30-60 minutes before surgery and continuing not more than 24 hours; most failures of antibiotic prophylaxis come from wrong timing of administration, not from the drug being expensive or the dose inadequate, but from orders being late.

## Fournier gangrene (p.161-162)
- Necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum, genital organs and perianal area; often in patients with low immunity: diabetes, HIV, steroid or immunosuppressive drug use, malnutrition, obesity, blood disease.
- Organisms are usually mixed infection; the origin of infection comes from an anorectal source such as hemorrhoid or diverticulitis 30-50%; from uro infection 20-40%; the rest from skin and subcutaneous infection.
- Diagnosis is mainly clinical: fever, painful swelling at the scrotum or perineum, skin swollen and red, crepitus, with foul-smelling discharge. Imaging is for unclear cases or those where the extent of perirectal involvement must be known; Greenfield advises CT scan in stable patients to assess disease extent and help decide about colostomy.
- Treatment:
  1. Radical debridement: the most important, emergency and aggressive, as early as possible; some even advise cutting into healthy tissue about 2-3 cm; the testis can usually be kept because its blood supply from the testicular artery is not usually involved.
  2. IV antibiotic.
  3. Urine and fecal diversion: proximal colostomy to prevent feces from contaminating the wound; consider case by case; indications for colostomy: 3.1 continued fecal contamination; 3.2 anal sphincter involvement; 3.3 rectal perforation; 3.4 fecal incontinence.
  4. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is not a standard treatment.

## Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) (p.162-164)
- Life-threatening infection of fascia and the underlying muscle; often in immunocompromised patients, e.g. DM; occurs as a skin wound even only a scratch; may occur at the arm in 50%, the perineum (Fournier gangrene) 20%.
- Etiology: most commonly from trauma with a wound deep to the fascia, then colon surgery, or surgery.
- Organisms often mixed: staph aureus, clostridium, beta-strep; blood culture often grows beta-hemolytic strep.
- 3 (some say 4) types by organism:
  1. Type 1 (75-90%): incubation 2-4 days, usually in diabetics, peripheral arterial disease, obesity, renal disease; organisms mixed aerobes (gram-positive and gram-negative) and anaerobes.
  2. Type 2 (10-25%): incubation 6-48 hours; usually group A strep pyogenes alone, or with staph aureus; often in patients who inject drugs into the arm; staph often community MRSA, which is sensitive to cloxacillin, not hospital MRSA.
  3. Type 3: usually in trauma patients with dead tissue, usually clostridium perfringens; if nontraumatic, it is usually in immunocompromised hosts or those with an underlying GI malignancy, especially colorectal cancer; in the nontraumatic group the organism is often clostridium septicum, and the patient should be sent for colonoscopy to exclude CRC.
- Clinical: pain of the explosive kind, but early signs of inflammation on the skin do not look like much (pain out of proportion); later there is swelling (tense edema) followed by hemorrhagic blebs, which arise from occlusion of deep vessels near the fascia, causing necrosis of tissue below the skin, so fluid collects and forms blisters.
- In the bleb is cloudy fluid, gray, with a faint smell (dishwater-gray exudate) coming from the wound, not yet pus; skin necrosis, crepitus; then the patient weakens, speaks softly, then septic shock; but hard signs such as hemorrhagic bleb, skin necrosis or crepitus are found in only 50%, so criteria for early NF diagnosis use labs: Hb <11, WBC >15,000, serum sodium <135, creatinine >1.6, serum glucose >180, CRP >150 suggests NF.
- Diagnosis: clinical, with high index of suspicion; do not wait for fluctuation or waste time on plain film, MRI or CT to look for air bubbles; soft tissue gas is specific for NF but not sensitive; most NF shows only thickened fascia, which has low specificity; time to surgery is the factor with highest mortality.
- LRINEC score (lab parameters) to separate NF from other severe soft tissue infections: a score >6 means likely true NF; accuracy >92% and helps diagnose NF earlier; problem: score 8 or more has higher chance of death or amputation.

| Parameter | Range | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Hb (g/dL) | >13.5 | 0 |
| | 11-13.5 | 1 |
| | <11 | 2 |
| White cells (10^9/L) | <15 | 0 |
| | 15-25 | 1 |
| | >25 | 2 |
| Sodium (mmol/L) | <135 | 2 |

(Score <=5 = <50% risk (low); 6-7 = intermediate risk; >=8 = >75% risk (high).)
- In practice, to confirm NF easily without calculating LRINEC, use the bedside finger test: inject local anesthetic at the thigh, make a 2 cm incision down to the deep fascia, and sweep a finger at the deep fascia: if there is no bleeding and dishwater fluid with a faint smell comes out, and the finger sweeps easily because the layers between subcutaneous tissue and fascia separate without the normal connective tissue resistance, think NF (the author finds this works better than the LRINEC score).
- Treatment:
  1. Rapid resuscitation with crystalloid, rush the patient to OR for immediate radical debridement (the most important): cut until healthy tissue with bleeding at the edge is seen; serial debridement is often needed until all necrotic tissue is gone; send tissue culture with blood culture; I&D alone is too little. For arm or leg, amputation is used in cases with very rapid spreading infection, entering bone, joint or chest, abdomen, or massive muscle necrosis, with a guillotine cut straight down without debridement.
  2. Antibiotic: give within 1 hour before going to the OR: penicillin 12-24 million units per day; currently must be extended-spectrum penicillin (piperacillin/tazobactam) + vancomycin, or carbapenem level if methicillin-resistant staph (MRSA), found up to 70%, needing vancomycin; for staph, strep, gram-negative that produce exotoxin give clindamycin 900-1200 mg every 8 hours; aminoglycoside works poorly in anaerobic conditions.
  3. Hyperbaric therapy is not recommended as routine because studies found unclear benefit, and importantly it cannot replace radical debridement.
  4. If lucky not to have an amputation, after survival wait and bring the patient back to close the wound, mostly with a graft.

## Rare skin infection (p.164-165)
- Know some organisms and drugs for the exam.

### Actinomycosis (p.164-165)
- A gram-positive anaerobic bacterium, strangely named like a fungus; distinct feature: sulfur granule drainage; common in the head and neck area; at the foot it is called Madura foot.
- Histology: pink-purple granule (silver granule) surrounded by lymphocytes.
- Treatment: penicillin and sulfonamide together with debridement of infected tissue.

### Lymphogranuloma venereum (p.165)
- Caused by Chlamydia trachomatis (a gram-negative bacterium), a sexually transmitted disease with incubation about 2 weeks; a painless ulcer or discharge, with inflamed groin nodes.
- Treatment: doxycycline.

## Hyperhidrosis (p.165)
- Excessive sweating beyond what the body needs for temperature control, until the palms are wet.
- Most cases are an issue for medicine: look for causes: stress, anxiety, nervousness, obesity, HTN, DM, thyrotoxicosis; treating the underlying disease reduces sweating.
- Unknown cause (cryptogenic): treat with drugs first, beta-blocker, roll-on applied to armpit; if drugs do not help, surgeons can help with thoracoscopic sympathectomy at the appropriate level, because sweat secretion of skin is controlled by the sympathetic nervous system: for the head and face only, sympathectomy at rib level 3 is best for the palm: cut at rib level 3 or 4; but if sweating is mostly at the armpit, cut at level 4 or 5.

## Hidradenitis suppurativa (p.165-166)
- Infection of apocrine sweat glands by strep or staph after duct obstruction; often at the axilla, nipple, perineum and perianal area. (Figure: axilla.)
- Treatment depends on severity and extent: Hurley classification into 3 grades (not necessary to know): 1. improve local hygiene; 2. give antibiotic; 3. acute infection should be drained or curetted.
  - (item 3 continues) after drainage/curettage, close the wound primarily or leave it to close by itself.
  4. Chronic infection that does not heal may need wide excision, then close the defect with a graft or local flap.
  5. Do not apply deodorant or powder to the inflamed area.

## Pyoderma gangrenosum (p.166)
- Destruction and necrosis with an ill-defined margin, red around. (Figure: rapidly enlarging necrotic lesion, ill-defined border.)
- Uncommon but progresses rather fast.
- 50% have an underlying disease: 1. IBD; 2. rheumatoid arthritis; 3. hematologic malignancy; 4. monoclonal immunoglobulin-A gammopathy.
- Treatment: 1. treat the underlying disease; 2. steroid, cyclosporine, even some chemotherapy helps the wound improve; 3. local wound care and cover with skin graft.

## Pyomyositis (p.166-167)
- Pus-forming infection in muscle, usually staph, entering muscle via minor trauma or hematogenous spread; classic: skin not very red, but pain increases on moving the leg (pain on movement).

| Stage | Symptoms | Physical exam |
|---|---|---|
| Invasive (myositis only) | Low-grade fever, malaise, dull cramping pain | Firm "woody" texture on deep palpation, no redness, no warmth |
| Suppurative (abscess formation) | Increased pain with more focal complaints | Significant tenderness with redness and swelling |
| Late (sepsis) | High fever and severe pain | Systemic manifestations including septic shock |
- Treatment: use US to confirm the pus pocket, then I&D with antibiotic.

## Cysts (p.167-168)
- Knowledge expected of medical students, especially in comprehensive exams; residents need only the pilonidal cyst.
- Epidermal inclusion cyst: formerly wrongly called sebaceous cyst; lies under the skin, caused by keratin trapped at the opening of the pilosebaceous unit; in the middle of the cyst is a dimple or punctum, the original site of the hair follicle (figure: black punctum).
- Trichilemmal cyst: common on the scalp of women; no epidermis; keratin comes from the outer sheath of the hair follicle.
- Ganglion cyst: cyst of tendon sheath with communication into the joint; content is synovial fluid; common at the wrist, then the back of the hand and back of the foot; cause unknown; about 50% resolve spontaneously, so only observe and reassure; aspiration has 50% recurrence; simple excision is still not enough (40% recurrence); removing the mass with ligation of the neck reduces recurrence to 5%.
- Pilonidal cyst: congenital coccygeal cyst; sometimes a sinus, with ingrowing hair causing infection (read more in the colon, rectum and anus chapter). (Figure: pilonidal dimple, skin, fat, bone.)
- Dermoid cyst: congenital cyst with several dermal tissues such as hair, sebaceous gland and sweat gland; common at the tail of the eyebrow and the head of the eyebrow.

## Vascular tumors (p.168-170)
- Overgrowth of blood vessels that develop abnormally. Two types to know:
  1. Hemangioma: bright red, raised from the skin = strawberry hemangioma; if bluish-purple under the skin = cavernous hemangioma.
  2. Vascular malformation: not really a tumor, more a developmental abnormality; formerly called port-wine hemangioma, now port-wine stain.

### Hemangioma (infantile hemangioma) (p.168-170)
- Benign vascular tumor from embryonal-type endothelium; this type regresses on its own.
- Arises after birth (may be a small spot or nothing at birth, then appears later); not present at birth as with port-wine stain.
- Early: skin patch; then gradually raised, bright red; common on the face, neck, scalp and shoulders.
- Course in 2 phases: 1. proliferative phase: rapid growth, in the first year; 2. involuting phase: starts spontaneous regression after the first year; tumor shrinks gradually, 70% by 7 years and almost 100% by 12 years.
- Treatment:
  1. Observation: do nothing in the first 8 months, except when the lesion grows fast until it hurts the nerves or there is an indication for treatment (2.1-2.3).
  2. Early treatment starts with drug: propranolol 3 mg/kg/day for about 5 months; if no result use steroid (IV, oral or intralesional injection); interferon-alpha is an option; if still failing, laser or embolization. Indications for treatment: 2.1 interferes with function, e.g. feeding, urination, vision, breathing, or ulceration; 2.2 systemic problems, e.g. CHF, DIC from thrombocytopenia (Kasabach-Merritt syndrome); 2.3 (as printed) without involvement.
  3. Surgery is used last, when everything fails, or tracheostomy if airway obstruction.
  4. After early adolescence (8-12 months as printed in the original), if the mass does not go away, use laser (best cosmetic) or excision.
- Figure (algorithm): Infantile hemangioma (history, features, imaging): proliferative phase (<1 year): rapid growth, ulceration, disfigurement (superficial, deep, compound) -> propranolol (1st line), corticosteroids (2nd line), pulsed dye laser (superficial), alpha-IFN (Kasabach-Merritt syndrome), imiquimod (superficial, small-medium), vincristine (multiple, life-threatening), surgery (special areas); stable/inconspicuous lesions -> clinical observation. Involuting phase (>1 year): clinical observation/propranolol/surgery. Involuted phase (>3 years): clinical observation. After involution: atrophic scar -> CO2 laser; residual telangiectasia -> diode laser; residual lesions/red discoloration -> pulsed dye laser; fibrofatty remnants/redundant skin -> surgical correction.

### Vascular malformation (p.170)
- Developmental abnormality of vascular endothelium; present from birth, then grows slowly with the child's growth; no rapid growth like hemangioma. (Figure: right cheek, present since birth.)
- Because it arises from adult-type endothelium, there is no spontaneous regression.
- 2 groups: 1. slow flow: 1.1 capillary malformation (formerly port-wine hemangioma); 1.2 venous malformation (formerly cavernous hemangioma). 2. Fast flow: blood flows fast, in large amount, may cause pain from pressure effect or bleeding: 2.1 arterial malformation; 2.2 AVM; 2.3 AVF: a direct connection between artery and vein; if the hole is large and a lot of blood passes from artery to vein, CHF may result.
- Investigation: CTA or MRA helps diagnose and distinguishes it from hemangioma.
- Treatment, several: 1. sclerosing injection; 2. selective embolization, may be used preop to shrink the mass; 3. surgical excision; 4. ablation with laser to destroy capillaries without damaging the overlying skin, especially for port-wine stain; laser is the treatment of choice, start before 1 year of age, then follow up with laser at intervals until the red patch disappears (figure: before and after laser, much lighter).

## Glomus tumor (p.171)
- Benign tumor of blood vessel and nerve; found anywhere but most often at the fingertip; a blue-gray nodule; symptom: severe pain.
- Treatment: excision.

## Keloid and hypertrophic scar (p.171-172)
- Similarities: 1. overfibroblast activity, with tissue raised above the level of normal skin; 2. usually follows skin trauma, with no known mechanism, only theories; 3. cannot be separated by gross appearance or histology (remember this).
- Differences (figure: hypertrophic scar, keloid): 1. keloid is a mass growing out from the original wound into normal tissue or unwounded area, whereas a hypertrophic scar stays only within the original wound; 2. keloid appears about 3-6 months after the wound and does not regress, whereas a hypertrophic scar appears about 4 weeks after the wound and can regress; 3. steroid injection: for keloid intralesional steroid is first line, whereas for hypertrophic scar it is second line.
- Treatment of keloid: 1. excision alone has very high recurrence, 45-100%; 2. excision together with: 2.1 intralesional steroid to reduce fibroblast proliferation; 2.2 topical silicone sheet for 24 hours a day, to prevent rebound hypertrophy; 2.3 RT to destroy fibroblasts (because recurrence is otherwise up to 100%); 2.4 pressure on the keloid, using an elastic cloth with the purpose of flattening and thinning the keloid; the problem is that it must be applied 23-24 hours per day for up to 1 year; 3. new modalities: 3.1 topical retinoid; 3.2 intralesional interferon, 5-FU, bleomycin.

## Benign tumors (p.172)
- Many; what matters for the exam is benign tumors that are precancerous lesions or hard to separate from cancer.
- Those that do not turn malignant and are often exam choices: seborrheic keratosis, keratoacanthoma, intradermal nevi.

### Seborrheic keratosis (p.172-173)
- Keratosis = hypertrophy of the epidermis. The keratosis group such as actinic (solar) and arsenic keratosis are precancerous lesions.
- Except seborrheic keratosis, which is not precancerous; a benign epidermal lesion: well-demarcated papule or plaque on the face, neck or chest and the back of older people, brown to black, rough, related to sun exposure; not found in people under 30; not found on mucosa, palms, soles; recently some doubt whether it is a hyperplastic lesion, because there is autosomal dominant inheritance suggesting a tumor.
- Treatment: ablative techniques such as cryotherapy, laser, electrodesiccation; if it looks like melanoma, biopsy. (Figure: pigmented lesions.)

### Keratoacanthoma (p.173)
- Benign epithelial lesion, very common in older people exposed to sunlight; lesion has rapid growth, keratin-filled crater, a nodule with a rolled edge.
- Problem: it looks like BCC or SCC.
- Treatment: 1. small lesion: excise; 2. large lesion: biopsy to rule out cancer; if not cancer, may observe; keratoacanthoma has been reported to involute within 3-6 months.

### Actinic (solar) keratosis (p.173-174)
- Neoplasm of epidermal keratinocytes in skin exposed to heavy sun; considered a precursor of SCC, with 10% turning into invasive SCC; usually in older people.
- Diagnosis from skin biopsy; must not just be observed, since cancer will come.
- Treatment: 1. cryotherapy is more popular than surgical excision, which looks good for cosmetics; 2. alternatives: topical 5-FU, chemical peel, electrocautery or curettage.
- After topical treatment, if a lesion arises with a raised edge, or recurrence appears, suspect it may turn to SCC and re-biopsy at once.

| Lesion | Appearance | Reason for surgery | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| AK | Sun-damaged skin, adherent hyperkeratosis on red base, lacking the thickness of SCC | 6-10% lifetime SCC risk; rule out SCC | Facial razor; biopsy/excision; topical 5-FU; cryotherapy |
| Keratoacanthoma | Similar to SCC, rapid growth for 1-2 months then involution for 1-2 months | Rule out SCC | Excisional biopsy |
| Bowen disease (SCC in situ) | Thin, erythematous plaque with scale | Rule out invasion | Excision preferred; topical 5-FU/imiquimod or radiation therapy if very large |
| Nevus, sebaceous | Hairless plaque on face/scalp since birth, enlarges during puberty | 10% develop BCC | Excise with 1 mm margin |
| Acrochordon (skin tag) | Soft skin tag with narrow pedicle | Cosmetic | Scissor pedicle |
| Epidermoid cyst (trichilemmoma or pilar cyst on scalp) | Cyst attached to epidermis, often has punctum | Infection, cosmetic | Excision with small ellipse around punctum |

## Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) (p.174-175)
- The most common skin cancer, 70%.
- Arises from the basal epithelial cells of the epidermis; often at the center of the face, neck and cheek (80%); classic: a lesion at the tail of the eyebrow of an old person (rodent ulcer) with telangiectasia at the edge; thinking of BCC is right >90%. (Figure: clean ulcer, rolled edge, telangiectasia.)
- A slowly growing cancer; may cause local destruction as an ulcer called rodent ulcer; the base of the ulcer is clean, does not bleed much.
- Chance of distant metastasis is very low, or rare.
- Some books divide BCC by growth pattern: circumscribed BCC with clear border: cystic, pigmented, nodular (the most common pattern is nodular); and diffuse BCC with ill-defined border, such as morpheaform.
- The interesting type is morpheaform BCC, which has local invasion and is very aggressive, so surgery should cut wider than usual.
- Risk factors: sunlight exposure and rare syndromes such as Gorlin, Bazex, Rombo (not needed to memorize).
- Precancerous lesions: 1. nevus sebaceous of Jadassohn (10% develop BCC); 2. xeroderma pigmentosum; 3. arsenic keratosis.
- Treatment:
  1. Surgical excision with histology control to get a free margin is the preferred treatment.
  2. At the nose, e.g. nasal ala, cut the cartilage out together with the BCC then close with a local nasal flap.
  3. Alternative treatment (margin 2-3 mm): suits small, shallow lesions that are not diffuse BCC; disadvantages: no histology to see whether the margin is free, no tissue diagnosis, slow healing and a large scar. Methods: 3.1 topical 5-FU or imiquimod; 3.2 curettage; 3.3 ablation with laser, electrocautery or cryosurgery (Sabiston likes cryosurgery); 3.4 RT suits locations where skin should be preserved, or recurrence after surgery.
  4. Mohs surgery: increasingly popular, especially for BCC of the face (cheek, nose, eyelid, lip).

### Mohs surgery (p.175)
- A surgical technique advised by Dr. Frederic E. Mohs; accepted as having the highest cure rate, lowest recurrence, and least loss of skin around the tumor.
- Has many names, e.g. chemosurgery, microscopically controlled excision, microscopically oriented histographic surgery (as printed; the exam name is not needed).
- In textbooks after 2007 it is described as removing BCC or SCC 2-3 mm at a time from all tumor edges, with margin control by frozen section, then cutting more until a clear margin is obtained. (Figure: serial thin layers removed, mapped.)
- In 1940 he used chemical zinc paste to mark the lesion and then cut it out little by little (hence "chemosurgery").
- So Mohs technique suits skin in areas needing maximal preservation, e.g. around the eye, nose, ear.
- Sabiston 2008 recommends Mohs technique for facial BCC, aggressive BCC, large BCC in every case if possible.
- Currently Mohs surgery is used for BCC and SCC, but not melanoma.
- Indications for Mohs surgery: 1. tumor larger than 2 cm; 2. lesion >6 mm in a high-risk area: eyelid, eyebrow, skin of face, cheek, nose, mouth, behind the ear, hand, foot; the rest of the head and neck: lesion size >1 cm; 3. sites where cutting too much causes a cosmetic problem, e.g. around the eye, ear, nose; 4. incompletely excised tumor; 5. aggressive growth pattern, e.g. morpheaform, ill-defined border, recurrent tumor, previous RT.

## Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (p.176-178)
- Skin cancer from the epidermis of the skin and oropharyngeal mucosa.
- Key point: the lesion has a rolled or overturned wound edge, irregular, ulceration with a foul smell and easy bleeding; more aggressive than BCC.
- WHO SCC classification by cell character: large cell, transitional cell, basaloid cell, cloacogenic cell (need only a little).
- Location: lower lip (whereas upper lip is BCC), vermilion border, paranasal sinus, forearms, back of the hand.
- Risks: sun (UV) exposure, albinism, smoking, patients with weakened immunity such as cancer or transplant.
- Precancerous lesions: 1. actinic or solar keratosis; 2. xeroderma pigmentosum; 3. cutaneous horn; 4. leukoplakia, erythroplakia; 5. chemical dermatitis from arsenic, nitrite, hydrocarbon; 6. chronic wounds such as pressure sore, hidradenitis suppurativa, venous ulcer, anal fistula, pilonidal abscess, chronic osteomyelitis with sinus, post-RT skin and burn scar, or Marjolin ulcer: a wound that is dry or healed then breaks down again, raised rough edge, bleeds easily: biopsy; 7. chronic infection such as HPV.
- Marjolin ulcer: rather aggressive and metastasizes faster than SCC from other causes.
- Bowen disease: SCC in situ; predisposing factors: sun exposure, immunosuppression (HIV), HPV infection; histology: cell atypia within the epidermis without invasion through the basement membrane; about 10% of Bowen turn into invasive SCC; treatment: wide local excision.
- Erythroplasia of Queyrat: SCC in situ of the glans penis, seen as a red patch (read carefully; the name is not a mouse-like tumor).
- Treatment of SCC is the same as BCC: 1. SCC in situ or Bowen disease: WLE with free margin (best); 2. SCC arising from chronic wounds, e.g. burns: aggressive behavior and high risk of lymph node metastasis, so even if nodes are not palpable (N0), do prophylactic LND.

## Malignant melanoma (p.178-182)
- Proliferation of melanocytes, which in fetal life are neural crest cells that migrate to the basal layer in parts of the body.
- Sun exposure is a risk factor, besides fair skin, red or blonde hair, family history.
- Number of normal nevi as a risk factor: in adults >100 moles; in children >50.
- Location: common in men: posterior trunk; in women: legs.
- Precancerous lesions:
  1. Xeroderma pigmentosum: autosomal recessive inheritance; a typical skin cancer syndrome.
  2. Congenital nevi (giant): black, large >8 mm, giant if >20 cm; chance of cancer 5-8%; often on the back; no regression; treatment of choice is total excision, but there is not enough tissue to close, so do staged resection with local tissue expansion, closing with skin graft until complete. (Figure: giant congenital nevus.)
  3. Dysplastic nevi.
  4. Acquired nevi group: junctional and compound nevi, except intradermal nevi, which are nodular with hair growing out; these nevi are not precancerous.
  5. Spitz nevus or juvenile nevus or epithelioid cell melanoma: mostly in children under 10 years.
  6. Familial melanoma: risk 5-10%.
  7. Hutchinson's freckle: freckle means pigment; seen on the face of older people; the present consensus is that Hutchinson's freckle is lentigo maligna melanoma.
- Subtypes of melanoma:
  1. Superficial spreading melanoma: the most common, 75%; a flat lesion found anywhere except the hands and feet; size about 1-2 cm; aggressiveness medium; slow growth.
  2. Nodular melanoma: 15%; the most aggressive form because of vertical growth deep down more than other types, and at diagnosis often already spread.
  3. Lentigo maligna melanoma: 10%, at the head, neck, back of the hand, especially in older people with frequent sun exposure; a fairly flat, slow lesion spreading sideways, not growing deep, so the type with the best prognosis.
  4. Acral lentiginous melanoma: <2%, on the palm, sole, and if under the nail, it is called subungual melanoma (remember); the least common type, and prognosis is not good, often neglected, leading to late diagnosis; patients are usually dark-skinned; this type is a favorite in exams. (Figure: sole lesion.)
  5. Non-pigmented: 5%.
- Note: whatever the subtype, if the thickness is the same and the stage is the same, prognosis is the same.

### Warning signs: ABCDE (p.180)
- Benign vs malignant (figure): A: symmetrical vs asymmetrical; B: even vs uneven borders; C: one colour vs multiple colours; D: diameter smaller than 1/4 inch vs larger; E: not evolving vs evolving (changes in colour, shape and size).
- Use ABCDE rules: if a mole looks like cancer, biopsy quickly.
  1. Asymmetry: fold the mole in half and compare; if the mole is rough, or skin markings or skin creases are lost: biopsy at once.
  2. Border irregularity.
  3. Color heterogeneity: mole with many colours, darker, or more vascularity.
  4. Diameter >6 mm.
  5. Enlargement or Elevation or Evolution: raised mole looks suspicious, but a mole that is more raised and looks more attractive is suspicious (as printed).
- Moles with itching or altered sensation: beware early melanoma.
- Moles that bleed, ulcerate, or have satellite nodules are not benign.
- But if hair grows out of the mole, it is often benign.
- Any changing mole should be evaluated and biopsied quickly.

## Skin biopsy (p.180-181)
1. A small lesion with no cosmetic concern: do an elliptical (spindle-shaped) excision with length to width ratio 3:1, a complete excision 1-3 mm away from the edge of the mole; do not cut much wider than this since it affects lymphatic mapping or SLNB later; cut including subcutaneous fat down to muscle fascia; an excisional biopsy should not be larger than 1.5 cm, at most 2 cm. (Figure: usual dimensions of surgical excision: length 3 or 4 times width; angle 30 degrees or less.)
2. A lesion on an arm or leg should be cut longitudinally (or obliquely), or along the line of lymphatic drainage.
3. A lesion at a nail area: remove the nail first.
4. If the lesion is large (>2 cm) or near the eye, ear, nose, palm, sole, fingers: do incisional biopsy or punch biopsy at the thickest and darkest part; if under the nail, remove the nail then biopsy.
5. Do not do shave biopsy or curettage to take tissue for examination, or FNA, for a lesion suspected to be melanoma.
6. Do not forget to tell the pathologist the orientation of the specimen: which side is superior, inferior, medial or lateral.
7. Do not crush, cauterize, or squeeze the specimen.
8. Close the wound in 1-2 layers.
9. If the pathologist says the biopsy is inadequate, rebiopsy.
10. If the result is melanoma, look for ulceration, microsatellite nodule or not; assess margin, mitotic rate, Breslow and Clark level.
- Figure (workup algorithm): suspicious pigmented lesion -> biopsy -> inadequate: re-biopsy; melanoma confirmed -> pathology report should include Breslow thickness, ulceration status, dermal mitotic rate, deep and peripheral margin status, microsatellitosis, Clark level (for non-ulcerated lesions where mitotic rate is not determined, for lesions <=1 mm), pure desmoplasia if present -> history and physical exam with attention to locoregional area, draining lymph nodes, complete skin exam, assessment of melanoma risk factors -> manage according to clinical stage.

## Staging of melanoma (p.181)
- 3 systems:
  1. TNM AJCC 8th edition: Breslow thickness as T stage (Clark's level no longer used), N and M as usual, with added tumor mitotic rate, tumor ulceration and serum LDH; the higher the LDH, the higher the chance of metastasis.
  2. Clark level: microstaging by anatomical thickness, which does not work well.
  3. Breslow thickness: measures vertical thickness; suits use in boards and is practical (author's view).
- Figure: Breslow and Clark's levels: 1 epidermis only; 2 papillary dermis; 3 papillary-reticular junction; 4 reticular dermis; 5 subcutaneous tissue (depth in mm scale 0-5).
- Thickness is the strongest predictor of prognosis, survival, LN metastasis and distant metastasis; also ulceration and mitotic rate affect overall survival.
- Thickness vs LN metastasis:

| Breslow thickness | LN positive |
|---|---|
| <=1 mm | <3% |
| 1.1-2 mm | 13% |
| 2.1-3 mm | 23% |
| 3-4 mm | 32% |
| >4 mm | >35% |
- Breslow level in fact has 4 levels: 0.75, 0.76-1.5, 1.51-3 and 3 mm and up; not used in practice (as the author says), so divide thickness into thin, intermediate, thick (the way that suits exams):
  - Superficial (thin) lesion: <1 mm; LN positive very low, so prophylactic LND is not needed.
  - Intermediate lesion: 1.1-4 mm; LN metastasis 30% and distant metastasis under 20%; if no distant metastasis and nodes are not palpable, do SLN mapping like breast cancer; if SLN positive, LND clearly benefits.
  - Deep lesion: >4 mm; LN positive >60% and distant metastasis >70%; this stage is far gone; workup for distant metastasis (M) first, with expensive tools such as CT scan or PET scan; if M is present, LND is of no use.
- Poor prognosis factors: 1. thick tumor by Breslow (Clark level tells prognosis from melanoma stage 2 up); 2. ulceration; 3. nodular type; 4. high mitotic rate; 5. age over 60, male gender; 6. scalp, neck and truncal lesion; 7. lymph node with macrometastases and more positive nodes = worse; 8. elevated LDH: the higher the value, the more likely M1.

## Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) in melanoma (p.182-183)
- Indications:
  1. Primary invasive melanoma >=1 mm thick with clinically N0 and M0.
  2. Primary melanoma <1 mm thick (Clark/Breslow 0.76 to less than 1 mm) in patients with risk features: 2.1 young patient (age not more than 45); 2.2 ulceration (important); 2.3 angiolymphatic invasion; 2.4 extensive dermal infiltration to 1 mm, meaning that when thickness is close to 1 mm, more melanocytes are seen toward the deep side than the superficial; 2.5 high mitotic rate >=1/mm2; 2.6 Clark's level 4 (of interest).
  - Note: lesions not more than 0.75 mm have a very low chance of going to nodes; no SLN study needed.
- Steps: 1. preop lymphoscintigraphy to see the lymphatic basins; 2. intraoperative mapping with intradermal isosulfan blue dye; 3. identify and biopsy SLN, send for H&E and immunohistochemistry stain.
- If possible, do SLNB before WLE to reduce disturbance of the lymphatic pathway study.

### Lymphatic mapping (p.183)
- Finding the location of the SLN, for: 1. intermediate thickness 1.1-4 mm; 2. lesion at the trunk, e.g. chest or mid-back, where it is half-uncertain which LN basin it goes to: axilla, groin, one side or both sides.
- Technique: 2 ways: radioisotope localization and blue dye lymphatic mapping; using both together finds SLN with the highest rate.
  1. Lymphoscintigraphy: inject technetium sulfur colloid beside the melanoma, then wait about 30 minutes, use a handheld gamma probe to scan where the intradermal technetium goes, especially at the chest, abdominal wall or back, whether it runs up to the axilla or down to the groin; wherever the probe says there is a hot spot is where the SLN is, but it is not yet known which node it is. (Figures: injection site, surgically exposed node, lymphoscintigram.)
  3. Next, inject 1 cc of 1% isosulfan as intraoperative mapping (watch for allergic reaction) around the original biopsy or near the tumor site, wait about 5 minutes, then open an incision at the hot spot; the SLN will be stained blue; remove the SLN, send to the pathologist to check by H&E stain and immunohistochemistry: S100, HMB 45, MART-1/Melan-A stain.
  4. In the past, if SLN was positive, LND was done at once; but now this is being questioned as to whether LND is needed: the MCC group study found that in SLN-positive cases that had LND, occult metastasis in other nodes was only 12%; however, LND increased disease-free survival by another 7%; so currently available data still advise LND at once.
- Future: melanoma is common in America, Europe, Australia (people like sunbathing and have many pets - as printed), so many molecular-basis studies are looking for biomarkers to use in diagnosis and treatment choice, giving prognosis similar to breast cancer.

## Treatment of melanoma (p.184)
1. Skin lesion:
   1.1 Whether first presentation or recurrence, if it can be cut out, cut it out.
   1.2 Arm, leg, face, neck, front of chest, hand, foot: do wide local excision; the margin depends on thickness (see table).
   1.3 Under the nail or subungual melanoma: must amputate; the theory is to cut only the distal phalanx, taking dorsal skin more than volar skin and using volar skin to cover the stump. (Figure: subungual and acral melanoma.)
2. Regional LN:
   2.1 If clinically positive or palpable LN, especially nodes 1-1.5 cm, round, hard, attached to adjacent tissue or matted: do FNA or excisional biopsy; if positive, whether micro or macroscopic, do LND.
   2.2 Do not forget SLN study for 1.1-4 mm.
   2.3 Before LND be sure there is no M.
3. Distant metastasis: spreads most to the lung, but can still be treated; the author asks not to let patients waste time or play with expensive things until the hospital... (text continues on next page).

## Management of lymph node metastasis (p.185)
1. Axillary LN: do LND level 1, 2, 3; preserve the long thoracic and thoracodorsal nerves if there is no direct invasion from cancer; number of nodes removed should be more than 15.
2. Neck LN: do MRND level 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, preserving the internal jugular vein, CN 11 and sternocleidomastoid muscle; number of nodes removed must be more than 15.
3. Lesion in front of the ear, e.g. face, anterior scalp, eyelid, cheek, above the lip margin: besides MRND, do superficial parotidectomy; for lesions behind the ear read in the head and neck chapter. (Figure: melanoma near the ear.)
4. Groin LN: composed of the superficial inguinofemoral group and the deep group (iliac and obturator); currently removal of groin nodes with laparoscopic assistance, expanding the space with a balloon port like hernia surgery, has appeared; for the leg, at this time only the inguinofemoral group is removed. (Figure: lymphatic drainage of the inguinal region.)
- (continuing the distant metastasis point from p.184: the author asks that patients not be left to waste time or money on dubious treatments; the rest of this sentence is not study content.)

### Groin node dissection: points often asked (p.186)
1. Superficial inguinal LND (femoral nodes below the inguinal ligament): remove at least 8 nodes; the deep group along the external iliac artery, internal iliac artery and pudendal artery: remove at least 6 nodes.
2. Consider removing the deep group when: 2.1 gross node positive, especially at the apical saphenofemoral node, also called Cloquet node, the most superior node at the femoral ring beside the femoral vein; it must be gross positive, not micro-positive; 2.2 superficial node involvement more than 3 nodes (that is, 4 or more); 2.3 imaging, e.g. CT, shows enlargement of deep nodes (iliac and/or obturator lymphadenopathy).
- But if the node at the aortic bifurcation is enlarged, meaning the disease has gone far, doing LND of both superficial and deep groups is of no use.

## Management of distant metastasis (p.186)
1. Multiple metastases: chemotherapy; currently several new drugs, mostly targeted therapy, are prolonging life.
2. Isolated or single-site metastasis: 2.1 lung, GI, adrenal gland: if resectable, resect; 2.2 CNS: if resectable, craniotomy and resect the tumor, then postoperative RT; 2.3 bone metastasis: palliative RT, except pathological fracture, in which case do ORIF first followed by postoperative RT.

## In-transit metastasis (p.186-187)
- Local recurrence after WLE, with recurrence in the dermis and subcutaneous fat as nodules located between the primary site and the lymph node group. (Figure: leg with multiple in-transit nodules.)
- If a nodule is palpable, do FNA or excisional biopsy.
- If positive from FNA, do re-WLE with margin 3-5 mm, with frozen section control of the margin. (Figure: WLE for in-transit metastasis.)
- Multiple in-transit lesions that cannot be resected: chemotherapy by isolated hyperthermic limb infusion/perfusion (ILI/ILP) with melphalan: insert arterial and venous catheters into that limb, tie a pneumatic tourniquet above the catheter, inject chemotherapy that is warm and let it circulate through the leg; high dose can be used with less systemic toxicity; this gives the best result, with response rate up to 50-60%. (Figure: ILP circuit: drug solution in warm saline, pump chamber, arterial catheter, venous catheter, pneumatic tourniquet, Esmarch bandage, hot air blanket, warming coil; ILI/ILP: in-transit metastases almost gone.)

## Adjuvant therapy (p.187-188)
- Used in cases that cannot be operated on, or spread far; there are drugs to choose from but still many pitfalls; there are several kinds:
- Chemotherapy: CVD formula (cisplatin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) gives response of less than 5%.
- Interferon alpha-2b: useful in LN-positive cases or in-transit metastasis: said to help increase disease-free interval, but overall survival is still questionable.
- Biochemotherapy: chemo mixed with interferon; the figure quoted gives about 12% response.
- Isolated hyperthermic limb perfusion: use for in-transit lesions or multiple recurrence at a limb that cannot be resected, with melphalan at a temperature of 40 degrees C; reports suggest it seems to work.
- Immunotherapy: older textbooks mention injecting BCG or tumor vaccine for patients with distant metastasis; NCCN uses it together with targeted therapy (not needed).
- Targeted therapy in metastatic melanoma: several new drugs; two outstanding: ipilimumab, a drug that blocks activation of T cells, giving 3-year survival 21% (better than BCG injection); and vemurafenib, which selectively blocks the valine codon 600 (BRAF V600) mutation; both FDA approved; for vemurafenib, beware adverse events: skin SCC and keratoacanthoma. Schwartz 11th edition: high dose interleukin-2.

## Head and neck melanoma (p.188)
- More aggressive than melanoma of the chest or the arm and leg; about 26% of mucosal melanoma spreads to neck nodes, while diagnosis is available early, unlike ocular melanoma, which spreads to the liver.
- Treatment: wide local excision; if clinically palpable lymph nodes are found, do regional LND; but in clinically negative nodes, do not remove nodes.

## Melanoma of unknown primary (p.188)
- Found in 2% of node-positive cases where no primary is found; the author referred 1 case, followed 1 year, primary still not found.
- Approach: 1. ask history of previous skin tag excision or regressed lesion; 2. examine the scalp, eyes, ears, under the nails, mucosal areas both in the mouth, paranasal sinus and anus (in women add vaginal exam); 3. if still not found, treat the node first with LND then follow up regularly; on average unknown-primary patients have survival equal to or better than those who know the primary site.

## Melanoma in pregnancy (p.188)
- Diagnosis and treatment follow the approach for non-pregnant patients; no need to terminate pregnancy; do WLE under local anesthesia; SLN biopsy can also be done like breast cancer in pregnancy: use lymphoscintigraphy, but do not use blue dye.
- After finishing treatment, wait about 2-3 years before the next pregnancy.

## Margin for skin cancer (p.188-189)
1. Primary melanoma:

| Thickness | Margin | Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| In situ | 0.5-1 cm | Local recurrence may be present as invasive disease; microscopically, lentigo maligna may extend beyond normal-appearing skin and may require wider margins |
| <=1 mm | 1 cm | |
| 1.01-2 mm | 1-2 cm | A 1 cm margin may be acceptable in anatomic regions where obtaining a 2 cm margin would impact function |
| 2.01-4 mm | 2 cm | |
| >4 mm | 2 cm | Local recurrence rates can be as high as 11%, but no reported benefit in overall survival has been observed with wider margins |
- Note: cutting wider than 2 cm gives no benefit.

2. BCC:

| BCC | Margin (mm) |
|---|---|
| Low risk: trunk and extremities <2 cm; head and neck <1 cm; around eyes, ears, nose, mouth, hands, feet <6 mm | 4-5 |
| High risk: site, size >6 mm; recurrent tumor; immunocompromised patient; in radiotherapy field; morpheaform, sclerosing, micronodular; perineural invasion | 10 |

3. SCC:

| SCC | Margin (mm) |
|---|---|
| Low risk: site/size as per BCC | 4-6 |
| High risk: as per BCC and poorly differentiated; as per BCC and adenoid, adenosquamous, or desmoplastic types | 10 |

4. Depth: cut deep to subcutaneous fat; if no fascia invasion, no need to remove the fascia.
5. If the defect is large, close with graft or flap.
6. For high-risk BCC and SCC, Greenfield believes in Mohs only; Cameron says Mohs if possible, but if not (e.g. no pathologist available, or patient cannot tolerate a long time, since Mohs takes about 2-4 hours longer than WLE), WLE under frozen section control alone is acceptable.

## Rare skin cancers (p.189-191)
- Residents sitting boards should know a few rare diseases only briefly; the exam is usually tricky but easy to guess.
- Know 3: Paget, Kaposi, Merkel. Angiosarcoma and dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP): just know that DFSP is treated like soft tissue sarcoma: best is wide local excision with a margin of 3 cm down to the underlying fascia; imatinib, which treats GIST, also works fairly well in advanced disease.

### Extramammary Paget disease (p.190)
- Rare form of adenocarcinoma from the apocrine glands of the skin; often at the perineum, vulva and scrotum; looks like that at the nipple: eczema-like plaque with scaling.
- Important: 40-50% associate with GU (KUB) and GI malignancy, so investigate these 2 systems as well.
- Treatment: 1. WLE to histologically clear margin; 2. add postoperative RT for locoregional control if invasion into the dermis.

### Kaposi sarcoma (p.190)
- Low-grade malignancy from lymphatic endothelial cells, but Greenfield believes Kaposi is a vascular sarcoma.
- Often in HIV patients or those on long-term immunosuppression.
- Lesion: purple to brown nodules, along arms and legs, often multiple, local lesions; chance of distant metastasis very low, and interestingly some nodules can remit by themselves.
- Location: skin, mucous membrane, GI tract.
- A radiosensitive lesion, so RT is always the main treatment.
- Treatment: start with antiviral medication (impressive, often dramatic response); if no response, seek other drugs: 1. localized lesion: give RT alone; 2. advanced lesion: RT + chemo; 3. surgery only for tissue diagnosis, but some need surgery for lesions that cause problems, e.g. bleeding, or small bowel obstruction.

### Merkel cell cancer (MCC) (p.191)
- Some believe it is an SCC variant; it is now believed to be caused by Merkel cell polyomavirus.
- Arises from neuroendocrine cells or Merkel cells, which are neuronal mechanoreceptors in the skin.
- Often in the elderly, about age 70, with frequent UV exposure or immunosuppression such as AIDS, polyomavirus infection; except RT of skin, which is not related to MCC.
- A single nodule, painless; some present as a red-purple papule.
- Very aggressive, grows and recurs quickly; at diagnosis about 1 in 3 have distant metastasis to liver, lung, bone or brain; about 30% die of this cancer within 5 years.
- Treatment: 1. WLE at least 2-3 cm and down to the fascia, with frozen section; even with this, recurrence is still as high as 55-80%; or use Mohs technique. 2. Because 30% have LN metastasis: Schwartz advises: if node positive do LND followed by RT directly to the WLE site and the nodal basin; if node negative do SLNB. 3. Advanced case: add chemo.
- Prognosis: currently 5-year survival 40-68%.

## Points to remember before finishing (book p.191)
- Necrotizing fasciitis does not involve only the fascia, but even the underlying muscle also dies; treatment must be radical debridement, and using hyperbaric therapy in every case is overdoing it.
- Routine colostomy in Fournier gangrene is excessive, making it more like a mess with many wounds that are hard to care for.
- Actinic keratosis is precancerous for SCC, not BCC.
- Xeroderma pigmentosum or atrophic dermatitis and UVB are precancerous and risk for BCC, SCC and melanoma.
- Nodular BCC is less aggressive, but nodular melanoma is extremely aggressive.
- Marjolin ulcer or post-burn wound means SCC, but rodent ulcer means BCC.
- Prognosis of melanoma depends on depth, not size; and there is no role for Mohs surgery for melanoma.
- No major amputation such as BK or AK for extensive locoregional recurrence of melanoma, except for a foul-smelling or bleeding leg.
