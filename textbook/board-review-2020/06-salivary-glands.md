# Surgery of the Salivary Gland 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 348-364 (PDF pages 352-368)

## Introduction (book p.348)
- Salivary gland disease is a topic for general surgeons that often gets handed to ENT, breast or plastic units; in some hospitals only ENT does it. It appears in boards. The author wants general surgeons to be able to operate on a few cases (e.g. a patient shot in the cheek, then parotid gland injury) to treat correctly. (The rest is a dedication to a teacher; not study content.)

## Anatomy (p.348-350)
- Major glands: 3 pairs: parotid, submandibular, sublingual. (Figure: parotid gland and duct, masseter, submandibular gland and duct, sublingual ducts, lingual frenulum.)
- Minor glands: 750-1000, spread in the mouth, at the lip, tongue margin and palate. (Figure: serous, mucous, mixed areas.)

### Parotid gland (p.349)
- The largest; lies in front of the ear, over the masseter muscle and the ramus of the mandible, covered by parotid fascia, which lies between skin and parotid gland.
- Upper border is the zygomatic arch; lower part lies on the SCM.
- Saliva drains through Stensen's duct, about 5 cm long, passing in front of the masseter muscle, piercing buccal fat and the buccinator muscle, opening into the mouth opposite the upper 2nd molar tooth.
- CN VII runs inside and divides the parotid into superficial and deep lobes.
- A paired gland with lymphoid tissue in the gland, 20-30 follicles; it receives drainage from the parotid itself and from skin in front of the ear; then drains down to the neck, levels 2, 3, 4, 5; does not go to level 1.

### Submandibular gland (p.349)
- Saliva drains via Wharton's duct, opening beside the frenulum of the tongue. (Figures: duct and nerves: lingual nerve, CN IX, CN XII, lingual artery, stylohyoid ligament, hyoglossus, mylohyoid, deep process of submandibular gland.)
- At surgery beware the mandibular branch of CN VII.

### Sublingual gland (p.350)
- Lies directly against the inner table of the mandible; saliva drains through the small Rivinus ducts at the floor of mouth; Rivinus ducts then combine as Bartholin's (sublingual) duct, which opens together with Wharton's duct as a common channel.

### Facial nerve (p.350)
- Exits the skull base at the stylomastoid foramen, with upper and lower trunks; divides into 5 branches (top to bottom): temporal, zygomatic, buccal, mandibular, cervical.
- Passes through the parotid gland: above (superficial to) CN VII is the superficial lobe and below (deep to) CN VII is the deep lobe.
- The branch that, when cut, gives the most serious problem is the temporal branch (as printed; elsewhere the zygomatic branch is cited for eyelid closure), because the eyelid cannot close, causing corneal inflammation and, in some, blindness.
- Stensen's duct runs together with the buccal branch.

### Saliva (p.350)
- Parotid saliva is serous, controlled by parasympathetic fibers that come with the auriculotemporal nerve of CN V; if cut, parotid atrophy or Frey syndrome can result.
- Submandibular saliva has high mucin content, thick, so stones form easily.

## Acute suppurative parotitis (p.351)
- Classic: an old bedridden patient with NG tube, on a ventilator in the ICU, poor oral hygiene and dehydration.
- Most common organism: Staph. aureus 50-90%.
- Symptoms: toxic patient with high fever, pain, swelling and redness in front of the ear; on bimanual palpation, pus may come out of Stensen's duct opening.
- Treatment: 1. antibiotic covering gram-positive and anaerobic bacteria; 2. hydration, do not let the mouth dry, keep saliva thin; 3. mouth care and warm compress on the gland; 4. if no improvement in 12-24 hours, consider US or CT scan to confirm (fluctuation may not be waited for because the parotid fascia is thick and fluctuation is hard to feel); 5. [as printed no item 5] 6. incision at the parotid gland, open the capsule and drain along a line parallel to the branches of CN VII. (Figure: incision options and drain placement relative to facial nerve.)

## Sialolithiasis (p.351-352)
- Found in the submandibular gland up to 90%, while the parotid only 10%, because submandibular saliva is thick mucin; also Wharton's duct is longer and more tortuous than Stensen's duct, and its opening is smaller.
- Symptoms: postprandial pain, i.e. pain after chewing food; colicky pain lasting about 2-3 hours; some have inflammation that comes and goes, or pus from the opening at the frenulum under the tongue.
- Stone location: most often near the duct opening.
- Plain film may show an opaque stone; if a non-opaque stone is suspected, consider sialography first before injecting dye; be sure it is not a tumor.
- Treatment: 1. stone at the opening: squeeze it out; 2. stone inside the duct: use the same method as for a bile duct stone; if it does not work, open the duct directly at the floor of mouth and squeeze the stone out; 3. recurrent: resection of the whole gland.

## Salivary gland tumor (p.352-)
- 80% found in the parotid; about 2% of head and neck cancers.
- History: 1. age: if <20 years, usually benign, and if benign, usually hemangioma; 2. duration: within 2-3 days the cause is likely infection, but weeks and up, especially unilateral, think of tumor; 3. symptoms: patients come with a benign mass, usually not painful, but if painful or rapidly growing beware cancer.
- Physical examination: 1. palpate the outer side first, then look inside the mouth; if the deep lobe is large you see a mass pushing the tonsil and soft palate toward the midline; feel the mass for firm or fluctuation; look at the face for facial palsy; 2. a mass that can be pressed down and recoils, especially in a child, think hemangioma.
3. Features suspicious for cancer: 3.1 large and fixed mass; 3.2 CN VII palsy; 3.3 enlarged lymph nodes; 3.4 skin invasion; 3.5 trismus.

### Investigation (p.353)
1. US: helps evaluate the mass, shows invasive properties somewhat, and guides FNA.
2. CT: for a benign superficial-lobe lesion it is not very necessary, but for a deep lobe lesion CT should be sent; for any suspected malignancy do CT in every case: 2.1 tells whether the mass is parotid or not; 2.2 separates a pharyngeal mass from a deep lobe tumor; 2.3 assesses extent of the cancer and relation to neurovascular structures; 2.4 CT shows bony invasion well, while MRI shows facial nerve invasion.
3. MRI (Schwartz favors MRI): for masses larger than 4 cm: 3.1 shows soft tissue and nerve invasion; 3.2 helps assess minor salivary glands; 3.3 separates tumor from infection; 3.4 reduces artifacts from dental material.
4. FNA (should be done in every case): accurate preoperative diagnosis about 70-80%.
5. Do not do open biopsy or sialography: it makes the cancer spread and the patient die faster.

### FNAC or FNAB (p.353-354)
- Trend: now advised to take a fine needle aspirate, like breast, thyroid and neck node.
- With a skilled cytologist sensitivity, specificity and accuracy are 92-98%; the cytology of the surgeon does not add accuracy.
- FNA results can change the treatment approach in up to 35%.
- Risk of needle tract contamination by tumor is very low.
- Advantages: 1. is the mass a salivary gland or not; if yes, benign or malignant; 2. if malignant, primary or metastasis; 3. if malignant, lymphoma or not; 4. helps in diagnosing when both sides are enlarged; 5. helps decision in high-risk patients who could not even tolerate a local anesthetic or tooth extraction. If FNA is benign, do not bother the patient with surgery; if cancer, 2 options: chemoradiation and surgery.
- 6. Useful in HIV patients with a parotid mass, which is usually a benign lymphoepithelial lesion (TB, cyst) most often; no need for any operation.
- Because of these reasons FNA is recommended before going to the OR.
- Some still believe FNA is not needed since it relies on a cytologist whose reading is difficult (it is hard to interpret and translate; reading malignant does not tell which cancer type); so if lymphoma or inflammatory mass is not suspected, just operate. The theory says do FNA but in practice the problem is that the cytologist does not work (as the author's view).
- Parotid lymphoma: suspect if: 1. parotid gland is very enlarged; 2. cervical nodes enlarged on both sides; 3. other lymph nodes enlarged too; 4. HIV patient.

## Parotid tumor (p.354)
- A mass in front of the tragus of the ear or at the angle of the mandible.
- Unilateral enlargement: think tumor, parotitis or calculi. Bilateral enlargement: think systemic disease, e.g. mumps or fatty infiltration.
- Rule of 80%: 80% of all salivary gland tumors are in the parotid; 80% of parotid tumors are benign; 80% of benign tumors are benign mixed tumor; 80% of parotid tumors are in the superficial lobe.

### Benign mixed tumor (pleomorphic adenoma) (p.354-355)
- Found in 50% of all salivary gland neoplasms. Women > men 4:3; age about 30-40.
- A single mass, unilateral, well-defined border, firm, painless, in front of the ear for years, slightly mobile.
- Malignant transformation 1.5% at 5 years, 10% at 10 years; suspect if a long-standing mass suddenly grows fast or CN VII palsy appears.
- Do superficial parotidectomy at once; do not do enucleation alone, because benign mixed tumor has pseudopods extending out of the capsule, leaving tumor behind, so recurrence is high.

### Warthin tumor (papillary cystadenoma lymphomatosum) (p.355)
- Found in men aged about 40-60; painless, slowly enlarging; found only in the parotid; CN VII involvement very rare.
- Rule of 10%: 10% of benign parotid tumors; 10% are bilateral (think of this disease when both parotids are enlarged); 10% multicentric; 10% turn malignant (but Sabiston 2008 disagrees, saying 100% benign).
- Radionuclide scan shows a hot spot (technique outdated, not done).
- Treatment: superficial parotidectomy alone; RT does not work because it is radioresistant.

## Malignant parotid tumor (p.355-356)
- 20% of all parotid tumors; at diagnosis 20% have already spread to lymph nodes.
- Most common is low-grade mucoepidermoid cancer 44%, then malignant mixed tumor 17%.
- CN VII palsy is found in 12%; 95% of patients with CN VII palsy have cancer.
- Many cell types; prognosis depends on stage more than cell type.
- Metastasis goes to lymph nodes more than hematogenous spread.
- Low-grade cancers: only acinic cell cancer and low-grade mucoepidermoid cancer; the rest are all high grade.
- High-grade cancers: malignant mixed tumor, SCC, high-grade mucoepidermoid cancer, salivary duct cancer, oncocytic cancer, large cell cancer.
- Staging: AJCC 2010, same as head and neck cancer.

### Low-grade mucoepidermoid cancer (p.356)
- Most common type; lives long, dies slowly.
- Wide range of aggressiveness; not like "good by day, bad by night"; it may behave like high grade and die quickly.

### Acinic cell cancer (p.356)
- Very good prognosis.

### Adenoid cystic cancer (p.356)
- Called cylindroma in the old days. Slow growing, submucosal spreading, often perineural invasion causing pain or CN VII palsy, so this cancer should get adjuvant RT. Often bony invasion; can spread to lung.

### Malignant mixed tumor (p.356)
- Believed to be malignant transformation of a long-standing benign mixed tumor (many years), which suddenly enlarges; suspect it has turned cancer; prognosis not good since it goes to nodes quickly.
- This is why a parotid tumor should not be kept: it may betray you one day, we do not know when.

## Surgical treatment (p.356-357)
- Surgery is the main treatment; radiation and chemotherapy are supplements that help delay death.
- Principles of surgery (figure: incision and facial nerve branch preservation): 1. adequate resection margin; 2. cut through normal tissue, do not cut into tumor, since seeding occurs; 3. even if FNA was done and says cancer, use intraoperative frozen section; 4. preserve CN VII as much as possible.
- Superficial parotidectomy: removes only the superficial lobe; used for: 1. benign lesions such as chronic recurrent sialadenitis or stones; 2. low-grade malignant tumor.
- Total parotidectomy: after finishing the superficial lobe, deal with the deep lobe; used for: 1. deep lobe lesion; 2. high-grade malignant tumor; 3. recurrent tumor; 4. mandibular invasion; 5. skin invasion.
- Greenfield advises total parotidectomy for every cancer regardless of low or high grade; the author does not favor this view, as it lacks supporting evidence.

### Surgical tactic (p.358)
1. Anesthesia without muscle relaxant, because facial muscle contraction must be seen.
2. Open the parotid fascia; use small clamps, spread gently and dissect the parotid so that it lifts up, working under the fascia plane; done this way there is no Frey syndrome (as the author puts it).
3. Cauterize bleeding points only as needed (the nerve may be burned); use 4x4 gauze to press, go and dissect elsewhere, come back to look; the bleeding usually stops.

### Facial nerve in parotid surgery (p.358)
1. Try to preserve it: generally find it directly at its origin behind the ear (antegrade technique, finding the nerve from where it leaves the stylomastoid foramen); but if the tumor is large or extends backward, find it from the periphery inward (retrograde technique), which the author prefers because the origin is narrow, a small hole, with thick skin.
2. If CN VII is clearly invaded or encased by the tumor, or already paralyzed: sacrifice CN VII; send frozen section from both ends to get a free margin; then use the great auricular nerve or sural nerve as an interposition graft.
3. Total parotidectomy together with excision of CN VII is called radical parotidectomy.

### Lymph node dissection (LND) (p.358-359)
1. Therapeutic LND: do in every case where cancer has spread to neck nodes; treatment is comprehensive MRND (levels 1-5).
2. Prophylactic LND: do in cases with a chance of nodal spread even if N0. Indications: 2.1 high-grade tumor; 2.2 size >=4 cm (T3).
- Levels of LND: 1. submandibular or sublingual gland: levels 1-3; 2. parotid gland: levels 2-5.

## Adjuvant therapy (p.359)
- RT: in the past it did not work and was sent for RT only for show; but currently the radiation machine model changed to the newer neutron type, so RT is usable, with indications: 1. high-grade tumor (low grade needs no RT); 2. large primary tumor; 3. recurrent tumor; 4. deep lobe tumor (DeVita's Oncology recommends); 5. perineural, vascular or bony invasion; 6. invasion outside the parotid capsule into surrounding tissue; 7. R1 resection (not completely removed) or positive margin; 8. node positive >3 nodes (DeVita) or extracapsular invasion; 9. palliative treatment for unresectable cancer.
- Chemotherapy is still only palliative for: 1. unresectable cancer; 2. distant metastasis; 3. adenocarcinoma.

## Submandibular gland tumor (p.359-360)
- Risk of cancer 50%. Most common benign tumor: benign mixed tumor. Most common malignant tumor: adenoid cystic carcinoma, then malignant mixed tumor.
- Treatment: 1. benign or low-grade cancer: submandibular gland resection only; 2. malignant: besides resecting the gland, do LND at once: 2.1 N0: SOHND only; 2.2 N1: MRND.
- Beware the mandibular branch of CN VII at surgery; landmark is the facial artery; if cut the corner of the mouth droops.

## Minor salivary gland tumor (p.360)
- A submucosal lesion with cancer risk 80-90%; the most aggressive salivary gland tumor, spreading and killing quickly.
- Most common is SCC; at the hard palate cancer risk is about 60%; at the tongue and floor of mouth cancer risk is as high as 90%.
- If operable, operate, but do not forget CT to assess extent of the tumor.
- Treatment: 1. WLE + LND; 2. most are advanced, so give chemoradiation too.

## Facial nerve injury (p.360)
- Facial nerve dysfunction is found in 40%; luckily most are temporary and improve in more than 90% over about 1 year.
- Risk rises if there is inflammation of the parotid gland as well.
- Intraoperative facial nerve monitoring does not reduce the risk of facial palsy; however, outcome depends on the surgeon's hands.

## Sialocele (p.360-361)
- Salivary fistula: found in 1-15% of parotidectomies; generally a minor complication, and the fistula often closes by itself.
- Cause is gland disruption more than duct transection; in the latter, the fistula is hard to close.
- Treatment: 1. compression dressing works well; 2. anticholinergic agent may help; 3. refractory case may need: 3.1 low-dose RT; 3.2 completion parotidectomy; 3.3 tympanic neurectomy.
- Prevention: place closed suction drain for 2-3 days to get adhesion between the skin flap and the underlying parotid parenchyma.

## Frey syndrome (auriculotemporal nerve syndrome) (p.361-362)
- The text says it is very common, maybe up to 50% (even more), but in real reviews it is found at not more than 10%. A late complication, because nerve fiber regeneration takes time: found about 6-11 months after surgery.
- Cause: the auriculotemporal nerve, a branch of CN V carrying parasympathetic fibers that stimulate salivary secretion, is cut; then the regrowing nerve ends connect with sympathetic fibers that stimulate sweat glands and control skin temperature; when eating, the sympathetic fibers are stimulated too, so the cheek goes red and sweats on the operated side. (Figure: normal vs Frey syndrome fiber connections; gustatory sweating.)
- Symptoms: the Frey triad: sweating, flushing, warmth of the skin around the ear and cheek on that side during eating, especially sour food and spicy food; usually mild; most resolve on their own within 2-4 months without anything done, but if it does not go away, treat.
- Treatment: 1. start by explaining to the patient that it is not dangerous, together with topical anticholinergic (scopolamine cream) to reduce sweating; 2. atropine injection is an option; 3. if still not better, inject botox to block the parasympathetic: reported very effective and safe; the text recommends botox as first-line treatment; 4. if all else fails, there are 2 operative ways: 4.1 re-operate and do a neurectomy (easy to say, hard to do); 4.2 re-operate and use a muscle flap, fascia, or silicone sheet placed as a barrier between the skin and the parotid fascia (figure: temporalis fascia flap; sternocleidomastoid muscle flap).

## Points to remember before finishing (book p.362-363)
1. Stones are most common in the submandibular gland.
2. Benign parotid tumor: enucleation is too little; total parotidectomy is too much, except benign deep lobe tumor, which needs total.
3. Warthin tumor is radioresistant and considered benign; surgery should not be enucleation alone because recurrence is high.
4. Do not forget history of skin cancer of the face and ear, since it may have spread to the parotid.
5. Do FNA in every case if possible, except when the cytologist does not work: go straight to superficial parotidectomy.
6. If tumor is suspected, do not do open biopsy or sialography.
7. A parotid mass with CN VII palsy has a 95% chance of being cancer.
8. Before every parotid tumor surgery, do not forget to tell the patient the chance of facial palsy.
9. Frey syndrome is a late-appearing, harmless condition; even when needed to treat, only a little; when the patient is bothered the doctor treats.
