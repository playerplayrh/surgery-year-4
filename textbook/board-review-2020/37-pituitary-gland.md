# PITUITARY GLAND 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 852-860 (PDF pages 1949-1957)

## Introduction
- The pituitary is one of those subjects learned in medical school and long since handed back to the teachers; the surgery "tent" still needs a little, as it may appear within MEN and adrenal gland topics. Written simply, not complicated — knowing this much is enough.

## Anatomy And Physiology
- Endocrine gland in the sella turcica.
- The author has heard it has the highest blood flow in the body per gram of tissue.
- 2 parts:
  1. **Anterior lobe or adenohypophysis** — 80% of the pituitary; secretes TSH, ACTH, GH, LH, FHS (as printed), prolactin
  2. **Posterior lobe or neurohypophysis** — secretes only ADH and oxytocin
- Figure: hypothalamic neurons send releasing hormones (CRF, TRH, GHRH etc.) via portal capillaries to the anterior lobe (TSH, ACTH, GH, PRL); oxytocin and ADH travel down the pituitary stalk to the posterior lobe.
- Just remember the posterior lobe hormones — everything else comes from the anterior lobe.
- ADH increases renal water reabsorption; ADH deficiency → heavy urination = DI.
  1. **Central DI** — e.g. after head injury: polyuria, thirst, rising plasma osmolarity; treat with exogenous vasopressin.
  2. **Renal DI** — the problem is in the kidney: cannot retain water, polyuria like central DI, but vasopressin does not work; for treatment, consult — find someone to help (as printed).
- Oxytocin stimulates uterine contraction.

## Pituitary Tumor
- Most common mass lesion in the sellar region.
- Most common pituitary tumor (printed "pituitry") = pituitary adenoma.
- Pituitary adenomas — 2 groups:
  1. **Endocrine inactive tumor** — still makes hormone, but not enough to cause symptoms; the problem is **mass effect**.
  2. **Endocrine active tumor** — secretes excess hormone → problems of **hormonal effect**.
- **Size**
  1. < 1 cm = microadenoma
  2. > 1 cm = macroadenoma
- **Mass effect** — residents must know what the mass presses on and what problem results.
- Figure: coronal view of the sella — pituitary gland and stalk below the optic chiasm and hypothalamus, sphenoid sinus below; laterally the cavernous sinus containing the internal carotid artery and CN III, IV, V1, V2, VI.
  1. Upward growth presses the optic nerve at the optic chiasma → bitemporal hemianopsia.
  2. Lateral pressure:
     - 2.1 CN 3, 4, 6 → diplopia
     - 2.2 CN 5 → ipsilateral facial numbness
     - 2.3 Internal carotid A. → cerebral infarction
     - 2.4 Third ventricle → hydrocephalus
- Imaging study of choice for pituitary tumor: MRI, both sagittal and coronal sections.

## Endocrine Active Tumor
- **Prolactinoma**
  - Most common pituitary adenoma (40%); mostly microadenoma (< 1 cm), few > 1 cm. Common in women aged 20-50; F : M = 10 : 1.
  - **Symptoms**: intermittent headache, plus:
    1. Women: secondary amenorrhea > galactorrhea
    2. Men: impotence, decreased libido
  - As printed mnemonic: "dry (no menses), breasts leaking (both sides), shrunken testes, lost voice" (จิ๋มแห้ง, นมไหล, ไข่หด, หมดเสียง); lab shows increased prolactin.
  - **Treatment**
    1. The one disease whose main treatment is a dopamine agonist: bromocriptine 1.25-10 mg bid or once daily, or cabergoline 0.5-2 mg OD once a week (as printed); the drug inhibits prolactin secretion, so prolactinoma rarely needs surgery.
    2. **Indications for surgery**
       - 2.1 Cannot take the drug, or drug fails
       - 2.2 Macroadenoma causing problems, e.g. hemorrhage, visual loss
- **Acromegaly**
  - Tumor secreting excess growth hormone; patient's face, hands and feet become large, but body short with short limbs; may have HT and DM; > 90% are macroadenoma.
  - Figure: features of acromegaly — pituitary adenoma (CT or MRI), hypertrophy of sweat & sebaceous glands, galactorrhoea (prolactin), cardiomegaly, hypertension, sexual dysfunction, peripheral neuropathy, visual field defects, prominent supraorbital ridge, large nose and jaw, teeth separated or lacking, abnormal glucose tolerance test, glucosuria/polyuria, spade-shaped hands and feet, arthrosis.
  - May develop life-threatening heart disease, e.g. valve dysfunction and cardiomyopathy.
  - **Treatment**
    1. Mostly macroadenoma, so surgery plus RT.
    2. Somatostatin inhibits GH release — used to prepare for surgery, or in poor-risk cases who cannot tolerate surgery (drag them into the OR and they will surely die).
- **Pituitary Cushing**
  - Tumor secretes excess ACTH, which drives the adrenal cortex to secrete corticosteroid.
  - Treatment: surgery plus RT.
- Others: TSH-secreting tumor → hyperthyroidism; LH and FSH → gonadal insufficiency.

## Management Of Pituitary Tumor
- **Medical treatment**
  1. Bromocriptine for prolactinoma
  2. Somatostatin for acromegaly
- **RT**
  1. Recurrent pituitary tumor
  2. Macroadenoma — combined with surgery
  3. Poor risk
- **Surgical treatment**
  - Almost all pituitary tumors are treated surgically; best surgical approach is transnasal transsphenoidal: small rongeurs cut the sellar floor to expose the pituitary from its inferior aspect, then resect the tumor.
  - Before surgery, evaluate cortisol and thyroid hormone, since both ACTH and TSH will surely fall after surgery — beware postop adrenal crisis.
  - **Contraindications to transsphenoidal approach**
    1. Suprasellar extension
    2. Massive lateral extension
    3. Dumbbell-shaped tumor (printed "dumbbel")

## Craniopharyngioma
- Calcified cyst in the remnants of Rathke's pouch (printed "Rathake"), which lies near the pituitary gland.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Endocrine abnormalities
  2. Visual disturbances
  3. Headache
  4. Hydrocephalus
- Imaging (boxed text on the page):
  - Characteristic finding in a child: an enhancing suprasellar mass that is calcified and cystic on CT; with two of these three features, craniopharyngioma is still the most likely diagnosis.
  - The tumor usually shows T1 high intensity on MR, reflecting protein or cholesterol content of the "motor oil-like" fluid in the tumor cysts.
  - Rarely, on plain skull film it may be suspected from sellar or suprasellar calcifications.
- **Treatment**: surgical resection through the nose, followed by postop RT.
- Common PO complication: diabetes insipidus (DI).

## Nelson's Syndrome
- Seen after bilateral adrenalectomy: CRH rises and stimulates pituitary enlargement.
- The enlarged gland presses the optic chiasma → bitemporal hemianopsia (printed "bitempotal"), with headache.
- Patients often have amenorrhea.
- Classic: hyperpigmentation, from increased beta melanocyte stimulating hormone, a by-product of ACTH.
- Figure caption: generalized hyperpigmentation — face, bilateral adrenalectomy scar, oral mucosa and hands.
- **Treatment**: steroid.
- Note: always remember — some things you simply have to memorize shamelessly (ใช้ความหน้าด้านจำ), without needing to know the reason or mechanism.

## Sheehan Syndrome
- During pregnancy the pituitary enlarges about 50% and needs more blood flow; pituitary ischemia from hemorrhage or shock during delivery → hypopituitarism after delivery.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Postpartum trouble with lactation — often the first sign
  2. Amenorrhea
  3. Adrenal insufficiency
  4. Hypothyroidism
- Photo caption describes the patient's face: puffy, pale, sluggish/lethargic, weak, no menses.
- **Treatment**: hormonal replacement.

## Pituitary Apoplexy
- Bleeding into a pituitary tumor, causing rapid enlargement; pressure on the optic nerve may cause blindness; usually ends with hypopituitarism.
- **Treatment**: fairly emergent — urgent surgery plus hormone replacement.
