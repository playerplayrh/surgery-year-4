# Gastrointestinal Carcinoid Tumors 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 1036-1054 (PDF pages 1040-1058)

- Intro: after the WHO stepped in and tried to systematize the topic, carcinoid was folded into the NET (neuroendocrine tumor) group, which made the carcinoid topic more confusing; this chapter first recaps NET basics briefly, then covers carcinoid by organ of origin as before.

## Neuroendocrine Tumor (NETs)
- NET = tumors arising from neuroendocrine cells (found in many organs), cells that make and secrete hormones.
- Two major groups:
  1. Gastroenteropancreatic NETs (GEP-NETs): 75%, from neuroendocrine cells of the GI tract; divided in 2:
     - 1.1 GI NETs: what was previously called carcinoid.
     - 1.2 Pancreatic NETs: pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (read in the pancreas chapter).
  2. Bronchopulmonary NETs: 25%.

## Pathological Classification
- In the past, the embryological classification was used by location: foregut, midgut, hindgut (WHO does not like it, but the author does).
- WHO 2010 classifies by cell differentiation: well differentiated, poorly differentiated; popular in Europe.

| Grade | Mitotic count (per 10 HPF) | Ki-67 index | Traditional nomenclature | WHO/ENETS nomenclature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grade 1 | <2 | <3% | Carcinoid, islet cell tumour | Neuroendocrine tumour, Grade 1 |
| Grade 2 | 2-20 | 3-20% | (Atypical) carcinoid, islet cell tumour | Neuroendocrine tumour, Grade 2 |
| Grade 3 | >20 | >20% | Small cell carcinoma, large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma | Neuroendocrine carcinoma (large cell or small cell type) |
- Also listed: mixed adenoneuroendocrine carcinoma; hyperplastic and pre-neoplastic lesions.
- AJCC 2010 takes some of WHO but grades: grade 1 (G1) low grade, grade 2 (G2) intermediate grade, grade 3 (G3) high grade.
- Table (AJCC criteria of grading, GEP NETs): Low grade G1: under 2 mitoses/10 HPF and/or under 3% Ki-67, well-differentiated NET. Intermediate grade G2: 2-20 mitoses/10 HPF and/or 3-20% Ki-67, well-differentiated NET. High grade G3: over 20 mitoses/10 HPF and/or over 20% Ki-67, poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma.
- Carcinoid is a neuroendocrine tumor that arises from the enterochromaffin cell, because it stains with chromaffin; some call the cell of origin the Kulchitsky cell; some call it the APUD cell.
- If stained with silver and positive, it is called the argentaffin cell (fancy cell names; reading in a book you will know it is about carcinoid); remember this cell comes from ectoderm, not mesoderm (memorize).
- These cells are found in epithelial organs; found most often in the respiratory tract (bronchus) and the GI tract.
- Carcinoid makes and stores vasoactive substances and several other hormones, e.g. serotonin, chromogranin, synaptophysin and histamine.

### Incidence
- 1-2 cases per 100,000 population in the USA.
- Age 50-60 years; female : male = 5.5 : 4.5.
- 64% originate from the GI tract; GI NETs are rare tumors anyway, and among NETs the number 1 is carcinoid; among GI carcinoid the small bowel is most common.
- 28% from lung and bronchus.

### Classification (by embryologic origin)
1. Foregut: 7%. 2. Midgut: 63%. 3. Hindgut: 30%.
- Foregut: esophagus, stomach, duodenum, liver, GB, pancreas, including trachea, bronchus, lung; found less (7%); can produce carcinoid syndrome and metastasize.
- Midgut: jejunum, ileum, Meckel, appendix, right colon, ovary, testis; carcinoid is found most at the midgut (63%); produces carcinoid syndrome a lot, and metastasis is also a lot.
  - Interesting: a second synchronous primary carcinoid can be found at another site in up to 25%, so at operation one must look for it and remove all; it is associated with CRC in 17-33%. In the past it was believed the appendix is the most common site, which is no longer right; currently rank 1 is the ileum, next rectum and colon, and the appendix has fallen to number 4.
- Hindgut: transverse colon, left colon and rectum.
  - Memorable point: hindgut carcinoid does not produce carcinoid syndrome, but can metastasize; strange.

### Gross pathology
- Whether from a specimen or endoscopy, the classic look: a small nodule, rather firm, well circumscribed, in the submucosa; the cut surface is yellow because of high lipid content (memorize). Figure: endoscopic nodule (skip).
- Around the mass there may be desmoplastic reaction and extensive fibrosis; in the small bowel this may cause kinking and fixation of small bowel loops, causing obstruction. Figures: operative and CT photos (skip).

### Histopathology
- Typical cell: round cells with uniform nuclei and cytoplasm arranged in an orderly monotonous pattern, no necrosis; with this uniform appearance, histology cannot say it is cancer, except rarely for poorly differentiated ones with variable nuclear size, or mitotic figures more than 1 per 10 HF; this means a poor prognosis, tending to be cancer.
- Histologic growth patterns, 5 types (memorize the names):
  1. Insular: cells arranged in peripheral palisading; 22%.
  2. Trabecular: cells arranged in rows or ribbon-like; 18%.
  3. Glandular: arranged as glands or acini or rosette-like; 2%.
  4. Undifferentiated pattern: 4%.
  5. Mixed type: 43%.

## Clinical Features
- Depends on size and origin of the carcinoid, which are very important; physical character and hormonal effect are second.
- Carcinoid is a slow growing tumor with an indolent course; 80% of tumors are small, with no symptoms; often found incidentally from imaging studies.
- When symptoms appear, they are usually long-standing, more than 2 years; and by the time symptoms come from the mass, 80-90% are already advanced stage or metastatic because the mass grows slowly; even with metastasis, patients may live more than 1 year.
- 30-50% are diagnosed by exploring patients with gut obstruction, then the pathology report says carcinoid.

### Aggressive carcinoid tumor
Features that mean aggressive behavior, which should be treated like cancer:
1. Size over 2 cm.
2. Poorly differentiated or undifferentiated tumor.
3. Proliferative activity: mitotic count over 20/10 HPF.
4. Ki-67 index over 20%.

## Carcinoid Syndrome
- Tryptophan metabolism is the pathway of the serotonin that produces the syndrome. Figure (pathway): tryptophan -> (hydroxylase) 5-hydroxytryptophan -> (decarboxylase) 5-hydroxytryptamine (serotonin) -> (monoamine oxidase in liver, aldehyde dehydrogenase in kidney) 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA); the other branch from tryptophan goes to nicotinic acid.
  1. In a normal person: tryptophan from food is oxidized to nicotinic acid 99%; only 1% becomes serotonin.
  2. In a carcinoid patient: abnormal tryptophan metabolism; as much as 60% is oxidized to serotonin and only 40% to nicotinic acid; causing symptoms of serotonin excess and symptoms of nicotinic acid deficiency, which is pellagra.
- Serotonin produced goes through the portal vein to the liver and is metabolized by liver enzyme from active form (memorize) to 5-HIAA, an inactive form, then excreted in urine; so a higher than normal urine 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) suggests the patient is likely carcinoid.
- When carcinoid syndrome symptoms appear, it shows that active serotonin passes into the systemic circulation without being metabolized by liver enzyme in the portal system:
  1. Presence of hepatic metastasis: the tumor in the liver releases serotonin into the hepatic vein, then to the IVC, without liver enzymes in the portal system inactivating it first.
  2. Primary disease outside the GI tract, e.g. bronchus, ovary, testis, bulky retroperitoneal tumor, pancreas; the serotonin of these primaries does not pass the portal vein but enters the systemic circulation directly.
- When there is carcinoid syndrome, think first of small bowel, especially ileum, before others.

### Clinical features of carcinoid syndrome
1. Flushing of the skin: the most common, 85-90%. Figure: patient photos with flushing (skip).
2. Secretory diarrhea: 70%, watery and explosive, episodic after meals.
3. Respiratory: patient has dyspnea, wheezing from bronchospasm.
4. Abdominal pain.
5. Retroperitoneal and pelvic fibrosis.
6. Hepatomegaly.
7. Face telangiectasia.
8. Right sided valvular heart disease, especially tricuspid regurgitation (TR), the most common (see figure); next is pulmonary stenosis (PS); left-sided valves are involved less, not more. Figure: valve specimen (skip).
9. Pellagra: symptoms are 3D: "Diarrhea, Dementia, Dermatitis".
- Gastric carcinoid: found most at the body and fundus; carcinoid symptoms are few; interesting that the syndrome is atypical, since the syndrome of the stomach is from hormonal effect more than serotonin effect (memorize).
- Small bowel carcinoid: found often at the distal third of the ileum and can produce carcinoid syndrome.
- Appendix: usually at the tip. Colon: at the cecum. Rectum: found 4-13 cm above the dentate line.
- Colorectal carcinoid produces syndrome very rarely, especially the rectum, a hindgut, which does not produce syndrome.

| Organ | Usual location | Carcinoid syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Stomach | body and fundus | rare |
| Small bowel | distal third of ileum | common |
| Appendix | tip | rare |
| Colon | cecum | rare |
| Rectum | 4-13 cm above dentate | rare |

- Carcinoid crisis (interesting): a life-threatening condition from release of large amounts of serotonin and catecholamine, causing acute hypotension, tachycardia, arrhythmia, bronchospasm, flushing; crisis is triggered by anesthesia, surgical procedure and chemotherapy.
  - Treatment: IV octreotide bolus 50 micrograms followed by continuous infusion 50 micrograms/hr (memorize).
  - Prevention: give preop octreotide.

## Diagnosis Studies
- Laboratory studies to confirm that it is carcinoid; two commonly used: 1. 24 hr urine 5-HIAA; 2. immunohistochemical markers: the most popular is serum chromogranin A, which helps both diagnosis and follow-up; next, synaptophysin and neuron-specific enolase.
- Serum serotonin level does not work, because it is hit and miss (the level has frequent paroxysmal elevation); other biochemical markers are not helpful.
- Imaging studies: to localize and see extension of the tumor; many choices, e.g. CT, MRI, PET, Octreoscan.
- Endoscopy: suits localization of intraluminal lesions (memorize).

### 24-hour urine 5-HIAA
- Sensitivity 70%, midgut has higher sensitivity than foregut carcinoid; specificity 88%.
- Before the test, the patient should avoid bananas, pineapple, kiwi, plums, tomatoes, beans.
- Helps diagnosis but does not tell prognosis (memorize).

### Serum chromogranin A
- Chromogranin A is in the neurosecretory vesicles of NETs; considered a universal marker.
- Has the highest sensitivity, 99-100%. Figure: tumor secreting CgA into blood (skip).
- Advantages: 1. does not rely on serotonin secretion, so it helps diagnose bronchial and hindgut carcinoid, where 5-HIAA is not high; 2. more sensitive than 5-HIAA, so it is the best for screening and is also used as a marker for follow-up after treatment; 3. level tells prognosis: the higher the value, the poorer (memorize).
- Currently advanced histology can stain chromogranin A from a specimen, helping confirm the diagnosis that the resected tissue sent for examination is really carcinoid.
- Chromogranin B has sensitivity 85%, but chromogranin C has sensitivity of only 5%.

### CT & Octreoscan
- Used most; experts advise doing both.
- Start with CT abdomen/pelvis as the primary study to see the primary lesion and metastasis to lymph node and liver; may also see thickening and shortening of the mesentery from fibrosis; but the drawback of CT is low sensitivity for intraluminal mass, which scope has more sensitivity for.
- Octreoscan: helps diagnosis, tells the location of the tumor and helps evaluate residual disease; because carcinoid expresses membrane peptide somatostatin receptors in more than 80%, sensitivity is 80-90%. Figure: CT liver metastases and Octreoscan.
  - Limitations of Octreoscan: 1. non-functioning tumors do not work well; 2. do not exhibit carcinoid syndrome [as printed].

### PET scan
- Currently not popular as a routine; should be used only in cases where Octreoscan is negative.

## Workup of Stomach, Small Bowel and Colorectal Carcinoid Tumors
- Every patient: CT or MRI abdomen/pelvis.
- Somatostatin receptor scintigraphy and CT chest: in cases where tumor spread is suspected.
- Gastric carcinoid: EGD, biopsy, serum gastrin level.
- Duodenal carcinoid: EGD/EUS.
- Colonoscopy for colon carcinoid; for jejunum and ileum carcinoid choose between CT enterography or capsule endoscopy.
- Rectal carcinoid: endorectal US or MRI.

## Treatment
- Should be a multidisciplinary approach; methods are surgery and non-surgery.

### Surgical treatment
- Objective: 1. curative; 2. cytoreductive to reduce tumor load; 3. palliative for obstruction or bleeding.
- Preoperative preparation: 1. echo to look at the valves; 2. CT and Octreoscan to localize tumor; 3. preoperative octreotide to prevent crisis.
- If the patient is fit enough to operate, consider exploring every case.
- Principles of operation, 5 main ones:
  1. Intraoperative assessment, especially of the primary, which may be hard to find, so aids are needed: 1.1 palpation (lucky if found); 1.2 intraoperative localization with IOUS or bring a scope into the OR; if a lesion is found, tattoo it; 1.3 a method like parathyroid: inject radiolabeled somatostatin before anesthesia, and when the abdomen is opened use a probe to scan for the tumor.
  2. Primary tumor resection.
  3. Do cholecystectomy, because after operation there is a chance that octreotide must be given at a high dose.
  4. If the patient is still stable enough to continue and has tumor at other sites, debulk everything; but if the patient looks bad, finish quickly, close, and choose helpers like drugs or RT for the rest.
  5. If there is liver metastasis, do not hurry to open HBP surgery (NCNE liver metastasis), since it can come out in many ways: if resection can remove less than 90% of the liver tumor, consider OLT; if the mass can be resected over 90%, resection is better; or if after operation it is not safe, TOCE or RFA can be used instead.

### Nonoperative treatment
- For patients not fit enough to operate, or after operation not all removed, the helpers are:
  1. Interferon: from trials, response 40%.
  2. Systemic chemotherapy: commonly used drugs are streptozotocin, 5FU and doxorubicin; response 0-29%.
  3. Targeted therapy, e.g. sunitinib, gefitinib.
  4. RT: in cases of painful bony metastasis.
  5. Octreotide: to reduce serotonin and hormonal effect:
     - 5.1 Long acting sandostatin or lanreotide injected every 14 days.
     - 5.2 Depot octreotide or octreotide LAR: the drug works for about 30 days; if depot is used, inject once a month.
     - 5.3 131I-MIBG octreotide.

## Gastric Carcinoid Tumor
- Mostly an incidental finding at upper endoscopy: a yellowish subepithelial lesion with a dimple in the middle called central umbilication, which looks very much like heterotopic pancreas. Figure: endoscopic and EUS pictures (skip).
- Gastric carcinoid is divided into 4 subtypes.
- Type 1: the most common, 75%; usually women aged 70-80; associated with chronic atrophic gastritis type A, pernicious anemia, and gastrin stimulation of enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cell proliferation, causing hypergastrinemia; the mass of type 1 is usually under 1 cm, indolent course; carcinoid syndrome very rare; metastasis under 10%.
- Type 2: found in 5%; F = M; age about 60; associated with ZE syndrome and MEN 1, so hypergastrinemia like type 1; mass is usually 1.5 cm, indolent, and syndrome rare like type 1; metastasis under 25%; because type 2 resembles type 1, treatment is similar.
- Type 3: 20%; M > F; this type is not associated with hypergastrinemia, unlike types 1 and 2; mass is usually over 1 cm (large); they release a lot of histamine, while serotonin release is not much; 15-25% develop atypical carcinoid syndrome (atypical but flushing is still the star as usual); type 3 is sporadic and very aggressive, so metastasis is found up to 54-66%; type 3 dies quickly.

| Gastric carcinoid | Type 1 | Type 2 | Type 3 (sporadic) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incidence (%) | 75 | 5 | 20 |
| Sex | F | F=M | M |
| Acid output | low | high | ? |
| Hypergastrinemia | yes | yes | ? |
| Carcinoid syndrome | rare | rare | 15-25% |
| Metastasis (%) | <10 | <25 | >50 |
| Prognosis | good | fair | poor |
| Associated | chronic atrophic gastritis | ZE syndrome, MEN1 | - |

- Type 4: a newly added type; usually a single large mass associated with parietal cell hyperplasia; type 4 has a very poor prognosis, spreads fast, and most patients live no more than 1 year (all dead).
- Remember: gastric carcinoid is associated with MEN 1 and atypical carcinoid syndrome; types 1 and 2 are associated with hypergastrinemia.
- Management: look at size and hypergastrinemia together.
  - Type 1 and 2:
    1. Size under 1 cm: endoscopic or limited resection.
    2. Size 1-2 cm: still debated where to cut; the principle is to lower gastrin, since carcinoid in the stomach is believed to grow fast in a hypergastrin environment, but if gastrin is low the tumor can regress:
       - 2.1 The popular choice is to do little: just antrectomy (usually type 1) with tumor resection, because these patients are old and have a low chance of syndrome; the author agrees with this approach.
       - 2.2 For type 2 (ZE syndrome): resect the primary gastrinoma (read more in the pancreas chapter).
       - 2.3 Aggressive ones are few: this group is advised to have subtotal or total gastrectomy and clear nodes as D1.
    3. Size over 2 cm, recurrent tumor or more than 6 masses: all agree on subtotal or total gastrectomy with D1.
  - Type 3: very aggressive, so treat like gastric adenocarcinoma: D1 or D2 subtotal or total gastrectomy only.
  - Table (summary): types 1,2: under 1 cm endoscopic or limited resection; 1-2 cm antrectomy + tumor resection; over 2 cm D1 gastrectomy; type 3: D1 gastrectomy.

## Duodenal Carcinoid Tumor
- Common location is the 1st and 2nd parts.
- Mostly an incidental finding at upper endoscopy: a submucosal mass like gastric carcinoid.
- 85% are under 2 cm, with few symptoms; if symptoms occur, they relate to obstruction.
- Treatment: depends mainly on size (figure: endoscopic resection steps, skip):
  1. Under 1 cm: choose endoscopic first before open transduodenal excision.
  2. 1-2 cm: still debated whether to do as for 1 cm or open for segmental resection.
  3. Over 2 cm: treat aggressively like cancer: open, segment resection with lymphadenectomy, or may need PPPD.

## Small Bowel Carcinoid Tumor
- The site most often found. Figure: specimen and operative photos (skip).
- Small bowel carcinoid is found at the distal third, about 2-3 ft from the IC valve.
- Multicentricity 30-50%.
- Second primary about 20-30%.
- 88% are found by chance (incidentally). Figure: ileal carcinoid CT (hypervascular mass in the wall of a distal ileal loop near a nodal mesenteric mass; dilated loops from obstruction by the primary tumor).
- If there is carcinoid syndrome, it shows the tumor has already gone to the liver.
- Be suspicious in a patient with a small bowel tumor and mesenteric fibrosis, called desmoplastic reaction.
- Relation of size with node involvement and metastasis:

| Size (cm) | % LN involvement | % metastasis |
|---|---|---|
| <1 | 20-30 | 0 |
| 1-2 | 60-80 | 20 |
| >2 | >80 | 40-50 |

- Treatment:
  1. Segmental resection with lymphadenectomy.
  2. Should add cholecystectomy, because after operation octreotide may be needed, which causes gallbladder stasis, especially in cases with only palliative resection.
  3. Even if liver metastasis is found preop or intraop, most experts still advise small bowel resection with lymphadenectomy, to reduce complications at the primary site, which often is obstruction from the mass.
  4. Liver metastasis: use the same principles as above.

## Appendiceal Carcinoid Tumor
- Generally an incidental finding from the pathology report, over 90% in patients whose preoperative diagnosis was acute appendicitis. Figure: gross and microscopic pictures (skip).
- 90% are at the tip, 10% at the base; 70% are under 1 cm.
- Chance of multicentricity about 4%, and a second primary found in 13%.
- Note: for appendiceal cancer, currently adenocarcinoma is found more than carcinoid.
- Treatment:
  1. If size is under 2 cm and tumor confined to the appendix, appendectomy alone is accepted, because positive node is under 3%; next step in management is surveillance with abdominal CT.
  2. Indication for re-exploration and right hemicolectomy: 2.1 size over 2 cm (positive lymph node 30-60%); 2.2 unknown tumor size; 2.3 initial incomplete resection; 2.4 base location with positive margin; 2.5 positive margin; 2.6 mesoappendix invasion; 2.7 angiolymphatic involvement; 2.8 serosal involvement; 2.9 mixed histology (goblet cell or adenocarcinoma tumor); 2.10 high mitotic index or high grade tumor (intermediate or high); 2.11 Shackelford 2019 p.1956 advises, if the patient is young, even a tumor over 1 cm should go straight to right hemicolectomy.
  - Figure (algorithm): appendiceal carcinoid: 1 cm or less: appendectomy; over 1 to 2 cm: if location at tip or mid appendix: appendectomy; if location at base, mesoappendiceal invasion, or metastases: right hemicolectomy; over 2 cm: right hemicolectomy.

## Colon Carcinoid Tumor
- 50% are found at the cecum.
- As usual, often found incidentally at colonoscopy. Figure: endoscopic picture (skip).
- Few symptoms; when symptoms appear the mass is usually large, more than 60% have a mass over 2 cm, and at diagnosis 70% have metastasis, so the prognosis is very poor.
- Colon carcinoid has little chance of multicentricity or of producing carcinoid syndrome; the patient's symptoms resemble adenocarcinoma, so they are hard to tell apart; the diagnosis usually comes from the pathology after operation.
- Second primary found in 25-40%.
- Treatment: same as colon adenocarcinoma, except in highly selected cases of intramucosal lesion not over 1 cm, where endoscopic submucosal dissection is acceptable.

## Rectal Carcinoid Tumor
- Interesting: the teacher has examined on it.
- Usually found 4-13 cm above the dentate line.
- As usual 85% have no symptoms; if there are symptoms they are anorectal discomfort, bleeding or bowel habit change. Figure: endoscopy, EUS and resection (skip).
- Key point: rectal carcinoid does not produce syndrome (memorize).
- Do ERUS to see tumor thickness; the cut point is muscle invasion: if there is muscle invasion, or it is T2 positive, treat like cancer.
- Size: under 1 cm has 5% metastasis, but over 2 cm has 70% already metastasized.
- Treatment: rectal carcinoid differs from colon carcinoid in that rectal carcinoid has more chance of being removed by endoscope:
  1. Size under 1 cm, no symptoms, must not be ulcerated (memorize this too), EUS shows no lymph node involvement, lesion not over T2 and must be N0, no lymphovascular or perineural invasion: local excision by transanal, TEM, or endoscopic resection.
  2. Size 1-2 cm: still debated whether to do as in item 1 or go for radical cancer-type resection; NCCN endorses local excision.
  3. Size over 2 cm, or smaller but with ulceration, symptoms at diagnosis, T stage over T2 or positive N, or poorly differentiated tumors such as small cell or large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma: must have radical oncologic resection straight away, may be LAR or APR.
- Prognosis: even with metastasis, the prognosis is still better than adenocarcinoma, because they are mostly well differentiated and do not secrete hormone.

## Metastatic Disease
- Currently the role of surgery is accepted as reasonable and worth doing: try to remove the primary tumor and metastatic foci as completely as possible, except where there is truly clinically significant tumor burden, then give life-long octreotide or lanreotide.

## Future Directions
- Challenges waiting for answers in the future, 3 issues (listed as 4 items):
  1. Tumor marker: to help diagnose the disease sooner, since waiting for symptoms is usually late, not less than 2 years, and metastasis is usually present already.
  2. Localization: for small tumors.
  3. Targeted therapy: now in phase 3 trial with sunitinib.
  4. The group with size 1-2 cm, especially at the duodenum and rectum, which still debates whether to treat like cancer or only local excision; those who work on neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) set helpers for the decision:
     - 4.1 If well differentiated or low grade histology: local excision is acceptable.
     - 4.2 No angioinvasion: local excision is OK.
     - 4.3 Look at the proliferation index (Ki-67): if under 2 it points toward benign; if over 20/10 HPF it points toward cancer; the intermediate ones should be considered case by case.
- For pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNETs), read the pancreas chapter and multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) too.
