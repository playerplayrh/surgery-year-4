## Parathyroid
- Chief cells make parathyroid hormone = **parathormone (PTH)**.
- PTH is the single most important regulator of calcium metabolism: direct effect on bone and kidney, indirect effect on GI.
- Half-life of PTH about 2-4 minutes.

## Anatomy
- 90% have 4 glands (2 upper, 2 lower); 9% have more than 4 (supernumerary) — the extra glands are mostly in the thymus, then within the thyroid; about 1% have only 3.
- **Size** 5×3×2 mm; each weighs about 35-50 mg.
- **Colour**: yellowish-brown or reddish-brown. **Shape**: oval, like a bean or kidney.
- **Texture**: fairly soft and elastic; thin capsule with small blood vessels on it.
- At surgery, touching the gland makes it turn dark/bruised; cutting the gland gives oozing bleeding.
- **Telling parathyroid from other neck tissue**
  1. From fat: fat is yellower and more friable
  2. From thyroid: thyroid is brighter red and firmer
  3. From lymph node: node is firmer, larger, thicker capsule, and purplish
- **Blood supply**: both upper and lower glands from the inferior thyroid artery (remember — จำ).
- **Relation to recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN)**
  1. Superior gland lies dorsal to the RLN
  2. Inferior gland lies ventral to the RLN

## Calcium Metabolism
- **PTH** is the single most important factor; it raises serum calcium via:
  1. **Kidney**: increases calcium resorption at the distal tubule and phosphate excretion at the proximal tubule
  2. **Bone**: increases osteoclasts, releasing calcium into serum
  3. **GI**: increases active vitamin D so more calcium is absorbed from the GI tract
- Figure: PTH stimulates bone resorption, tubular reabsorption, and (via 1,25(OH)2D3 from the kidney) gut absorption → increases serum calcium.
- PTH has direct effects on kidney and bone; GI effect is indirect (remember — จำ).
- **Vitamin D**: increases GI calcium absorption.
- **Calcitonin** lowers calcium by:
  1. Reducing bone calcium resorption (osteoclast inhibition)
  2. Increasing calcium and phosphate excretion in urine

## Embryology
- Parathyroids arise from the 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches, developing into the superior and inferior glands.

## Superior Gland
- Figure: distribution of superior gland positions around the posterior thyroid — percentages drawn 80%, 12%, 4%, 2%, 1%, 0.8%, 0.2%.
- From the 4th pharyngeal pouch; descends with the thyroid; related to the tubercle of Zuckerkandl.
- Constant location in 80-90%.
- **Position**: around the junction of the upper 1/3 and lower 2/3, toward the back of the thyroid lobe, similar on both sides; or about 1 cm above the crossing of the inferior thyroid artery and recurrent laryngeal nerve.
- **Ectopic locations**
  1. Tracheoesophageal groove
  2. Central neck compartment
  3. Retropharyngeal plane
  4. Above the upper pole of the thyroid

## Inferior Gland
- Figure: distribution of inferior gland positions — mostly around the lower pole (44%, 26%, 17%), with smaller percentages higher in the neck (6%, 2%, 1%, 1%, 0.8%, 0%) and in the mediastinum (2%, 0.2%).
- From the 3rd pharyngeal pouch; descends with the thymus (not the thyroid), so it may go deep into the mediastinum — the lower pair's runway is long, so location varies a lot.
- 60% lie behind the lower pole of the thyroid, anterior to the RLN; otherwise, when exploring 4 glands, it is a matter of luck — the 4th gland may not be found.
- **Simple summary** (สรุปง่ายๆ)
  1. Upper from 4, lower from 3
  2. Upper with thyroid, lower with thymus
  3. Upper constant, lower varies
  4. Supplied by the inferior thyroid artery

## Hypercalcemia
- Most body calcium is stored in bone; normal intake 1-3 gm/day, excreted via GI (not kidney).
- **Normal value** 9-11.
- 55% is bound to serum protein (non-ionized form); 45% is free (ionized form), the active form (remember — จำ) for neuromuscular activity.
- **Causes**
  1. Hyperparathyroidism (HPT): 1ry and 3ry, except 2ry
  2. Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH)
  3. Malignancy — from bone metastasis or a cancer making PTH-like hormone; most common breast, then thyroid, lung, kidney, prostate, lymphoma
  4. Endocrine disease, e.g. adrenal insufficiency, Graves', pheochromocytoma, Vipoma (PET group)
  5. Granuloma, e.g. TB, sarcoidosis, leprosy
  6. Drugs
     - 6.1 Thiazide (remember) — absolutely forbidden in hypercalcemia because it blocks renal calcium excretion
     - 6.2 Lithium, vitamin A and D
  7. Prolonged immobilization
- **Points professors may use to trick you**
  1. Pancreatitis usually has hypocalcemia — except pancreatitis with HPT, which has hypercalcemia
  2. Terminal ileal resection gives hypocalcemia — calcium is bound by free fatty acid
- **For the exam only (remember)**
  1. Most common cause of hypercalcemia overall and in OPD patients = 1ry HPT
  2. Most common cause of hypercalcemia in IPD patients = breast cancer
- Classic symptoms ending in "-oan" (สระโอน: stones, bones, groans, moans…) are read under 1ry HPT.
- EKG: bradycardia, short QT and widened T wave.
- **Treatment** — depends on whether the patient is non-urgent or in crisis:
  1. More fluids — hypercalcemic patients are dehydrated
  2. Restrict calcium diet to ≤ 500 mg/day
  3. Avoid drugs that raise calcium: thiazide, lithium
  4. Treat the cause
     - 4.1 Granulomatous disease such as sarcoidosis (what is that disease? — the author does not know) → steroid
     - 4.2 Cancer → mithramycin or plicamycin, a chemo drug that lowers calcium by inhibiting osteoclasts; others such as 5FU are a decoy
     - 4.3 Renal failure → may need HD or CAPD
  5. Follow up: serum calcium every 6 months; serum creatinine, plain KUB for stones, and bone density yearly
  6. In the waiting group, after 10 years of follow-up about 10% end up with parathyroidectomy

## Hypercalcemic Crisis
- (Heading printed "Criss".) An emergency needing urgent treatment: clear classic "-oan" symptoms and serum calcium > 14 mg/dL.
- Patient sweats, drowsy, confused without head injury, thirsty from volume depletion, nausea, vomiting, muscle weakness.
- **Treatment**
  1. The heart of treatment and the first thing to do: rapid hydration — load NSS about 2 times the calculated amount
  2. Then lasix; no thiazide
  3. Continuous cardiac monitor
  4. Treat the cause of the crisis
- Other treatments lower calcium but are not for crisis — too short-acting or too slow, e.g.:
  1. Oral biphosphonate — takes 24-48 hours to work
  2. Subcut calcitonin — fast but very short-acting
  3. Parathyroidectomy during crisis — whoever thinks of it is in trouble for sure (as printed)

## Indications for Surgery
- Which hypercalcemic patients need parathyroidectomy has been debated between endocrine physicians and endocrine surgeons (who should get drugs, who should be sent to surgery), leading to NIH consensus conference guidelines: first 1990, then 2002, 2009, and latest 2014. Because delayed surgery raises long-term risk, especially bone mineralization, the trend is to operate sooner. The author took the points both sides agree on and split the surgical indications into asymptomatic and symptomatic — looks best.
- **Asymptomatic** — even with only high calcium and no symptoms, operate if:
  1. Serum calcium above the upper normal limit by ≥ 1 mg/dL
  2. Creatinine clearance reduced > 30% or GFR < 60 ml/min (stage 3 renal insufficiency)
  3. Bone mineral density (BMD) at distal 1/3 radius, hip, femoral neck, lumbar spine: T score ≤ 2.5 (as printed) shows bone loss — used for women and men > 50 years; men < 50 years operate if Z-score < 2.5 (as printed)
  4. Age ≤ 50 years (remember — not > 50)
  5. Patient cannot attend regular follow-up
  6. Patient preference
  - Note: 24-hour urine calcium > 400 mg/dL is no longer an indication for surgery.
- **Symptomatic**
  1. Previous hypercalcemia crisis attack (lucky to have survived a close call)
  2. Nephrocalcinosis or renal stone on x-ray, US or CT
  3. Bone symptoms, e.g.:
     - 3.1 Osteitis fibrosa cystica — very classic, shows rapid bone turnover
     - 3.2 Brown tumor — porous bone with cysts alternating with solid areas, brown from hemosiderin deposition
     - 3.3 Pathological fracture, especially spine — seems a common indication locally
- In 2016 endocrine surgeons met and added indications for surgery:
  1. Neurological or neuropsychiatric symptoms — confusion, memory decline, or sleep disorders
  2. Risk of CHF or coronary artery narrowing
  3. Gastroesophageal reflux
  4. Muscle weakness — weak, unable to work
- **Good response to parathyroidectomy**
  1. Neuromuscular symptoms — proximal and respiratory muscles stronger
  2. Psychiatric symptoms — alert, coherent, not confused, no longer depressed
  3. Increase in bone density, especially lumbar spine and hip
  4. Less urinary calcium → fewer kidney stones
- No response after parathyroidectomy: hypertension and renal impairment (damaged kidneys stay damaged).
- Patients without surgical indications — check up:
  1. Serum calcium yearly
  2. BMD at least 3 sites yearly; back pain or height loss → film spine
  3. Serum creatinine and eGFR yearly; suspected renal stone → CT

## Hyperparathyroidism
- 3 forms:
  1. **Primary HPT** — disease of the parathyroid itself; mostly adenoma, fewer hyperplasia, very few malignant. In primary, PTH high, serum and urine Ca high.
  2. **Secondary HPT** — renal failure patients cannot excrete phosphate → high serum phosphate → body keeps pulling calcium out until hypocalcemia → parathyroids are driven to secrete PTH. In secondary, PTH high; serum Ca low, normal, or may be slightly high.
  3. **Tertiary HPT** — after long secondary HPT stimulation, all 4 glands get irritable and work autonomously → PTH high, serum Ca high.
- **Pseudo HPT**: renal cell cancer and lung SCC secrete PTH-related peptide; though not PTH from the parathyroid, it can cause hypercalcemia.

## Primary Hyperparathyroidism (1ry HPT)
- (Heading printed "Primaey Hyperparathyroism".)
- Now found more often — from check-ups, osteoporosis work-up, and urologists working up stones.
- Causes: mostly disease of the parathyroid itself, mostly sporadic: adenoma 90%, hyperplasia 9%, cancer 1%; a minority are part of MEN 1 — always think of it.
- **Lab**: PTH high, serum Ca high, urine Ca high, serum chloride high, serum phosphate low; Cl : PO4 ratio > 33.
- **Risk factors**
  1. Previous neck RT
  2. Previous RAI for Graves' disease
  3. MEN 1
  4. Over expression of PRAD 1 oncogene
- Most are asymptomatic; about 20% symptomatic; the elderly may show dementia; some come with bone or muscle pain — atypical symptoms.

### Manifestations of Primary Hyperparathyroidism

| System | Signs and symptoms | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Renal | Nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, recurrent UTI, renal impairment | Hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, hyperphosphaturia; calcium phosphate precipitates in alkaline urine, calcium oxalate stones form; stones in renal pelvis or collecting ducts raise infection risk |
| Renal | Polyuria, dehydration | Hypercalcemia directly affects renal tubules → decreased response to antidiuretic hormone |
| Musculoskeletal | Osteoporosis, osteitis fibrosa cystica, fractures, muscle weakness, myalgia | Excess PTH → metabolic acidosis, bone resorption, myopathic changes |
| Musculoskeletal | Arthralgia, arthritis | Hyperuricemia → gout, pseudogout |
| Gastrointestinal | Abdominal pain, constipation, anorexia, nausea, vomiting | Hypercalcemia decreases GI motility, stimulates the central vomiting center, increases gastrin secretion |
| Gastrointestinal | Peptic ulcer disease | Hypercalcemia stimulates elevated hydrochloric acid secretion |
| Gastrointestinal | Pancreatitis (less common) | Exact mechanism unknown; gastrin weakly stimulates pancreatic enzymes and gallbladder contraction |
| Neurologic/psychiatric | Memory impairment, depression, anxiety, confusion, stupor, coma | Hypercalcemia induces neuropathy, EEG changes |
| Cardiovascular | Hypertension | Hypercalcemia directly affects arterial smooth muscle and elevates plasma renin activity |

(Sources printed: Brashers et al. Pathophysiology 2015; Michels and Kelly, Am Fam Physician 2013; Bilezikian et al. J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2014.)
- Simply: symptomatic patients have symptoms ending in "-oan" — 4 "oans":
  1. **Bone** — bone pain, osteoporosis, easy fractures, muscle weakness. Locally bone pain is very common, but in the text about 15%.
  2. **Stone** — kidney stones in the parenchyma (nephrocalcinosis), renal pelvis or ureter → back pain, flank pain, hematuria; in the text about 30%.
  3. **Abdominal groan** — abdominal pain from peptic ulcer, pancreatitis, or constipation, but no diarrhea.
  4. **Psychic moan** — anxiety, depression.
- **Lab**: serum calcium and PTH both high.
- **Classic 1ry HPT** — think of 1ry HPT with:
  1. Chloride : phosphate ratio > 33. Phosphate is rarely given in surgical exam questions; when it is, beware 1ry HPT.
  2. Serum calcium : creatinine clearance ratio > 0.02
  3. Film hand: subperiosteal cortical resorption at the radial side of the middle phalanx — notched look; very classic, as the arrows show.
  4. Film mandible or long bone: multiloculated radiolucent mass — bone cyst or osteitis fibrosa cystica.
  5. Film skull: salt & pepper appearance — also seen in multiple myeloma.
- **Indications for surgery**: see the NIH guideline summarized above; even pregnant patients can be operated on — in the second trimester.
- **Preoperative localization**: in the view of the big shots — endocrine surgery masters such as Orlo H. Clark — any small procedure (light like a breeze; small-leg surgeons, as printed) still needs to bow to them (เงี่ยหูฟัง): the best localization is bilateral neck exploration, examining all 4 glands — the most reliable method; in expert hands bilateral neck exploration has a 95% success rate.
  - Orlo H. Clark uses localization such as MIBI scan only for reoperation.
  - Now most doctors think preop localization is needed, especially for minimally invasive surgery (widely used now) or recurrent cases needing reoperation.
  - **Current guideline the author finds suitable and most would accept**:
    1. Once primary HPT is diagnosed, decide sporadic vs MEN 1.
    2. In sporadic cases, MIBI scan first:
       - 2.1 MIBI +ve, mass in the neck or upper mediastinum → port-hole minimal invasive parathyroidectomy
       - 2.2 MIBI +ve, mass in the mediastinum below the aortic arch → VATS radioguided parathyroidectomy
       - 2.3 MIBI -ve → US; if found, port-hole surgery; if not found, 2 options: bilateral internal jugular vein sampling, or intraoperative localization with gamma probe (printed "intraopeartive") — if found, port-hole; if all that still fails → bilateral neck exploration.
- Figure: primary HPT localization algorithm, as steps —
  1. Primary hyperparathyroidism → sestamibi scan.
  2. Sestamibi + → minimally invasive parathyroidectomy.
  3. Sestamibi − → ultrasound: + → minimally invasive parathyroidectomy; − → bilateral internal jugular venous sampling.
  4. Venous sampling + → minimally invasive parathyroidectomy; − → bilateral exploration.
    3. In MEN 1 — no MIBI needed: definitely more than 1 enlarged gland; no port-hole surgery; do bilateral neck exploration.
- **Preoperative localization**: surgeons now like to know where abnormal glands are before operating, because parathyroid surgery now favours minimally invasive surgery — shorter operation, no long hospital stay, can be done under local or regional block.
  - Use at least 2 localization methods for more accurate localization:
    1. **Tc 99m sestamibi scan** — taken up by parathyroid mitochondria; useful when the parathyroid is overactive, as it holds MIBI longer than the thyroid; on delayed scan at 2-3 hours the parathyroid stands out. MIBI is the most sensitive preoperative study for adenoma, 80-90%. Weaknesses: false +ve with thyroid tumor or enlarged lymph nodes; errors in small lesions; sensitivity < 50% in hyperplasia (multi-gland disease) seen in 2ry and 3ry HPT. Now MIBI is usually combined with neck US.
    2. **High-resolution neck US** — sensitivity 70-80%; discrete oval, homogeneous hypoechoic nodule (as in the figure). Limits: poor for ectopic glands deep in the mediastinum; normal-sized or small glands are often negative because they are isoechoic with the thyroid.
    3. **CT** — in the neck, sensitivity no better than neck US, but good for finding ectopic glands in the mediastinum, especially **4D CT**, which gives structural & functional information: contrast enhances parathyroid tissue and scans during uptake and wash out; 4D CT detects abnormal parathyroid location in 88-92%.
    4. MRI and PET/CT are no better than CT.
- **Intraoperative localization**
  1. **Radioguided MIBI + gamma probe** — similar to SLN detection in breast cancer: inject MIBI 2 hours before surgery, then probe in the OR. Less used now — complicated, cumbersome, time-consuming.
  2. **Intraoperative PTH monitoring** — easy and practical, since PTH half-life is about 2-4 minutes; after removing the diseased gland, serum PTH falls > 50% within 10-30 minutes.
  3. **IOUS**
- **Surgery in hyperparathyroidism**
  1. Traditional parathyroidectomy: bilateral neck exploration to see all 4 glands; needs GA and frozen section.
  2. Minimally invasive parathyroidectomy under regional or local anesthesia: unilateral neck exploration; needs preoperative localization.

## Principles of Traditional Parathyroidectomy
1. Before surgery, hydrate the patient and lower calcium as much as possible — best if normal.
2. Position supine with slight neck hyperextension, head and neck higher than the trunk. If autotransplantation is planned, prep the forearm skin too. Incision as for thyroid; the thyroid arterial supply need not be ligated.
3. The author advises ligating and cutting the middle thyroid vein so that lobe can be lifted easily to see behind it.
4. Parathyroids are mirror images — normally both sides are similar in size and position. Some look for the upper pair first because its position is fairly constant, though it is a little harder to find (lies deep). Others look for the lower pair first, under the lower pole of the thyroid, which is shallower and easier to find; the problem is that the lower pair's position varies a lot.
5. If a lower gland is not found, especially the 4th, know where ectopic glands may be:
   - 5.1 Missing inferior glands are most often in the thymus — start by pulling the thyrothymic ligament to bring up the thymus above the sternal notch, open the central compartment, look and palpate.
   - 5.2 Still not found → open the carotid sheath, check in front up to the carotid bifurcation, and behind the esophagus.
   - 5.3 Use IOUS.
   - 5.4 Still not found → the surgeon is out of options; reasonable to stop — do not go cutting glands at random.
   - 5.5 Before closing, biopsy 1 gland to be sure it is parathyroid, then clip the positions of the 3 glands to help find them at re-exploration.
   - 5.6 Before reopening, do localization first.
   - 5.7 When the 4th gland is never found, some do thyroid lobectomy taking the lower pole, as there may be an adenoma under the thyroid capsule — most people disagree.
   - 5.8 Doing sternotomy to find the 4th gland — whoever does it deserves to die (as printed).
   - 5.9 Advice: handle parathyroids gently; hold the tissue beside the gland rather than the gland itself; do not break the capsule — seeding can cause new hypercalcemia after surgery.
   - 5.10 Remember: the best way to tell adenoma from hyperplasia is the naked eye during surgery — see all 4 glands.
6. Once all 4 glands are found:
   - 6.1 **One enlarged gland** — 75% lower gland; may be adenoma throughout, or adenoma compressing normal parathyroid to the rim. Remove the enlarged gland, and biopsy a normal gland for frozen section to be sure normal parathyroid remains.
   - 6.2 **2 enlarged glands** — remove the 2 enlarged (remember), and frozen the remaining glands to rule out hyperplasia.
   - 6.3 **3 enlarged, 1 normal** — debated endlessly among the big names. Advice: remove the 3 enlarged glands, biopsy the normal one for F/S to confirm parathyroid and keep it. The small-leg group (as printed) suggests removing only the largest gland and clipping the rest; if hypercalcemia persists after surgery, come back for the other 2 enlarged glands later.
   - 6.4 **All 4 enlarged** — 3 methods with similarly good results:
     1. **Subtotal parathyroidectomy** — remove 3½ glands: take the largest 3 first, then half of the 4th. Suits tertiary HPT after renal transplantation. Partially resect the most normal-looking gland first. Problem: recurrence needs reopening the neck — difficult.
     2. **Total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation** — after removing all glands, send frozen section to confirm parathyroid, then slice the most normal-looking gland into small pieces of 1-2 mm, about 20 pieces, buried in the subcutaneous layer or brachioradialis muscle of the nondominant forearm (printed "forarm"); mark the site with a metal clip or nonabsorbable suture. Remaining parathyroid tissue is kept by cryopreservation for later autotransplantation.
        - Test whether the autotransplant works: draw blood from both arms and compare — the grafted arm has PTH at least 1.5 times higher.
     3. **Total parathyroidectomy without autotransplantation** — increasingly popular, safe; aims to minimize recurrence, and the thymus still makes PTH. Suits 2ry HPT patients unlikely to get a kidney transplant and patients with calciphylaxis.

## Minimally Invasive Parathyroidectomy
- Residents should know about 2 methods, increasingly used by endocrine surgeons today.
1. **Radioguided parathyroidectomy** — inject MIBI 2 hours before surgery, sweep with the gamma probe to locate the parathyroid, make a small incision, remove that gland, and confirm with IOPTH that the diseased gland is truly out. No longer popular — takes longer and adds no information beyond preoperative MIBI.
2. **Endoscopic parathyroidectomy**
3. **Minimally invasive videoscopically assisted parathyroidectomy** — surgery using a VATS scope.

## Consider Criteria for Minimally Invasive Parathyroidectomy
1. Available preoperative localization
2. Single gland disease
3. Not familial 1ry HPT or MEN 1, MEN 2A — these often have hyperplasia
4. No associated thyroid disease needing bilateral neck exploration
5. Available intraoperative PTH assay

## Parathyroid Cancer
- 1% of primary HPT.
- Think of it in young patients with HPT who have:
  1. Very high serum calcium, possibly > 14 mg/dL; serum PTH > 5 times normal
  2. Fairly severe hypercalcemia symptoms
  3. Palpable hard neck mass in about 30% (printed "palpaple"), whereas in HPT the mass is usually not palpable
- **Diagnosis**: frozen section is hard to rely on; criteria for malignancy look at invasion of nearby tissue (common); 15% node metastasis and 33% distant metastasis to lung from the start.
- Surgery is the best treatment — even if not curative, at least debulking as much as possible lowers PTH and calcium and improves quality of life.
- At surgery the mass is greyish-white or brownish (dull), fairly adherent to nearby tissue/organs such as neck muscle, thyroid, recurrent laryngeal nerve, esophagus.
- **Histopathology**: the "tent" says remember — frozen section for cancer is unreliable; accuracy needs permanent section showing vascular, capsular or fibrous stroma invasion and many mitoses. But beware: these features can also appear in benign adenoma.
- **Treatment**
  1. Neck exploration + en bloc resection of adherent muscle or soft tissue; try to preserve the RLN unless truly stuck to the nerve; + thyroid lobectomy on that side + CND on that side, sweeping nodes at tracheo-esophageal, paratracheal and upper mediastinum (remember).
  2. +ve lateral node → MRND.
- Since node +ve is 30%, some recommend MRND even in N0; but few favour it — too costly — so it is not required.
- Problem: operated thinking adenoma, but pathology returns cancer → go back in and do the cancer operation: en bloc + thyroid lobectomy + CND on that side.
- Even after resection, if local neck recurrence occurs, consider re-resection.
- Postop RT to the neck for positive margin or tumor rupture during surgery.
- Chemo is not much use.
- **Cinacalcet**: a calcimimetic that increases sensitivity of the parathyroid calcium-sensing receptor; lowers PTH within 1-2 hours; very effective for controlling hypercalcemia.

## Metastatic Cancer
- Cancer spreading to the parathyroid is rare — whoever sees it is very unlucky.
- Primary usually from breast, melanoma, lung.

## Familial Hyperparathyroidism
- Seen in inherited syndromes such as MEN 1 and MEN 2A; how to treat, and which associated disease to treat first, is in the multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) chapter.

## Familial Hypocalciuric Hypercalcemia (FHH)
- AD; mutation of the calcium sensing receptor gene.
- PTH high, serum calcium high.
- Urine calcium low — this separates it from 1ry HPT, where urine calcium is high.
- Mostly asymptomatic; little treatment needed — observation and treat hypercalcemia problems.
- Parathyroidectomy is a disgrace — whoever does it is very wrong.

## Neonatal Hyperparathyroidism
- From mutation of the CASR gene (as printed: whatever gene, the author is fed up with it).
- **Symptoms**: severe hypercalcemia, lethargy (printed "lrthargy"), hypotonia and mental retardation.
- **Treatment**: urgent, or the baby will surely die.
  1. Total thyroidectomy with autotransplantation (as printed) is the best option
  2. Subtotal parathyroidectomy is acceptable, but recurrence is high

## Recurrent or Persistent Hyperparathyroidism
- About 5% (remember).
- **Recurrence**: calcium normal after surgery, then hypercalcemia returns after 6 months.
- **Persistent**: calcium never falls after surgery; more common than recurrence.
- **Causes**
  1. Truly missed diagnosis, e.g. cancer or TB
  2. Supernumerary gland (printed "supranumerary")
  3. Missed parathyroid gland, e.g. fat or LN removed instead
  4. Capsule broken → parathyroid implantation
  5. 4th gland not found
- **Approach**
  1. Check urine calcium — FHH or not
  2. Review pathology — was what was removed truly parathyroid
  3. Non-invasive MIBI, cervical US or 4D-CT; if +ve → parathyroidectomy
  4. MIBI and US -ve → selective venous catheterization for PTH:
     - 4.1 +ve and gland localized → operate, ideally with both gamma probe and intraoperative PTH assay
     - 4.2 Still not localized → do not plunge in; medical treatment for now, then reinvestigate
  5. Gland localized but patient unfit for surgery → may inject alcohol under US guidance

- Figure: management of recurrent or persistent HPT, as steps —
  1. Confirm diagnosis; rule out FHH; review operative notes and pathology.
  2. Noninvasive localization studies: positive → parathyroidectomy; negative → selective venous catheterization for PTH.

## Secondary Hyperparathyroidism (2ry HPT)
- Common in chronic renal failure; next in vitamin D deficiency, malnutrition.
- Failing kidneys cannot excrete phosphate, so calcium is excreted instead → hypocalcemia → stimulates heavy PTH synthesis and release → PTH high, serum calcium low. Key point: **serum phosphate is very high** — remember only 2ry HPT has very high serum phosphate.
- Chronic renal failure also reduces conversion of vitamin D to its active form → less GI calcium absorption → calcium falls further.
- In secondary HPT the parathyroids show hyperplasia — all 4 glands enlarged.
- **Treatment**
  1. Medical treatment is best — works in 90%:
     - 1.1 Low phosphate diet ≤ 1,000 mg/day
     - 1.2 Oral calcium and vitamin D to reduce PTH secretion
     - 1.3 Phosphate binding agent, e.g. carbonate jel (as printed)
     - 1.4 Calcimimetic, e.g. cinacalcet (sensipar) — binds the parathyroid receptor to reduce the response to low calcium → lower PTH
     - 1.5 Treat underlying disease — renal failure needs dialysis or transplant
  2. Surgical treatment — only about 10% go to the OR: long-stimulated glands become nodular hyperplasia that responds poorly to medical treatment, so surgery is needed.
- **Indications for surgery** (remember — จำ)
  1. Uncontrolled or persistent HPT
  2. Intractable & severe pruritus — itching worse than a social-media itch, disturbing daily life; after surgery itching resolves in up to 85%
  3. Extra-skeletal or ectopic calcification — calcium deposits in tissues outside bone, including vessel walls or heart valves
  4. Severe osteodystrophy — heavy bone calcium breakdown: bone pain, fracture, deformity, or progressive bone loss replaced by fibrous tissue = osteitis fibrosa cystica
  5. Calciphylaxis — a danger situation: vascular calcification narrows vessels → tissue necrosis, very painful, usually both arms or legs; found only in 2ry HPT (not 1ry). For the parathyroid: urgent total parathyroidectomy; the limb usually ends in amputation, with new problems, as prognosis is very poor.
  6. Japanese group recommendation (JSDT 2013, from nephrologists and parathyroid surgeons): operated patients live longer because cardiovascular risk falls and BP is better controlled:
     - 6.1 Intact PTH > 500 pg/ml (USA uses > 600), or whole PTH > 300 pg/ml
     - 6.2 Parathyroid size on US > 1 cm, or combined US volume > 500 mm³
     - 6.3 Anemia resistant to erythropoietin
     - 6.4 Elevated alkaline phosphatase
  7. Hyperphosphatemia
  8. Uncontrollable HT
  9. Impaired myocardial perfusion (cardiomyopathy)
  10. Peripheral neuropathy, psychiatric irritability
  11. Patient preference
  12. Medical surveillance difficult
- **Current indications for parathyroidectomy in Schwartz 2019** — from the National Kidney Foundation KDOQI (kidney disease quality outcomes initiative): patients already fully treated by endocrine physicians who still have:
  1. Severe HPT: PTH > 800 pg/ml
  2. Hypercalcemia, osteoporosis or pathological fracture
  3. Symptoms and signs such as severe itching, bone pain, severe vascular calcification (printed "cascular"), myopathy
  4. Calciphylaxis
- Before surgery, the parathyroid gurus say preoperative localization is not needed — most are hyperplasia, and all 4 glands should be explored; keep MIBI or CT for recurrent or persistent HPT. The problem is the gurus are mostly old and in poor health, and recurrence rates reach 10-30%; so current advice: do preoperative localization if possible — finding ectopic or supernumerary glands helps reduce re-operation.
- **Current concept of surgery for 2ry HPT**: operate earlier, without waiting for full indications, because of vascular and valve calcification after drug treatment. If calcium and phosphate are hard to control, send to the OR sooner.
- **Preoperative treatment**: 1 day before surgery, non-heparinized hemodialysis to correct fluid and electrolytes.
- **Operative treatment** — 3 methods:
  1. **Subtotal parathyroidectomy** — leave about 50-100 mg of an inferior gland, since reoperation is easier there than for an upper gland. Cut the gland to be left first, to see whether it loses blood supply; mark the remnant with a titanium clip for future re-entry. Suits patients who tolerate hypocalcemia poorly — the most-tired type (as printed).
  2. **Total parathyroidectomy without autotransplantation** — suits patients expected to live not long, with no plan for kidney transplant; subtotal leaves parathyroid that may recur during dialysis.
  3. **Total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation** — choose a normal-looking gland without scar, nodule or calcification; slice into 1-2 mm pieces; preferably bury in brachioradialis (printed "radiobrachialis") of the arm rather than sternocleidomastoid — if it recurs, removing tissue from the arm under local anesthesia is easier than the neck; graft function can be checked by PTH in both arms — grafted side > 1.5 times the other means the graft works.
- **Postoperative outcome for secondary HPT**
  1. Improves: less bone pain, less itching, fatigued muscles become firmer (meaning arm and leg muscles — do not imagine too much), less soft tissue calcification and calciphylaxis; overall better QoL.
  2. Does not improve: vascular and valve calcification — once hard, stays hard (do not imagine about the heart valve — as printed). Hence the school of thought favouring surgery before vessels and heart valves calcify.
- **Poor risk** patients — sending them to the OR is like sending them to meet Indra (as printed: likely to die); with chest pain, or parathyroid in a hard-to-operate position, or gland not found before surgery, re-entry risks scattering the RLN → give calcimimetic cinacalcet HCL first, which competes for the calcium sensing receptor.

## Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism (3ry HPT)
- After 2ry HPT with relentless parathyroid stimulation, the glands become confused, believing calcium is always low, and work autonomously regardless of calcium level.
- Renal failure or renal transplant patients: about 3-5% develop 3ry HPT.
- PTH high, calcium high, phosphate low.
- **Treatment**
  1. Medical treatment does not work as well as in 2ry HPT
  2. Surgical treatment, if needed, as for 2ry HPT
- **Indications for surgery**
  1. Serum calcium > 11.5 mg/dL, or persistent hypercalcemia > 10.2 mg/dL for > 3 months after renal transplant
  2. Persistent or autonomous PTH secretion > 1 year after successful renal transplant
  3. Severe osteopenia (low BMD) with bone pain or pathological fracture
  4. Nephrocalcinosis, renal stone
  5. Acute pancreatitis
  6. Peptic ulcer
  7. Pruritus, fatigue

## Hypoparathyroidism
- Most common cause: previous thyroid surgery.
- Treatment: see the thyroid chapter, postoperative complications.
- Postoperative hypocalcemia after parathyroidectomy — think of:
  1. Bone hunger
  2. Remaining parathyroid hypofunctioning or not working
  3. Hypomagnesemia
