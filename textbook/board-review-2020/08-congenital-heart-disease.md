## Fetal and Newborn Circulation
- Know this a bit — it has been asked, quite often.

## Fetal Circulation
- During pregnancy the fetus does not use its lungs for oxygen exchange; it gets oxygen from the mother's blood via the placenta. From the placenta, blood runs in the umbilical vein in the cord to the portal vein, bypasses the liver via the ductus venosus shunt directly into the IVC, then into the right atrium.
- Since the lungs are not used, oxygenated blood from the right atrium passes through the foramen ovale into the left atrium, then the left ventricle, then the aorta to supply the organs — blood does not enter the fetal lungs at all.
- After oxygen is released to the cells, the deoxygenated blood with waste returns to the mother for excretion and oxygen via the umbilical artery.
- Some blood does enter the pulmonary artery, but the fetal lungs are full of amniotic fluid → high (+ve) pressure in the lungs, so blood cannot enter; it shunts through the ductus arteriosus (derived from the 6th aortic arch) into the aorta. After birth, once blood can enter the lungs, the ductus arteriosus closes.
- The placental fetal circulation has 2 umbilical arteries and 1 umbilical vein.

## Newborn Circulation
- When the baby emerges from the birth canal (vagina) and cries, the first breath drives amniotic fluid out of the alveoli → lungs expand; alveolar pressure goes from positive to negative.
- Pulmonary pressure falls → blood is drawn into the lungs via the pulmonary artery to take up oxygen from the alveoli, returns by the pulmonary veins to the Lt heart, then out through the aorta.
- With rising blood O2 tension the ductus arteriosus is no longer needed → it contracts and closes.
- When Lt atrium pressure rises, the foramen ovale closes.
- When the umbilical cord is cut, the ductus venosus closes.
- What residents must remember: the infant's pulmonary artery pressure falls to adult level within 3 months.

## Congenital Heart Disease
- **Non-cyanotic heart disease**
  1. Lt-to-Rt shunt: ASD, VSD, PDA — blood from the Lt heart goes through the defect back to the Rt heart and into the lungs again instead of all leaving through the aorta → CHF (first sign = hepatomegaly), tachycardia, tachypnea, failure to thrive
  2. Obstructive lesions, e.g. AS, PS, coarctation of aorta
- **Cyanotic heart disease** — these usually start with T: Tetralogy of Fallot, Transposition of the great vessels, Tricuspid atresia, Tricuspid valve in Ebstein anomaly, persistent Truncus arteriosus, Total anomalous pulmonary venous return.
- Most common congenital heart disease = VSD.
- Most common congenital heart disease at birth = VSD.
- Most common congenital heart disease diagnosed in adulthood = ASD.

## Atrial Septal Defect (ASD)
- A hole in the atrial septum → Lt-to-Rt shunt.
- 3 types:
  1. Ostium primum (30%), also called atrioventricular canal defect or endocardial cushion defect: hole lies in front of the fossa ovalis; often with other cardiac anomalies, most commonly mitral insufficiency, then tricuspid valve
  2. **Ostium secondum or ovale type** (70%): most common; hole at the fossa ovalis or about mid-septum; after closure, residual problems are uncommon
  3. **Sinus venosus defect**: hole near the SVC or IVC
- Child tires easily, not cyanotic.
- Heart sound: systolic ejection murmur at Lt parasternal border, 2nd space.
- CXR: enlarged Rt atrium and Rt ventricle with increased pulmonary vasculature.
- Echo: accurate diagnosis, shows location and size of the defect more clearly.
- **Management**
  1. Observation: ASD < 6 mm may close within 2 years (beyond that, closure is unlikely)
  2. Open repair: asymptomatic ASD should be repaired at age 4-5, except large defects, especially ostium primum; with heart failure operate sooner. Direct suture or patch repair with pericardium.
  3. Percutaneous closure with a transcatheter umbrella-like device that opens to plug the hole; suits secondum (ovale type); in skilled hands closes the defect in 95%.

## Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD)
- Most common congenital heart disease; a hole in the ventricular septum.
- May have associated anomalies: PDA, AI, coarctation of aorta.
- Most common type = perimembranous (80%), then subarterial, atrioventricular, muscular type.
- Symptoms: large defect → CHF, failure to thrive, tachypnea, tachycardia.
- Most sensitive indicator of CHF in children = hepatic enlargement.
- Precordial pansystolic murmur at Lt parasternal border, 3rd-4th space.
- **Management**
  1. Small VSD: spontaneous closure 80% (as printed: "80% in 1 month, 30% in 6 months and 25% within 1 year")
  2. Large VSD: often CHF or AR after 4-6 weeks; treat CHF and repair early — some need surgery at 3-6 months. Usually VSD is repaired at preschool age (about 4-5 years).
- **Repair**
  1. Open repair = standard treatment
  2. Percutaneous catheter-directed membranous occlude (as printed) is an option, but results are not as good as in ASD
- Major determinant of VSD operability = pulmonary vascular resistance; if > 50% of systemic resistance → inoperable case.

## Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA)
- In utero the ductus arteriosus stays open thanks to prostaglandin E2 from the mother; after birth, the placenta is delivered and without prostaglandin E2 the smooth muscle contracts and the ductus closes.
- Normally the ductus closes completely, becoming the fibrous ligamentum arteriosum, within 2 months.
- If it fails to close → abnormal communication between the descending aorta and pulmonary artery.
- Common in premature infants and when the mother had rubella in pregnancy.
- **Physical examination**
  1. Bounding pulse from wide pulse pressure
  2. Continuous systolic murmur or machinery murmur
- CXR: increased lung marking.
- Echo: diagnoses PDA and shows blood flow.
- **Causes of death in PDA**
  1. Infants: CHF
  2. Untreated PDA at age 20-30: bacterial endocarditis
- **Management**
  1. **Preterm**: PDA usually from abnormal surrounding mediators, especially much residual prostaglandin E2 from the placenta. Indomethacin 0.1-0.2 mg/kg every 8 hours × 3 doses blocks prostaglandin so the defect can close.
  2. **Term**: PDA usually from an abnormal ductal wall that does not close with drugs. Timing of surgery depends on symptoms: heart failure not controlled → operate now; no or few symptoms → operate at 6 months to 1 year.
     - 2.1 PDA < 6 mm → percutaneous transcatheter closure
     - 2.2 Large PDA → open or VATS, clip, ligate, or divide and close both ends

## Eisenmenger Syndrome
- Normally the volume of blood going to the lungs (pulmonary blood flow, Qp) equals the volume leaving the heart to the body (systemic blood flow, Qs).
- So normally Qp = Qs, or Qp : Qs = 1.
- Eisenmenger (Eissenmenger, as printed) is irreversible: a Lt-to-Rt shunt sends more and more blood to the lungs until pulmonary vascular resistance and pulmonary pressure exceed systemic (Qp > Qs; Qp : Qs > 1) → vessel walls thicken, lumens narrow progressively, until blood cannot enter the lungs → reverse shunt Rt to Lt = Eisenmenger syndrome; the patient becomes cyanotic and dies.
- So ASD, VSD or PDA patients older than 40 need cardiac cath first to assess pulmonary hypertension; if very high or reverse shunt already present → do not close the defect.
- Figure text: Right >> Left shunting = cyanosis; increased cellularity (muscular and interstitial) >> fixed pulmonary vascular resistance = Eisenmenger syndrome.
- So a non-cyanotic case that suddenly turns cyanotic: remember — absolutely do not close the defect (ห้ามปิด defect เด็ดขาด); reverse shunt has occurred.
- Time to Eisenmenger syndrome is unpredictable — depends on defect size; with a very large defect a child can be cyanotic by 2 years.

## Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)
- Most common cyanotic heart disease (Rt-to-Lt shunt).
- Components:
  1. VSD (not ASD)
  2. Pulmonary stenosis (PS)
  3. Overriding of the aorta (as printed "overlyding")
  4. Rt ventricular hypertrophy
- PS → right ventricular outflow tract obstruction → blood reaches the lungs poorly; with the VSD, blood shunts through the hole without passing the lungs → cyanotic from birth (blue baby); episodes of transient loss of consciousness from O2 lack = hypoxic spell. The child likes to sit knee-chest (squatting, bent forward) to raise systemic vascular resistance and increase venous return, sending more blood to the lungs and getting more oxygen.
- Because of the VSD, TOF children get brain abscess and paradoxical emboli more than with other congenital heart diseases.
- Fingertips become bulbous = clubbing finger.
- Heart sound: systolic ejection murmur at Lt parasternal border.
- Hb usually > 20.
- Chest x-ray: boot shaped heart. (Figure text: typical "boot-shaped" heart secondary to RVH and small main pulmonary artery segment; pulmonary vascular markings decreased.)
- Short survival: follow-up of TOF children shows 30% die within 2 years; by age 7 no more than 20% survive. Survivors have poor quality of life — intermittent hypoxic spells, poor growth, stunted.
- **Management**
  1. Formerly TOF surgery was 2-stage:
     - 1.1 Modified Blalock-Taussing (as printed) shunt — a temporary bypass before age 3 months so systemic blood reaches the pulmonary system for oxygen; just enough to relieve cyanosis
     - Figure: shunt types labelled — modified Blalock-Taussig, classic Blalock-Taussig, Waterston shunt, Potts shunt.
     - 1.2 Wait until age 3-6 years, then complete repair; after TOF repair beware arrhythmia
  2. Now one-stage is popular:
     - 2.1 Mild cases: usually wait until > 3 months or weight > 4 kg, then repair; unless the child is very sick, then 2-stage
     - 2.2 Symptomatic: weight > 2.5 kg → repair at once; < 2.5 kg → 2-stage

## Transposition of the Great Vessels (TGA)
- Complex cyanotic disease, 2nd most common (after TOF).
- A true surgical emergency in the newborn: the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left ventricle, so only deoxygenated blood goes to the body. Without an ASD, VSD or PDA, oxygenated blood cannot reach the body; most die within 6 months.
- Cardiac surgeons divide TGA into 4 groups (general surgery residents and medical students need not know).
- Symptoms: cyanotic from birth, heart failure, too weak to suck milk.
- **Chest x-ray**: heart shadow egg-shaped (TGA: egg-shaped or apple on a string).
- **Diagnosis**: echo and cath.
- **Treatment**: the child's condition is key. If very poor → palliative transcatheter balloon atrial septotomy to allow blood mixing so blood going to the body has a higher % oxygen. Then repair within 2 weeks after birth with atrial switch operation (as printed "swtch") — what cardiac surgeons call the Mustard operation — moving the vessels back to their proper places; how it is done is the cardiac surgeons' business.

## Summary: Auscultatory Finding in Congenital Heart Disease
- ASD: systolic ejection murmur
- VSD: pan-systolic murmur
- PDA: continuous systolic or machinery murmur

## Summary: CXR Characteristic of Congenital Heart Disease
- ASD: Rt-sided cardiac enlargement
- VSD: Lt-sided cardiac enlargement
- PDA: cardiomegaly & prominent pulmonary artery
- TOF: Rt ventricular hypertrophy & boot shaped heart

## Coarctation of the Aorta (COA)
- Narrowing or waist of the thoracic aorta; most common site near the ligamentum arteriosum or ductus arteriosus, or the aortic isthmus just distal to the Lt subclavian artery.
- May occur with bicuspid aortic valve, then ASD, VSD, PDA.
- Mild narrowing is often asymptomatic, diagnosed when the child is older.
- Severe narrowing → heart failure, usually within 6 weeks.
- **Symptoms** — by position relative to the narrowing:
  1. Above it: headache, symptoms from aortic aneurysm, chest pain from coronary artery disease, left ventricular hypertrophy; the key suggestive feature is severe upper extremity hypertension
  2. Below it: weak or absent lower extremity pulses, and problems from reduced renal perfusion
- Classic chest x-ray (in asymptomatic patients): blood reaches the legs via newly opened collaterals — subclavian artery branches to intercostal arteries to the lower aorta → rib notching on the inferior rib margins from prominent intercostal artery pulsation (arrows).
- Work up: echo, CTA.
- **Management**
  1. Symptomatic: repair at about age 1-2, unless symptoms are severe — then operate at once. Asymptomatic cases also need surgery, because the high pressure in the descending aorta (as printed) makes the aortic valve leak.
  2. Treatment of choice: Lt thoracotomy, resect the narrowed segment and reanastomose (best), or patch the narrowing.
     - Figure: repair options — (A) resection/end-to-end, (B) patch angioplasty, (C) interposition graft, (D) subclavian flap.
  3. Endovascular treatment is now starting: percutaneous balloon dilation + stent, with good results; mostly used in recurrence after surgery.
- Beware post-op problems (may appear in MCQ):
  1. Patient may still have HT even after surgical correction
  2. Abdominal pain from sudden perfusion of visceral arteries — treat with antihypertensive drug (จำ)
  3. Recurrent COA after surgical repair → balloon dilatation alone; no stent needed
