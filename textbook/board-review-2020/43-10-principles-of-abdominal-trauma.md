# Principles of Abdominal Trauma
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 338-370 (PDF pages 2472-2504) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 4 มกราคม 2559)

- This chapter covers the approach to abdominal trauma patients and the principles of trauma laparotomy. Specific organs (liver, spleen, stomach, bowel) have their own chapters.

## Initial Assessment and Management
- Follow ATLS.
- **History to ask the patient or whoever brought them**
  1. Mechanism of injury: how it happened; driver or passenger; speed; was it fast; hit from the side or the front; if thrown out, landed on head or side; wearing a safety belt or not
  2. Timing of injury
  3. Status at scene: shock? unconscious?
  4. If able to talk, ask the complaint
- About 20% of patients look normal at first — little abdominal pain, only a seat-belt mark or skin bruise. Do not be careless (อย่าประมาท): if history and mechanism are fairly severe, admit, observe and do serial physical examination.
- PR in every case, especially with associated pelvic fracture — strongly stressed in ATLS 9th ed.; a rectal mucosal tear found on PR should raise suspicion of pelvic fracture.

## External Anatomy of the Abdomen
- **Anterior abdomen**: upper border = line between both nipples (T4); lower = both inguinal regions and pubic symphysis; lateral = both anterior axillary lines.
- **Flank**: upper = 6th ICS at the inferior scapular tip; lower = iliac crest; lateral = area between anterior and posterior axillary lines.
- **Back**: upper = tip of scapula; lower = iliac crest; lateral = area between both posterior axillary lines.
- **Thoracoabdominal**: upper = nipple (4th ICS) anteriorly and tip of scapula (7th ICS) posteriorly; lower = inferior costal margin.

## Blunt Abdiminal Trauma (as printed)
- From a direct blow to the abdomen or deceleration injury, e.g. fall from height.
- If there is an indication to operate — e.g. peritonitis, distended tense abdomen with shock and no evidence of bleeding elsewhere → OR, explore laparotomy.
- No obvious indication for laparotomy:
  1. Unstable → FAST; if FAST does not work, DPL. Schwartz 10th ed. uses DPA instead of DPL.
  2. Stable → if one best answer, the author chooses CT; using US as screening first per the Schwartz 10th ed. guideline, then CT if significant injury is suspected, is also acceptable.
  3. Algorithms from Schwartz (9th and 10th ed. are the same) compared with Mattox's Trauma:

Figure: Schwartz blunt abdominal trauma algorithm, as steps —
1. Hemodynamically stable?
   - No → FAST: + → laparotomy; equivocal → DPA → + → laparotomy.
   - Yes → step 2.
2. Peritonitis? — Yes → laparotomy. No → FAST.
3. FAST + → candidate for nonoperative management or patient with cirrhosis? — No → laparotomy; Yes → abdominal CT.
4. FAST negative → indications for CT (altered mental status, confounding injury, gross hematuria, pelvic fracture, abdominal tenderness, unexplained Hct < 35%)? — Yes → abdominal CT; No → repeat FAST in 30 minutes.

Figure: Mattox blunt abdominal trauma algorithm, as steps —
1. Hemodynamically abnormal → FAST available?
   - Yes: positive → laparotomy; negative or equivocal → DPL.
   - No → DPL: positive → laparotomy; negative → observe.
2. Hemodynamically normal → CT available?
   - Yes: positive → laparotomy if indicated; negative → observe.
   - No → FAST vs DPL: positive → laparotomy; negative → observe.

## Penetrating Abdominal injury
- Injury from stab wound or gunshot wound.
- Now, even when a stab wound has passed through the peritoneum, 30-50% of operations prove unnecessary, so nonoperative management is increasingly recommended.
- Even GSW penetrating the abdomen hits a vital organ in 90%, although some recommend selective management in some patients. For GSW to the anterior abdomen the author opens every case, except a bullet in the RUQ, a well-looking patient with stable vital signs, or a trajectory unlikely to enter the abdomen (e.g. through flank or back) — then DPL, CT or serial examination, case by case.
- **Patients for nonoperative management**
  1. Stable vital signs, fully alert, no need for blood
  2. No peritonitis
  3. No bowel or omentum protruding from the wound
  4. Alert, talking sense, no brain or spinal cord injury, not drunk, no need for sedation or anaesthesia, and a doctor can follow the patient all the time
- **Indication for explore laparotomy**
  1. Shock or hemodynamically unstable
  2. Peritonitis
  3. Evisceration
  4. Hematemesis or gross blood in NG tube
  5. Hematuria
  6. Bleeding per rectum
  7. Knife still impaled in the abdomen
  8. GSW with the bullet entering the abdominal cavity
- Stomach or omentum protruding from the wound: do not push it back; cover with gauze soaked in NSS and take the patient to OR.

## Stab Wound
- Indication to operate → operate.
- No indication yet → depends on the site:
- **Anterior abdominal wound**
  1. Local wound exploration (LWE)
  2. LWE +ve → two options:
     - 2.1 CT or DPL; if +ve → operate.
     - 2.2 Serial PE/labs: examine at least every 2 hr for the first 6 hr, then every 6-12 hr for 24-48 hr; if the abdomen becomes rigid or the patient worsens → operate. The author prefers 2.2, as does Schwartz 10th ed.
- **Back and flank wound**
  1. Rely mainly on physical exam; equivocal cases, besides observation, should get CT with triple contrast.
  2. Do not probe or do LWE — a wound near the 11th or 12th rib may enter the chest or the abdomen; probing a chest-entering wound is guaranteed trouble.
- **Thoracoabdominal wound**
  1. Indication to operate → operate.
  2. No indication → exclude diaphragmatic injury by laparoscopy or thoracoscopy. If only one, the author chooses laparoscopy — done supine, and can convert to open surgery straight away if needed.
  - Thoracoscopy needs a decubitus position; converting to open surgery means turning the patient supine and re-prepping and draping — a big waste of time.

## GSW Abdomen
- The trend is still to operate, as before.
- Low-velocity injury (1,000 ft/sec): damage confined to the missile tract.
- High-velocity injury (> 1,000 ft/sec): blast effect and cavitation in addition to missile-tract damage.
- **Indication for explore laparotomy in GSW**
  1. Shock, peritonitis or evisceration → operate on all.
  2. Even without abdominal signs or other indication, if the bullet track passes through the abdomen or the bullet lies in the abdomen — even questionable — take to OR; safer.
- Unnecessary operation in GSW is about 10-20%; GSW to the back has significant injury in about 50-66%; so selective management is increasingly recommended, with more supporting reports. Nonoperative management only when:
  1. Vital signs stable
  2. No peritonitis
  3. No GI bleeding
  4. Patient talks sensibly
  5. Capacity to observe: doctors, OR, ICU, lab and blood bank all ready
  6. Investigations such as CT available
- **SGW** (as printed): about 1/3 of anterior abdominal SGW and about 2/3 of back SGW have no serious intra-abdominal injury. So with no indication to explore → investigate, e.g. triple-contrast CT, DPL or laparoscopy.
- **Nonoperative management in penetrating abdominal trauma**: an interesting EAST guideline (J Trauma 2010), recommended by Schwartz 10th ed. for selective management of GSW abdomen — no routine laparotomy for hemodynamically stable patients; a tangential entry wound in the RUQ usually injures only the liver; with no peritoneal sign → CT abdomen and pelvis first; if no injury needing laparotomy → observe with serial examination and FU 24 hr; if stable and eating → home. Use the EAST/Schwartz 10th ed. approach only in a trauma-center level hospital with available surgical teams.

## GSW RUQ, Flank or Back
- Besides physical exam and triple-contrast enhanced CT, some big names say explore laparotomy is a safe approach for GSW here in hospitals not ready for after-hours CT. The author thinks CT first is safer — gives information before going in.
- Algorithm for penetrating abdominal injuries: Schwartz 9th and 10th ed. are the same except: (a) added penetrating left-sided thoracoabdominal injury — if stable, DPL vs. laparoscopy; (b) in 10th ed., SW/stab wound gets serial exam/labs only (DPL and CT dropped); evisceration or peritonitis → operate.

1. Hemodynamically unstable → operating room.
2. Hemodynamically stable, GSW:
   - Anterior abdomen → operating room.

3. Hemodynamically stable, SW:
   - Back/flank → CT scan → + → operating room.

## Diagnostic modalities
- Purpose: help diagnose abdominal injury and its severity, and reduce unnecessary operation.
- Helpful tests (only what is needed is covered):
  1. Plain film
  2. FAST
  3. CT
  4. DPL & DPA
  5. Laparoscopy
  6. Local wound exploration

## Plain Abdomen
- In theory not needed routinely — low sensitivity; sending an unstable patient for a film gets you scolded by the staff.
- The author personally does it whenever possible — it does little harm; whoever wants to scold can go ahead (as printed).
- **What to expect from a plain film**
  1. Blunt trauma: may indicate organ injury and severity — free air or perinephric air suggests bowel perforation; high diaphragm may mean ruptured diaphragm; fractured spine or pelvis means it is not trivial.
  2. Penetrating injury: put markers at entry and exit wounds before the film; a retained bullet helps show the trajectory and injured organs.

## Focused Assessment Sonography in trauma (FAST)
- Adjunct to the primary survey; readily available, done in the ER, quick results; suits patients not stable enough for CT.
- US detects intraperitoneal fluid — screens shocked patients for an abdominal cause; if in doubt, FAST can be repeated.
- In pregnant women with blunt abdominal trauma, FAST is more suitable than plain film or CT.
- Contrast-enhanced US is now used, giving higher sensitivity and specificity, especially for solid organ injury (liver, spleen, pancreas).
- **Sites examined** (perihepatic, pericardial, perisplenic, pelvic):
  1. Pericardial sac
  2. Hepatorenal fossa (Morrison's pouch)
  3. Splenorenal fossa
  4. Pelvis (pouch of Douglas)
- **Weaknesses of FAST** (ข้อด้อย)
  1. Poor for hollow viscus
  2. Poor for retroperitoneal organs
  3. Poor for diaphragm
  4. With pelvic fracture, cannot tell whether blood is from the fracture or a ruptured liver
  5. Distended abdomen with much gas disturbs interpretation
  6. Previous abdominal surgery makes interpretation hard
  7. Free fluid < 50 cc is hard to see
  8. Worse for penetrating injury than for blunt trauma
  9. Operator dependent — needs > 30 scans to reach 90% accuracy
  10. Cannot tell the severity of solid organ injury

## CT Scan Abdomen
- Common contrast enhancement:
  1. Double — IV and peroral
  2. Triple — IV, oral and rectal enema
- Suits abdominal trauma with intraperitoneal and retroperitoneal injury.
- Patients sent for CT should be reasonably hemodynamically stable with an uncertain FAST, and must not be too restless in the scanner; in shocked patients beware contrast nephropathy.
- **Advantages of CT**
  1. Noninvasive study
  2. Sensitivity 92-97%, specificity 98%
  3. Grades severity of solid organ injury well; a decision tool for nonoperative management of liver and spleen
  4. Shows the bullet trajectory
  5. Quick — new CT scanners take 15 seconds, done before you can even hold your breath (as printed)
  6. Roughly estimates the amount of bleeding
  7. Suits penetrating injury to flank or back
- **Character of blood** (ลักษณะของเลือด)
  1. Fluid = 0 HU
  2. Fresh blood = 25 HU
  3. Clot or hematoma = 60 HU
- **Amount of blood**: the abdomen has 8 intraperitoneal spaces:
  1. Right and left subphrenic
  2. Right and left subhepatic
  3. Right and left paracolic
  4. Pelvic
  5. Intramesenteric
- Each space holds about 125 cc of blood:
  1. Small (1-2 spaces) — blood loss ≤ 250 cc
  2. Moderate (3-4 spaces) — about 500 cc
  3. Large (> 4 spaces) — > 500 cc
- **CT abdomen checklist**:
  1. Hemoperitoneum
  2. Solid organ injury
  3. Free air
  4. Urinary phase shows the bladder
- Note (หมายเหตุ): CT's weakness — the patient must be moved into the scanner, so should be reasonably hemodynamically stable. CT is also weak for diaphragmatic injury compared with diagnostic laparoscopy, and adds radiation.

## Diagnostic Peritoneal Lavage (DPL)
- Popular 1965-2000; since then CT and FAST work well and are noninvasive, and blood on DPL may not need surgery anyway. Though less used now, residents should still know it — old examiners may ask, and Trauma 7th ed. still keeps it in guidelines.
- Now most just do diagnostic peritoneal aspiration (DPA) without instilling fluid; if blood is aspirated → operate.
- **Indication for DPL**
  1. Equivocal or not available US or CT
  2. Suspected hollow viscus injury
  3. Multiple stab wounds — LWE of every wound is impractical
  4. Surgeon unfamiliar with or unreliable at reading FAST
- **Technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. Insert NG and Foley first.
  2. Closed DPL (needle puncture) or open DPL (vertical incision below the umbilicus); with pelvic fracture → supraumbilical DPL.
- **Positive DPA & DPL**: aspirate > 10 cc of fresh blood → operate. If < 10 cc, instill warm NSS or RLS about 1,000 cc in adults (children about 10 cc/kg), leave 10 min, then lower the bottle below the bed to drain and send the fluid for analysis.
- **Positive DPL when**
  1. RBC > 100,000 cell/mm3 in blunt trauma
  2. RBC > 10,000 cell/mm3 in thoracoabdominal stab wound
  3. WBC > 500 cell/mm3
  4. Amylase > 19 IU/L
  5. Alkaline phosphatase > 2 IU/L
  6. Bilirubin > 0.01 mg/dl
  7. Green bile, food particles
  8. Feces, or bacteria on gram stain
  9. Lavage fluid draining out of an ICD or Foley already in place
- **Absolute contraindication for DPL**: an existing indication for laparotomy.
- **Relative contraindications for DPL**
  1. Previous abdominal operation
  2. Morbid obesity
  3. Advanced cirrhosis
  4. Coagulopathy
- **Weaknesses of DPL**
  1. Retroperitoneal injury
  2. Contained hematoma
  3. Positive result may be old blood from a liver or spleen tear that has already stopped bleeding
  4. Invasive study
  5. Shows bleeding but not which organ
  6. Disturbs serial physical examination
  7. Air and fluid left in the abdomen make follow-up films hard to interpret

| | DPL | FAST | CT |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time | Rapid | Rapid | Delayed (depends on CT model) |
| Transport | No | No | Required |
| Sensitivity | High | High (operator dependent) | High |
| Specificity | Low | Intermediate | High |
| Eligibility | All patients | All patients | Hemodynamically stable ("atable" as printed) |

## Diagnostic Laparoscopy
- **Indications** (ข้อบ่งชี้)
  1. Penetrating injury to the left thoracoabdominal area
  2. Tangential GSW or stab wound of the abdomen whose depth passes the posterior sheath, but uncertain whether there is intra-abdominal injury
  3. Shotgun or blast injury from a long distance
- Patients for diagnostic laparoscopy must have stable vital signs.
- **Disadvantages** (ข้อเสีย)
  1. Needs general anaesthesia
  2. Lots of equipment; needs a skilled operator
  3. May miss hollow viscus and retroperitoneal injury ("retroperiteneal" as printed)
  4. Unsuitable for head injury with increased ICP — insufflation raises ICP further

## Local Wound Exploration (LWE)
- For stab wounds of the anterior abdomen, where the muscle is not very thick.
- **LWE should not be used in**
  1. Very obese or uncooperative patients
  2. Oblique stab tracks
  3. Chest, thoracoabdominal, flank and back wounds
  4. GSW
  5. Soft tissue hemorrhage or distortion
- **Technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. Local anaesthetic, good light
  2. Extend the wound — but this is not an exploratory laparotomy
  3. Gradually spread with a clamp; cauterize bleeding points along the way
  4. Assistant retracts with Army-Navy retractors; spread down to the deepest point of the wound and see it clearly with your own eyes — not by poking with a finger and imagining
- **Interpretation** (การแปลผล)
  1. **Negative**: wound depth stops at the posterior fascia — not through the peritoneum → close the wound and discharge (to be safe, in practice the author observes 12-24 hr).
  2. **Positive**: wound passes through the posterior fascia — it has entered the peritoneal cavity.
- **Positive LWE — 2 practice options**
  1. **DPL**: RBC > 10,000 → explore lap; negative explore rate about 50%.
  2. **Serial physical examination**: abdomen becomes rigid or the patient worsens → explore lap.

## Investigation ที่ไม่ควรใช้แล้ว (investigations no longer to be used)
- If seen among exam choices, mark them as wrong:
  1. **Four quadrant tapping**: blood is aspirated only when there is a lot of intraperitoneal blood; since FAST, this should be abandoned.
  2. **Sinogram**: injecting dye or air into the stab wound to see whether contrast or air enters the abdomen — do not answer this; high chance of confusion/error.

## สรุป Management of Abdominal trauma (summary)
1. Reestablishing vital functions and optimizing oxygenation and tissue perfusion
2. Prompt recognition of sources of hemorrhage with efforts at hemorrhage control (such as pelvic stabilization)
3. Delineating the injury mechanism
4. Meticulous initial physical examination, repeated at regular intervals
5. Selecting special diagnostic maneuvers as needed, performed with minimal loss of time
6. Maintaining a high index of suspicion for occult vascular and retroperitoneal injuries
7. Early recognition for surgical intervention and prompt laparotomy
8. Preoperative antibiotic and 24 hr postop, covering both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria
9. Combined head/abdominal injury that certainly needs laparotomy — CT brain before or after laparotomy, per these guidelines:
   - 9.1 Hemodynamically stable with GCS > 12 with localizing signs → CT brain before laparotomy. GCS < 12 with localizing signs, likely to need craniotomy → CT brain first, then laparotomy, then craniotomy.
   - 9.2 Unstable patient with GCS < 9 and localizing signs → clearly cannot go into the CT scanner → laparotomy first; once abdominal bleeding is controlled, judge whether craniotomy can follow; if the patient can tolerate it, do the craniotomy, then CT brain post-op. If GCS > 9 and no localizing signs → laparotomy first, then post-op CT brain.

## Principles and Techniques of trauma Laparotomy
- **Preparation**: prep from neck to knees (the patient's, not ours — as printed).
- **Incision**: incision of choice is midline — quick entry, full view, allows everything (meaning any operation).
- The problem: patient in persistent shock, SBP < 60 and not rising, abdomen increasingly distended → indicates severe, rapid intra-abdominal bleeding. We often ask ourselves whether to do EDT and cross-clamp the descending aorta, or just open the abdomen and take the chance.
- **Opinions of 2 senior teachers**
  1. Moore (น้า Moore): EDT is rarely necessary, except a patient who arrests in front of him, or SBP < 60 persistently with no response to initial resuscitation — then he would hope for open cardiac massage and aortic cross-clamp to increase cerebral and coronary blood flow and reduce subdiaphragmatic hemorrhage.
  2. ครูเกียรติ กังวาลไกล: taught that if possible, controlling the abdominal aorta via the abdomen is better and faster than via the chest. The author follows him: stab wound penetrating the abdomen with persistent shock not responding to IV → explore laparotomy and compress the aorta in the abdomen.
- **Intraoperative management**: following Mattox's Trauma, 4 steps:
  1. Control of bleeding
  2. Identification of injury
  3. Control of contamination
  4. Decision

## Control of Bleeding
1. On opening, tamponade is lost and shock worsens — tell the anaesthetist to help push fluid; do not complain, otherwise saliva will spray everywhere (as printed).
2. Sweep the small bowel upward and to the right.
3. Scoop out blood clot with hands (residents are more skilled at this than staff, as printed) — suction also fine, hands better. Note (สังเกต): where clot is thickest is usually the bleeding point.
4. In blunt trauma do 4-quadrant packing, in order: RUQ, LUQ, left paracolic gutter and right paracolic gutter.
5. In gunshot or stab wounds, follow where the blood spurts and you will soon find the source.
6. If BP collapses badly, compress the supraceliac aorta; to clamp it, pull the stomach down and approach the aorta lying to the right of the esophagus (if an NG is in place, feel for the NG tube); gently dissect beside the right crus of the diaphragm ("diaphragus" as printed), pinch the aorta between index and middle fingers, then place the clamp.
7. At this point bleeding usually stops temporarily — enough for us to catch our breath.

## Identification of injury
1. Explore the inframesocolic compartment: lift the transverse colon, run the small bowel from the ligament of Treitz past the IC valve, check the cecum, down to the rectum; turn the bowel front and back and check the mesentery too.
2. Any bowel hole → clamp it with a Babcock clamp for now.
3. Also glance at the bladder, uterus and ovaries in the pelvis.
4. Explore the supramesocolic compartment: liver, spleen, stomach, duodenum, pancreas thoroughly; palpate both kidneys.
5. If retroperitoneal hematoma must be explored (e.g. penetrating injury), you must know how (the author can do it, but the patients rarely survive — as printed):
   - 5.1 Left-sided medial visceral rotation (Mattox)
   - 5.2 Right-sided visceral rotation (Kocher with Cattell-Braasch)

## Control of Contamination
1. Inspect bowel front and back, and the mesentery.
2. Holes found → close temporarily with an atraumatic clamp; what to do with them is decided later.

## Decision
1. Definitive treatment starts only after the whole abdomen has been explored.
2. Assess whether the patient is still OK or needs damage control.
3. Assess the surgeon and assistants — are they up to it; is there enough blood?
4. If continuing with multiple intra-abdominal injuries, manage the order — which organ first, which last.
5. **Appropriate surgical prioritization**
   - 5.1 **First priority**: deal with what will kill the patient first = control bleeding. Liver and spleen → pack. Vascular bleeding → ligate if ligation is acceptable; if not, repair (e.g. aorta); if repair is not feasible (e.g. common iliac or external iliac artery) → temporary shunt.
   - 5.2 **Second priority**: clean organs first.
   - 5.3 **Last priority**: dirty organs last, especially colon — if you have already done a resection-anastomosis and the patient deteriorates so a colostomy would have been better, you must take down the anastomosis, wasting time and making a mess; and your instruments are soiled with feces — think about using them to suture the stomach or small bowel.

## Precaution of Missed Injury
- When exploring severe abdominal injury, the emergency, the patient's condition, the assistants' condition, and the surgeon's state when called up at night may lead to overlooking injuries in places that are hard to see.
- **Missed injuries to watch for**
  1. Diaphragm — while packing the liver or when the assistant retracts upward, look, then palpate both sides of the diaphragm by hand
  2. Stomach — GE junction, cardia, posterior wall, and perigastric hematoma near the greater omentum
  3. Duodenum — D2, D3, D4
  4. Small bowel — perimesenteric hematoma adjacent to the bowel wall, and proximal jejunum near the ligament of Treitz
  5. Colon — posterior wall of the transverse colon and splenic flexure
  6. Extraperitoneum rectum
  7. Ureter
  8. Extraperitoneal bladder
  9. Distal external iliac artery
