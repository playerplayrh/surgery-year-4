# Splenic Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 501-520 (PDF pages 2635-2654) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 26 ธันวาคม 2558)

## Introduction
- In the past every ruptured spleen was removed. Now, with understanding of anatomy and immunological function, plus good tools such as CT and embolization, nonoperative treatment succeeds very often, especially in blunt trauma; and if we must operate, we try to preserve the spleen rather than remove it.
- This chapter focuses on criteria for selecting cases for nonoperative treatment, and how to handle a ruptured spleen at operation.

## Anatomy
- Enough for an idea and to answer back to staff.
- 30% of spleens touch the distal part or tail of the pancreas → pancreatic injury may coexist.
- **Blood supply**
  - Dual blood supply from the splenic artery and short gastric arteries. Vessels entering the spleen are end arteries, each supplying its own segment; if a branch is cut, that segment goes pale. This pattern allows segmental resection.
  - Splenic artery is quite variable, but usually divides into 2 before entering the spleen: the upper branch supplies the upper 2/3, the lower branch the lower 1/3.
  - Figure caption: diagram of a transverse laceration relative to splenic vasculature in a pediatric patient (transverse tear runs between trabecular vessels).

## Mechanism of Splenic Injury
- **Blunt trauma** — most common cause of ruptured spleen; if criteria are met, nonoperative treatment is likely to succeed.
- **Penetrating injury** — less common, but most go to operation, usually with diaphragmatic injury; at laparotomy, preserve the spleen if possible.
- **Iatrogenic injury** — e.g. during gastrectomy or vagotomy.
- Concept of **delayed rupture**: blunt abdominal trauma without initial signs of splenic injury, then bleeding about 48 hours or more later. Now it is believed delayed rupture does not exist — it is rather delayed diagnosis.

## Signs of Splenic Injury
- Good for residents to quiz medical students with.
- **Kehr's sign** — blood irritating the diaphragm → referred pain to the left shoulder; found in 20%.
- **Balance sign** — LUQ pain whose location does not change when the patient turns.
- Isolated ruptured spleen usually hurts around the LUQ; generalized abdominal pain → suspect heavy bleeding or associated bowel rupture.

## Investigation
- **Plain film** — may show fracture of left ribs 8, 9, 10, or a clot shadow pushing the stomach to the right.
- **DPL** — rarely used.
- **Tc sulfur colloid scan**, and laparoscopy — do not work.
- **FAST** — for unstable patients; only tells whether fluid is pooled around the spleen; may miss subcapsular hematoma.
- **CT scan** — very suitable for hemodynamically stable patients. (Caption: a child with grade 4 splenic injury, one grade less severe than complete destruction.)
- **Advantages of CT**
  1. Gives grading and severity
  2. Shows contrast blush or not
  3. Helps rule out other associated injuries
  4. High accuracy; important for nonoperative management

## AAST Splenic Injury Scaling System

| Grade | Type | Description of injury |
|---|---|---|
| I | Hematoma | Subcapsular, < 10% surface area |
| I | Laceration | Capsular tear, < 1 cm parenchymal depth |
| II | Hematoma | Subcapsular, 10%-50% surface area; intraparenchymal, < 5 cm in diameter |
| II | Laceration | 1-3 cm parenchymal depth; does not involve a trabecular vessel |
| III | Hematoma | Subcapsular, > 50% surface area or expanding; ruptured subcapsular or parenchymal hematoma |
| III | Laceration | > 3 cm parenchymal depth or involved trabecular vessels |
| IV | Laceration | Laceration involving segmental or hilar vessels and producing major devascularization (> 25% of spleen) |
| V | Laceration | Completely shattered spleen |
| V | Vascular | Hilar vascular injury that devascularizes spleen |

(AAST Splenic Injury Scale, 1994 revision; advance one grade for multiple injuries up to grade III.)

## Nonoperative Management (NOM)
- Patient selection matters most: **vital signs stable** and no peritonitis requiring operation.
- In the past NOM only for grade 1, 2; now even grade 3, 4, 5 can have NOM if vital signs are stable — but grade 4, 5 fail more.
- Factors such as age, head injury, spinal cord injury or pre-existing splenic disease now seem less important.
- **Age** — formerly cut-off 55 years: > 55 should be operated rather than NOM. Now found untrue: a stable older patient can still have NOM, but beware that age > 55 with injury grade 3 or more fails more often.
- **Head injury** — NOM still possible; head injury is only a relative contraindication to NOM (fine in theory), but in practice the author finds it does not work: NOM patients should be awake, alert and cooperative with examination. Severe TBI (score < 9) → better to take the spleen out.
- **Diseased spleen or existing pathology** — NOM possible if vital signs are stable, but most favour splenectomy.
- CT showing contrast blush, or ongoing bleeding needing transfusion → NOM still possible with selective arterial embolization, which stops bleeding in up to 80%.
- Associated liver or kidney injury with splenic injury → NOM still possible as long as hemodynamics are stable.

## Criteria for Nonoperative Management (NOM)
1. Hemodynamically stable; SBP < 90 with poor response to IV resuscitation → OR instead.
2. No other indication for exploration, e.g. peritonitis.
3. No coagulopathy, no hepatic failure.
4. Doctors ready — surgeon, radiologist, anesthetist.
5. Hospital ready — ICU, OR, blood bank, x-ray standing by 24 hours.

## Angiographic Embolization
- Adjunctive treatment to NOM; raises success rate to 96%.
- Patient must be reasonably stable.
- Studies show immune function remains as before after embolization.
- **Indication**
  1. Grade 4, 5
  2. Contrast blush or active extravasation
  3. Continued bleeding, Hct dropping steadily
  4. Pseudoaneurysm
  5. AV fistula
- **Success of NOM**
  1. Grade 1, 2: success 90%
  2. Grade 5: only 25%
  3. Children 95%, adults 60-80% — children succeed more because their vessels clot spontaneously better than adults' so bleeding stops more easily, and their thick splenic capsule tears less.
  4. Intra-abdominal blood estimated on CT:
     - 4.1 Small bleed: < 250 cc, blood only around the spleen → NOM success 87-90%
     - 4.2 Moderate bleed: 250-500 cc, blood at the spleen and around the left colon → NOM success 60%
     - 4.3 Large bleed: > 500 cc, blood at the spleen, left colon and pooled in the pelvis → NOM success 10%; so blood pooled in the pelvis → the author thinks operate.
- **Cases likely to fail NOM**
  1. Age > 55 years
  2. Injury grade 3 or more
  3. Multiple injury
  4. Initial fluid resuscitation > 2 litres
  5. Blood transfusion > 2 unit
- NOM failure usually occurs within 24 hours, from continuing bleeding.
- **Key factors causing NOM failure**
  1. Inappropriate initial decision to proceed NOM
  2. Misinterpretation of imaging study
- **Indication for operation in NOM patient**
  1. Abdominal signs worsen, peritonitis
  2. Hemodynamics become unstable though initially stable
  3. Increasing transfusion need — Hct still dropping after 4 units
  4. No embolization available in the hospital
  5. Complication, e.g. abscess
- **Follow up CT**
  - Still debated; some do, some don't; most believe there is no routine CT follow-up.
  - If done, choose patients with injury grade 4, 5 or contrast blush in the splenic parenchyma: repeat CT 1-2 weeks after the first; may find pseudoaneurysm, which can be treated with embolization.
- **Activity**
  - Based on adequate healing time: injury grade 1, 2 about 3 weeks; grade 3 and above about 6 weeks.
  - Activity post NOM:
    1. Normal activity after 2 months
    2. Non-contact sports after 3 months
    3. Contact sports, e.g. football, after 6 months

## Nonoperative Management Order
1. Admit ICU 24-72 hours.
2. NPO, NG, Foley — some omit NG (the author places one).
3. Absolute bed rest the first 2-3 days; if stable, early mobilization to reduce thromboembolic complication.
4. Frequent serial PE by the same doctor (exam answer: theory of picture-building, ทฤษฎีการสร้างภาพ — as printed).
5. Serial Hct every 6 hours for the first 24 hours, then every 8 hours for the next 24 hours; if stable, space out.
6. After 72 hours, if stable, transfer to the ward; if no ileus, allow eating.
7. Keep in hospital about 7 days, since NOM patients needing operation almost all declare themselves in the first 7 days; grade 4, 5 or those living far away should stay longer.
8. Vaccination — give it anyway, even if this patient does not lose the spleen; how to give it is below.
9. DVT prophylaxis in every case with sequential compression device; do not give heparin.

## Operative Management for Splenic Injury
- Removing the spleen is easy; the skill is preserving it.
- **Technique**
  1. Start with midline — comfortable for the hair (as printed); in very fat, potbellied patients the author has had to add a transverse cut making a T shape, which is miserable.
  2. On entry you will likely find clot around the LUQ: pack with swabs first, look elsewhere and deal with life-threatening injuries first, then come back to the spleen and see if it has stopped bleeding. Grade 1, 2 can stop; if it has stopped, a smart resident does not meddle or dig (อย่าไปซน อย่าไปคุ้ย) or there will be trouble.
  3. If not stopped and oozing continuously → mobilize the spleen up: right hand holds the retractor, left hand gently scoops the spleen; the author usually starts by freeing the lateral side, then scoops it up — in no time the spleen and tail of pancreas are lying on the abdomen; place a swab pack in the splenic bed. (Caption: mobilization of the spleen begins with early division of its lateral attachments.)
  4. Examine the spleen carefully, like looking at the pretties at a motor show — touch it gently; heavy-handed scooping usually ends in splenectomy.
  5. Beware 2 points:
     - 5.1 Short gastric arteries lie close to the gastric wall — when clamping, avoid catching the greater curve; if unsure whether the fundus will become ischemic, cover with silk 3-0 seromuscular sutures or plicate.
     - 5.2 At the tail of the pancreas near the hilum — do not clamp blindly and catch the pancreas.
  6. Patient can still tolerate it, no damage control needed: inspect the spleen carefully and preserve it if possible, by several methods:
     - 6.1 **Electrocautery** — for shallow surface tears.
     - 6.2 **Hemostatic agent** — e.g. fibrin glue, or gelatin sponge laid over shallow wounds; enough (as printed, a joke "ว. 5 ชั้น 7 four seasons (เอาอยู่)").
     - 6.3 **Wrapping with absorbable mesh (dexon)** — sew the mesh around the spleen; for severe injuries; takes some time, so the patient should be stable during operation.
     - 6.4 **Suture repair** — chromic 3-0 horizontal mattress; the author bolsters with perinephric fat; tie gently or it cuts through.
     - 6.5 **Partial splenectomy** — only for injuries of the upper pole or lower pole.
  7. Subcapsular hematoma → do not dig, as the hematoma absorbs by itself.
  8. Clot plugging the wound with the spleen now in front of you → gently irrigate the clot away, then cauterize, glue or suture as convenient.
  9. Drain usually not needed, except with pancreatic injury → close suction drain.

## Splenic Autotransplantation
- Out of fear of OPSI, some recommend implanting splenic tissue after splenectomy, like implanting parathyroid.
- Slice the spleen into pieces width × length × thickness = 40 × 40 × 3 mm, about 5 pieces, implanted in the gastrocolic omentum.
- About 6 months after autotransplantation the implanted spleen can make tufsin, properdin and IgM — but it cannot filter and catch bacteria.
- Supporters say rising IgM should help somewhat and it beats doing nothing. The author would just discard the spleen: he believes preventing OPSI needs > 30% of the spleen remaining with a large vessel supplying it, so it can catch bacteria.

## Splenectomy
- **Indication**
  1. Hilar injury
  2. Splenic avulsion
  3. Massive or extensive injury
  4. Continuous bleeding after splenorrhaphy
  5. Multiple injury — must finish fast
- If splenectomy is done, do not forget to tell the patient about OPSI risk and give vaccine.

## Postsplenectomy
- D1-4: WBC and platelet may rise.
- D5: WBC > 15,000 and platelet : WBC < 20 → likely sepsis; watch closely for subphrenic collection.
- **Vaccination** — vaccines covering strep pneumonia, H-flu and meningococcus; if not given preop (usually not), give at PO. D14.
- Although overall OPSI risk is very low, vaccinate every case. CDC standard recommends about 10 vaccines, but most say the 3 key ones above are OK.
- Figure: Vaccination timing after splenectomy, as steps —
  1. Elective splenectomy → at least 2 weeks preoperatively: pneumococcal PPV 23 vaccine; children < 2 years pneumococcal 7 valent vaccine; Haemophilus influenza B; meningococcal C vaccine.
  2. Emergency splenectomy →
     - Immediate postoperative period: pneumococcal PPV 23 vaccine; children < 2 years pneumococcal 7 valent vaccine.
     - At least 2 weeks postoperatively: Haemophilus influenza B; meningococcal C vaccine.

- **Prophylactic antibiotic** — in children, most recommend penicillin or amoxycillin for children ≤ 5 years. In adults, the stand-by antibiotic approach: carry penicillin or amoxicillin, and at fever or sore throat take it at once and go to hospital quickly.
- Educate splenectomized patients about infection risk. In the USA they carry a card saying the person has no spleen; at every hospital visit, for any reason, they should always say they have no spleen.
