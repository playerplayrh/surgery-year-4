# Small Intestine 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 987-1035 (PDF pages 991-1039)

- Intro: the small bowel is the longest organ of the GI tract and holds about 90% of the mucosal surface, yet its diseases are uncommon; nothing much is new, tumor included. Some small bowel topics are covered in other chapters: Crohn in IBD, small bowel bleeding in GI bleeding, and carcinoid, GIST and lymphoma are covered in detail elsewhere.

## Anatomy
- Average length about 6 meters, in 3 parts:
  1. Duodenum, about 30 cm, 4 parts:
     - 1.1 1st part or bulb: 90% of DU is here.
     - 1.2 2nd part or descending part: has the opening of the CBD joining the duct of Wirsung at the major papilla; the duct of Santorini opens at the minor papilla.
     - 1.3 3rd part or transverse part.
     - 1.4 4th part or ascending part.
  2. Jejunum, about 100 cm (proximal 2/5).
  3. Ileum, about 150 cm (distal 3/5).
- Jejunum and ileum can stretch to 2 times their length; jejunum may reach 215 cm while ileum reaches 315 cm.
- From the ligament of Treitz to the IC valve: proximal 2/5 is jejunum, distal 3/5 is ileum.
- Jejunum vs ileum (Figure: vascular arcade drawing, shown in the table):

| | Jejunum | Ileum |
|---|---|---|
| Diameter | larger | smaller |
| Wall | thicker | thinner |
| Mucosal fold | more | fewer |
| Fatty mesentery | thinner | thicker |
| Arterial arcade | 1-2 | 3-4 |
| Vasa recta | longer | shorter |
| Peyer's patch | fewer | more |

- Mucosal fold, plicae circulares and valvulae conniventes are three names for the same thing.
- Normal size (upper limit): small bowel : transverse colon : cecum = 3 : 6 : 9 cm.

## Histology
- The small bowel is the organ with the most hormone-producing cells (a topic for those with free time).
- Layers from outside: 1 serosa (with subserous layer), 2 muscularis externa (circular and longitudinal layers), 3 submucosa, 4 mucous membrane (muscularis mucosae, lamina propria, villi with central lymph capillary/lacteal, crypts of Lieberkühn). Figure: wall layers (skip further detail).
- Mucosa: columnar epithelium with many villi to increase surface for absorption, via absorptive cells; between villi are crypts of Lieberkühn that make new cells to replace the old; turnover rate every 2-5 days, except after radiation or chemotherapy, which make turnover faster.
  - Paneth cell: makes enzymes, IgA.
  - Goblet cell: makes mucus.
  - Enterochromaffin cell: makes serotonin.
- Submucosa: the strongest layer, so a stitch through this layer holds well; poor suturing here leaks.
- Muscle: remember OLIC: outer longitudinal, inner circular.

## Physiology
- Main function of the small bowel is nutrient and water absorption, while the large bowel has water absorption only.
- Physiology is written briefly, just enough to understand fistula or short bowel.
- Figure (absorption map): electrolytes and water absorbed all along; fat, protein, carbohydrate; minerals Ca, Mg, Fe; vitamins B, C, folate, A, D, E, K; trace elements Zn, Cu are absorbed in the proximal bowel; B12 and bile acids at the terminal ileum.
1. Water absorption: most in the jejunum.
   - Secretion from the mouth onward plus what we drink totals about 9,000 cc per day.
   - Small bowel absorbs 7,500 cc, leaving 1,500 cc entering the colon.
   - Colon absorbs 1,200 cc, leaving about 300 cc in the stool.
2. Protein and carbohydrate absorption: related to the pancreas; written in the pancreas chapter, not repeated.
3. Fat absorption: most in the jejunum; dietary fat is mostly triglyceride.
   - 3.1 Long chain fatty acid (TG): after lipolysis binds with bile salt to form micelles, then chylomicrons, and is absorbed via the lymphatics; so for chylous ascites do not give long chain fats.
   - 3.2 Short and medium chain fatty acid (TG): absorbed through the mucosa into the portal system directly without needing lipolysis, by lipase from stomach and pancreas, to form micelles first; no bile salt needed; so chylous ascites and short bowel syndrome can use medium chain fatty acid.
4. Vitamin absorption:
   - 4.1 Fat soluble: A, D, E, K.
   - 4.2 Water soluble: B12, B1, B2, vitamin C, folate; B12 is absorbed at the terminal ileum, folate mostly at the jejunum.
5. Mineral absorption: 5.1 calcium: mostly duodenum and proximal jejunum; 5.2 iron: mostly duodenum and proximal jejunum.
6. Peptide hormones: several, e.g. cholecystokinin, secretin, somatostatin, motilin.
- Site of absorption for the distal ileum: B12 with bile salt; B12 absorption needs a helper, intrinsic factor from the parietal cells of the stomach.
- Transit time from small bowel to colon in a normal person: 30-60 minutes.
- Intestinal air: 80% from swallowed air, of which 70% is nitrogen; the small bowel absorbs CO2 but cannot absorb nitrogen; so in SBO an NG tube is needed to suck out nitrogen and decompress the distended bowel.
- Principal fuel of the small bowel is glutamine, which helps maintain mucosal integrity and promotes proliferation; so when giving TPN do not forget glutamine to prevent mucosal atrophy.
- Immunology: 70% of the body's immune system (GALT) is here; the small bowel is the major source of IgA, which serves as epithelial defence; beware the trap: IgG is not made by the bowel.

## Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO)
### Etiology
1. Extrinsic: 1.1 post-op adhesion 60%; 1.2 tumor 20%; 1.3 hernia 10%; 1.4 miscellaneous, e.g. volvulus from Meckel, inflammatory bowel disease.
2. Intrinsic: 2.1 tumor, e.g. carcinoid, lymphoma, leiomyoma; 2.2 inflammation, e.g. TB, Crohn; 2.3 hematoma; 2.4 endometriosis.
3. Intraluminal: 3.1 intussusception; 3.2 gallstone ileus; 3.3 FB; 3.4 bezoars; 3.5 tumor.

### Terms
- Proximal or high obstruction: SBO whose obstruction point is in the upper part.
- Distal or low obstruction: SBO whose obstruction point is low, toward the terminal ileum.
- Do not confuse these with high grade and low grade obstruction, which radiologists use for severity of SBO on imaging; high grade means almost complete blockage with little passage.
- Simple obstruction: obstruction with blood supply still intact.
- Strangulated obstruction: obstruction where blood supply to the bowel at the obstructed part is lost; needs urgent surgery.
- Closed loop obstruction: obstruction points at 2 places, so the segment between proximal and distal is very distended with nowhere to go; strangulation and perforation follow; also urgent surgery.
- Partial obstruction: SBO where gas or liquid stool still passes the obstruction point; the doctor has time to observe.
- Complete obstruction: SBO where nothing passes the obstruction point at all.
- Early postoperative SBO: SBO occurring within 30 days after surgery; the issue is how long to treat conservatively; some are given 7 days, some 14 days; one must agree with the team on the number of days.
- When obstruction occurs, intraluminal pressure rises so the small bowel loses its fluid absorption; if there is no resolution or treatment, the intestinal wall, especially the antimesenteric border, undergoes ischemic necrosis, then gangrene and perforation. From obstruction the wall thickens, bacterial overgrowth occurs, bacteria translocate into the circulation, finally causing sepsis; third space fluid goes into the bowel lumen and peritoneal cavity, with vomiting; the patient has volume depletion and electrolyte imbalance; air in SBO comes from swallowed nitrogen.

### History and physical examination
- History: colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, distension, obstipation, pointing toward SBO; previous abdominal surgery, whether days, months or years ago, can cause SBO; history of intra-abdominal cancer, abdominal radiation, previous SBO, gallstone, history of hernia — always ask about each of these.
- Physical examination: vital signs, hydration status, abdominal distension; in thin patients visible peristalsis may be seen; auscultation: high pitched and hyperactive bowel sounds; look at the groin for hernia.
- If fast heart rate, abdominal tenderness and rigidity from peritonitis, suggests strangulation needing urgent operation; do not forget PR in every patient; may palpate a mass or find blood on the glove, suggesting tumor; if the patient has an ileostomy or colostomy, put a finger in the stoma.

| | Proximal small bowel | Distal small bowel | Colon |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pain | intermittent, colicky, relieved by vomiting | intermittent | continuous |
| Vomiting | large volume, frequent, bilious | low volume, infrequent, progressively feculent | feculent when present |
| Tenderness | epigastric or periumbilical | diffuse | diffuse |
| Distention | absent or mild | moderate to marked | marked |
| Obstipation | may not be present | present | present |
| Onset of symptoms | sudden | gradual | gradual |

- Simple obstruction: send CBC, BUN, creatinine, electrolytes, LFTs.

### Imaging studies
- Plain film abdomen: the questions the film must answer: mechanical or functional obstruction, simple or closed loop, partial or complete, strangulation or not; send film in supine and upright positions.
- Findings consistent with SBO:
  1. Air-fluid level wider than 2.5 cm, and a difference in height (5 mm or more) of air-fluid levels in the same bowel loop.
  2. Multiple dilated small bowel loops with diameter over 3.5 cm, or a bowel loop wider than 1 vertebral body (spine) width.
  3. String of pears sign: the lumen is almost full of fluid, so little gas remains as air bubbles floating in a row, like a string of pearls. Figure: film showing a row of small gas bubbles (skip).
  4. Absence of air in the colon or rectum.
  5. Evidence of strangulation: thick wall, air in the bowel wall, portal venous gas, free intraperitoneal air.
- Plain film has sensitivity 70-80% but lower specificity; especially in proximal SBO it can be false negative.
- CT scan with IV contrast: recent guidelines advise CT in patients suspected of SBO (level 1) because it detects the obstruction, bowel ischemia, and tells the cause better than plain film; adding oral water soluble contrast raises sensitivity and specificity further.
  - In general CT has sensitivity 80-90% and specificity 70-90%; with oral contrast sensitivity 92% and specificity 93%.
  - Limitation: patients with low grade or partial SBO.
  - CT suits these patients: 1. recent post-op then SBO; 2. history of cancer before: CT can tell whether SBO is from tumor recurrence or adhesion; 3. no prior abdominal surgery: may be tumor or intussusception.
  - Figures (captions): SBO from peritoneal carcinomatosis of colon cancer; CT midabdomen with dilated fluid-filled small bowel loops and decompressed ascending and descending colon, typical of SBO.
  - CT finding of SBO: proximal bowel to the obstruction point dilated, with collapsed bowel below the transition zone, and the colon contains little air; it also tells how far along the obstruction is; when looking at the film, use signs 4 and 5 above.
  - Signs of impending ischemia: 1. beaklike narrowing; 2. mesenteric edema; 3. vascular engorgement; 4. intestinal wall thickening more than 2 mm; 5. no bowel wall enhancement.
  - Signs of strangulation: gas in the bowel wall or gas in the portal vein.
  - Oral contrast study: uses gastrografin, a water soluble hyperosmolar contrast that draws water into the lumen, diluting intraluminal content and stimulating peristalsis to move content down the distal small bowel; the patient swallows or is fed about 100 cc of gastrografin via NG tube, then films of the abdomen are taken at intervals up to 24 hr; by about this time contrast should be seen in the cecum; in some cases the study itself may resolve the obstruction; this method suits patients with multiple strictures, e.g. Crohn disease.
  - Enteroclysis: start by feeding about 200 cc barium followed by 1-2 L methylcellulose through a long nasoenteric tube whose tip lies in the proximal jejunum, then films at intervals; sensitivity and specificity over 80%; not suitable for the acute setting; for SBO, CT works better.
- Strangulation: the problem is that there is no clinical parameter or lab that diagnoses it precisely; careful history and physical examination are believed to be the most important.
- Features suggesting the bowel may be necrotic (need early surgical treatment):
  1. Clinical:
     - 1.1 Pain severe enough to be suspicious though the abdominal signs are not much (abdominal pain out of proportion to physical findings), or severe, constant, progressive, non-cramping pain.
     - 1.2 Systemic toxicity: high fever, tachycardia, abdominal tenderness with rigidity, WBC over 20,000, metabolic acidosis.
     - 1.3 Needs large, continuing IV fluid resuscitation to maintain blood pressure.
  2. CT scan: by the time a positive finding is seen the bowel is usually already gangrenous (late):
     - 2.1 decreased mural enhancement; 2.2 mesenteric congestion and thrombosis; 2.3 bowel wall thickening or gas in the bowel wall; 2.4 unexplained ascites.
  3. Superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) magnetometer: a non-invasive study for mesenteric ischemia; when the bowel is necrotic it shows abnormal electrical rhythm of the small bowel; still in the research stage.
- Criteria of intraoperative bowel viability: 1. normal color; 2. peristalsis present; 3. marginal arterial pulsation, or use a doppler probe to help; 4. IV fluorescein dye under UV light to see arterial perfusion, in theory the most reliable technique.

### Management of SBO
- If strangulation is not suspected, initial treatment is conservative; for post-op adhesion SBO it usually improves within 24-48 hours.
  1. Monitor airway, breathing and circulation.
  2. NPO, NG tube decompression (an RCT found NG gives results no different from a long nasoenteric tube).
  3. Foley to monitor urine output.
  4. Fluid and electrolyte correction.
  5. If NPO is expected more than 7 days, give nutritional support.
  6. Antibiotics are still debated; those in favor give them to prevent sepsis from bacterial translocation, but most believe they should be given when signs of peritonitis are seen.
- Conditions to treat conservatively as far as possible:
  1. Partial SBO.
  2. Early postop SBO (within 30 days of surgery; found in 5-10% after major abdominal operation, usually from adhesion): conservative 10-14 days; adhesions in a fresh operative field usually lyse by themselves.
  3. SBO from Crohn, carcinomatosis, post RT.
  4. Previous multiple operations from adhesion.
  5. Hostile abdomen.
- In conservative cases, watch symptoms and abdominal signs and follow plain abdomen films periodically; a first-time SBO has a 70-80% chance to resolve.
- If improving (less pain, abdomen softer, passing flatus, less NG output, x-ray shows collapsed bowel loops and air in the rectum), start oral intake with water, then soft diet, then ending with regular diet; if tolerated, discharge.
- The decision to continue non-operative treatment or operate rests mainly on clinical judgment.
- Indication for surgery:
  1. Peritonitis. 2. Strangulation. 3. Complete SBO.
  4. Failure of nonoperative management within 48 hr: operate; this item is still debated:
     - 4.1 Those who are a bit tolerant may be advised to operate immediately [as printed].
     - 4.2 Some advise feeding gastrografin 100 cc first; if no contrast is seen in the colon within 24 hr, it is a complete SBO, so operate (nobody will criticise); as far as seen this is in Schwartz 2019.
     - Figure: Schwartz 11e p.1232 algorithm for adhesive SBO, as steps: adhesive SBO -> signs/symptoms of strangulation and intestinal ischemia? Yes: operating room for exploration. No: NPO, IVF, NG tube, serial abdominal exam -> 100 mL water-soluble contrast through NG -> KUB after 8 hours -> contrast reached colon? Yes: high likelihood of nonoperative resolution, remove NG, start sips. No: repeat KUB after 24 hours -> contrast reached colon? Yes: same as above; No: strongly consider surgery within 72 hours of admission.
     - 4.3 The newer Schwartz guideline is similar to Greenfield 2017 p.791: in adhesive SBO, they send an acute abdomen film first; CT is done only if history, examination and abdomen film cannot say whether it is SBO or not; for simple SBO that looks not bad, they NPO, NGT decompress, and when the bowel has collapsed feed gastrografin 150 cc through the NGT, clamp the NGT, then send plain abdominal film at 8 and 24 hours:
       - 4.3.1 If contrast is seen in the colon within 24-36 hours, remove the NGT and let the patient eat.
       - 4.3.2 If not seen, wait the full 72 hours; if still not seen, operate.
- Surgical tips:
  1. Enter the abdomen at a virgin part; make the incision wide enough.
  2. Lysis of adhesion where it is loose first; an assistant pulls while the surgeon counteracts with a swab in the left hand and scissors in the right, sharp dissection, using the scissors as little as possible; if the patient is hard to cooperate [as printed] have the circulating nurse play soft calming music; if you finish this way the patient will heal well.
  3. Where adhesion is very dense, it may be necessary to cut part of the anterior abdominal wall that sticks to the bowel, or do extrafascial dissection.
  4. If a hole in the bowel is found, close it temporarily with a Babcock clamp first.
  5. After the long lysis of adhesion, repair every hole in the bowel; or if there are several holes close together, resect and anastomose.
  6. Use bypass selectively: otherwise one may have to resect so much bowel that short bowel or SBO from carcinomatosis results in not long; just bypass so the patient can eat.
- Laparoscopic adhesiolysis:
  - Recently there are reports advising lap for lysis because re-adhesion may be 4 times less than open; about every time we do open lysis there is a 15-30% chance of repeat SBO and 6% reoperation within 5 years, hence the push for lap; current data show lap has fewer overall complications and faster discharge; limitation: distended bowel makes lap lysis hard; conversion rate 17-43%.
  - Most experts say use lap in: 1. mild abdominal distention allowing adequate visualization; 2. proximal obstruction; 3. partial obstruction; 4. single band obstruction.
  - Most experts say do not use lap in: 1. peritonitis or free air, which may need bowel resection, closure, and washing out contamination; 2. very distended abdomen, NG tube decompression still not enough; 3. small bowel diameter over 4 cm (bowel is thin and tears easily); 4. advanced, complete or distal SBO (usually multiple matted adhesions); 5. carcinomatosis; 6. unstable patient with many diseases who needs to get in and out fast.

### Adhesion Formation
- On opening the abdomen there is an inflammatory response; handling and stretching and injury stimulate blood clotting; fibrinogen and thrombin in the fluid become fibrin sticking to the serosal surface of bowel and peritoneum.
  - If fibrin is dissolved, no problem; if fibrin remains, fibroblasts come in and mix with collagen forming fibrous adhesion, making bowel stick to itself or neighboring tissue.
- Factors promoting adhesion: 1. local tissue hypoxia; 2. mechanical injury under the peritoneal surface (the reason for barrier membranes); 3. intraabdominal infection.
- Every operation carries a 5% chance of SBO from adhesion and 3-8% get reoperated for adhesion problems; by operation, colectomy has adhesion problems in 9%; the worst is gynecologic surgery, especially open adnexal operations, with postoperative adhesion in 23% (the first rank), hysterectomy 15% being the most numerous.
- Adhesion prevention:
  1. Some tried NSAIDs, steroid, some chemo drugs, histamine, dextran, fibrinolytic compound, lubricant, hoping to reduce inflammatory response and increase fibrinolysis; results seem not to work well and may even raise the incidence of intraabdominal infection.
  2. Barrier membrane: a sheet placed between the peritoneum covering the viscera, to separate injured surfaces for at least 7 days, reducing adhesion between peritoneum and small bowel; an ideal barrier should be absorbed but not dissolve while blood or tissue fluid is present, cause no inflammatory response, be easy to use and not disturb wound healing.
     - 2.1 Early era: Gortex; the problem is it does not dissolve and the sheet slides, so time must be spent suturing it in place.
     - 2.2 Bioresorbable barrier membrane: the French use two:
       - 2.2.1 Interceed: oxygenated regenerated cellulose (Ethicon).
       - 2.2.2 Seprafilm: sodium hyaluronate based carboxymethylcellulose; evaluated by laparoscopy, it reduces vascularity, density and severity of adhesion; importantly the body absorbs it within 7 days, so it is not a persistent foreign body. The FDA has approved Seprafilm as truly reducing adhesion, but use it with care, because Seprafilm seems associated with a higher risk of intraabdominal infection.
  3. Minimal tissue trauma and reduce conditions that cause intraabdominal infection.
  4. Careful hemostasis.
  5. Use laparoscopic surgery instead of open surgery; most experts accept it because smaller wound, less tissue trauma, faster return of bowel function, less contamination; although it is reported that at 5-year follow-up the SBO rate was not significantly reduced.
  6. Avoid leaving foreign bodies in the abdomen, especially starch from gloves.
  7. Suturing the bowel or long tube plication to prevent recurrent SBO (this has many opponents; it does not work).
  8. When closing the abdomen, try not to suture the peritoneum.
- Remember: the most effective means of preventing adhesion is that the surgeon has good surgical technique.

### Recurrent SBO
- Principle of treatment: conservative as much as possible, especially in cases where: 1. nonoperative management has always succeeded; 2. had many previous adhesiolysis operations.
  - In both cases, as long as no signs suggesting strangulation, bowel necrosis or perforation, the large majority advise conservative treatment; wait patiently; prolonged NPO requires TPN.
  - If waiting long and the patient still cannot eat, and the patient and family are losing patience, go to the OR: long midline incision entering at a virgin area, careful adhesiolysis from the ligament of Treitz to the IC valve; close the abdomen, otherwise SBO may recur soon.
- Some therefore devised ways to reduce SBO incidence by plication or fixing the bowel in orderly arrangement so it can have peristalsis but not kinking:
  1. Long intestinal tube plication: the most popular; a long intestinal tube is passed through a Stamm gastrostomy, gradually advanced down to the terminal ileum, through the IC valve into the ascending colon; the alternative: place the tube through an appendiceal/ileal stoma [as printed: through a hole in the bowel] and advance upward; leave the tube about 3 weeks, then pull out after the operation when the patient can eat. Figure: long tube stenting the small bowel in an orderly loop arrangement (skip).
  2. Bowel plication:
     - 2.1 Noble plication: suture the seromuscular layer of the bowel wall from the ligament of Treitz to the IC valve, arranging bowel loops in rows, each row length 6-8 inches.
     - 2.2 Child-Phillips plication: after the small bowel is arranged neatly, sutures are placed through the mesentery; both plication methods commonly give enterocutaneous fistula and dense adhesion; re-exploration would be very difficult; the author as a young staff tried Noble 1 case with no good result; for technique see Artz and Hardy, Surgical Complications 3rd ed 1975.

### Small Bowel Intussusception
- Usually seen more in children than adults.
- In children under 2 years, often no cause is found.
- Adult intussusception: over 90% have an underlying GI tumor.
  - Small bowel intussusception: about 70% benign, e.g. polyp or leiomyoma; of causes from cancer, 30-50%, lymphoma is the most common.
  - Colon intussusception: 65% malignant cause.
- Symptoms: SBO symptoms without previous abdominal surgery; some patients may have a history of a mass or cancer that is the leading point of the intussusception. Figure: operative photo and CT of intussusception (skip).
- CT with contrast is the investigation of choice: shows the classic target sign or donut sign from bowel within bowel; CT is clear, so no need to look for other aids such as GI follow through or scope; but when reading CT, note that the bowel wall thickens, small bowel loops are dilated and the intussusceptum is seen (US can also show a target sign, if the examiner's eyes are good).
- Treatment: because adult intussusception usually has GI pathology, whether colon or small bowel, do not try air reduction as in children; in adults go to the OR and do oncologic bowel resection, continuing straight on.
- Exception: intussusception in a patient after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass for obesity with no lead point, believed to be from the motility disorder of the Roux-en-Y reconstruction; open and do only manual reduction without resecting bowel.

### Small Bowel Volvulus
- Few have a lead point, e.g. adhesion, tumor, node, Meckel, diverticulum, or disease.
- Presents as acute SBO; plain abdomen looks like SBO; CT shows whirl sign in 24-64%.
- Treatment: operate: explore, detort; about 50% need resection of the necrotic bowel; if the bowel is not viable (necrotic), resect; some advise cutting the affected segment that is twisted and leave it, to prevent recurrence [as printed: if the bowel is not necrotic, some advise resecting the twisted part to prevent recurrence].

### Vascular Compression of Duodenum
- SMA syndrome; some call it Wilkie syndrome, some Cast syndrome. Figure: anatomy of the SMA crossing in front of the 3rd part of the duodenum with the aorta behind (skip).
- The 3rd and 4th parts of the duodenum are compressed by the SMA in front and the aorta and spine behind, whenever the aortomesenteric angle narrows, compressing the duodenum. Figure: side views, normal with mesenteric fat between SMA and aorta vs absent mesenteric fat with compressed duodenum.
- More common in women than men.
- Causes:
  1. Rapid weight loss, the most common; classic: severe burn patients and patients after bariatric surgery with fast weight loss, whose mesenteric fat melts, so the SMA squeezes the duodenum until obstructed.
  2. Hyperextension of the spine from a body cast or traction.
  3. Abnormal take-off of the right hepatic artery and SMA.
- Symptoms: classic is a severe burn patient with weight loss who suddenly has bilious vomiting and cannot eat, vomiting all the time.
- Diagnosis: swallow gastrografin plus CT-A.
- Management:
  1. Start conservative; reports of success: feeding to gain weight, hoping fat will increase the SMA angle; if conservative fails and the patient keeps vomiting, operate.
  2. Operation: 2.1 duodenojejunostomy is best (Figure: duodenojejunostomy); 2.2 gastrojejunostomy is second best; 2.3 in children, ligation and division of the ligament of Treitz is usually done.

### Adult Midgut Malrotation
- Usually midgut malrotation is an anomaly found in children, with small bowel twisting around the SMA axis causing obstruction; in children it presents with bilious vomiting and abdominal distension in the 1st month of life.
- Adults can have it but rarely, mean age about 36 years; since it is a rare, hard-to-find condition, it is written for residents to read lightly.
- Presentation, 2 forms:
  1. Acute: bowel obstruction from volvulus followed rapidly by strangulation, like in children.
  2. Chronic: seen less; chronic vague abdominal pain, small nausea and vomiting, hard to diagnose.
- Adult patients are usually around 20 years old with a history of recurrent abdominal pain and are suddenly well then have sudden onset abdominal pain with vomiting. Figure: Ladd procedure drawings (skip).
- Diagnosis: CT with contrast: small bowel on the right and cecum on the left, accuracy 75%; small bowel follow through has almost 100% accuracy; often the diagnosis comes only on opening the abdomen.
- Management:
  1. Hurry: NG tube, NPO, IV, prepare for operation; do not waste time on scope, angiogram or the like.
  2. Explore (laparotomy): divide the peritoneal band or Ladd band and rearrange the bowel anew; there are early reports of success with the laparoscopic Ladd procedure.

## Postoperative Ileus
- A problem that keeps the surgeon in a mode of anxiety after abdominal surgery, especially bowel anastomosis; what one wants to see on morning rounds: a patient with no fever, a flat soft abdomen, no vomiting, no NG output, loud bowel sounds; the more the patient can pass flatus, the happier the surgeon.
- As long as the abdomen is still distended, no flatus, NG output about 1,000 per day, the whole team is depressed and the abdomen distended.
- Definition in the past: not able to take PO by POD 5 is PO ileus.
- Currently PO ileus means: after 24 hours post-op still nausea/vomiting more than 2 times, cannot eat by mouth, abdominal distension, no flatus, and plain film on post-op day 4 shows bowel still distended.
- Common etiology: 1. after abdominal laparotomy; 2. infection and sepsis, even if the cause is outside the abdomen, e.g. pneumonia; 3. electrolyte abnormalities: hypoK, hypoNa, hypoMg, hyperMg, hypoCa, hyperCa, hyperphosphatemia; 4. drugs: anticholinergic, opiates, phenothiazine, calcium channel blocker, tricyclic antidepressant; 5. uremia, diabetic coma, hypothyroidism; 6. ureteric colic; 7. mesenteric ischemia; 8. retroperitoneal hemorrhage; 9. spinal cord injury; 10. MI.
- Many causes; remember the exam trap: for electrolytes, hyperK or hyperNa is wrong (memorize).
- Plain film abdomen: distended loops of small bowel and large bowel with air throughout small bowel and colorectum, with air-fluid levels at the same level.

| | Ileus | SBO |
|---|---|---|
| Etiology | recent (hours) PO patient | prior surgery weeks to years |
| Pain | not prominent | colicky |
| Distension | generalized | mild to moderate |
| Bowel sounds | absent | active |
| Small bowel dilatation | present | present |
| Large bowel dilatation | present | absent |

- Prevention:
  1. Less tissue trauma: a gentle operator, not heavy-handed; choose lap where possible; bowel returns to work faster.
  2. Avoid excessive fluid during and after the operation; in the past water was given until shock, and the bowel became edematous causing ileus; so order post-op fluid as just enough.
  3. Correct fluid and electrolyte imbalance.
  4. Treat the cause.
  5. Analgesics: avoid, if possible, the morphine group; may add epidural anesthesia.
     - 5.1 Schwartz 8th advised the NSAID ketorolac for pain instead of morphine.
     - 5.2 Schwartz 9th advised mu opioid antagonists (alvimopan), which the FDA has approved: give 12 mg morning and evening for up to 7 days; patients tolerate and pass stool faster; but Schwartz 10th gives a warning to wait for more safety studies because of risk of CVS events with neoplasm, and gave only to patients in IPD; recent reports show it reduces ileus only in open abdominal surgery and not much in laparoscopic surgery.
  6. Fast-track protocol of Cameron 9th ed p.122-123 in open laparotomy: 6.1 epidural analgesia; 6.2 early ambulation and early nutrition: if PO D1 without vomiting, start sips of water, then clear liquid diet, then step up diet gradually.
  7. Motility-promoting drugs, e.g. Plasil, erythro (used for gastric dysmotility) but the effect on postoperative small bowel ileus is not convincing and does not help much; support of the patient's morale helps more.
- Controversies, since evidence-based data found these do not much reduce post-op ileus: early ambulation and early postop feeding.
- NG tube: many reports; the benefit of NG is that it reduces vomiting and abdominal distension, so suit in severe ileus, peritonitis, and multiple enterotomies; but NG increases pulmonary complications such as pneumonia, so the patient stays in hospital longer; NG also does not make bowel function return faster, does not reduce anastomotic leakage, and does not reduce wound infection; conclusion: NG tube is not routine.
- Gum chewing (non-sugar): strong support because it is cheap and easy; reduces postoperative ileus in both open and laparoscopic surgery by stimulating the cephalic phase of digestion, i.e. vagal cholinergic stimulation and release of GI hormones, helping the patient pass flatus and stool faster; but supporting data are limited.
- Laxatives: similar; chewing gum has the supporting data, but evidence is limited.

## Enterocutaneous Fistula
- Fistula = communication between 2 epithelized surfaces.
- Classification of GI fistula:
  1. Anatomic: 1.1 external fistula, i.e. enterocutaneous fistula (written here for the small bowel; enterovaginal fistula is in the colon chapter); 1.2 internal fistula: communication between the GI tract and adjacent organs inside.
  2. Physiologic, by output per day: 2.1 low: less than 200 cc/day; 2.2 moderate: 200-500 cc/day; 2.3 high: more than 500 cc/day.
  3. Etiologic: 3.1 iatrogenic, 75-85%, commonly after surgery for cancer, IBD, lysis of adhesion in SBO; 3.2 spontaneous, 15-25%, e.g. post RT, IBD, diverticulitis, appendicitis, DU, severe acute pancreatitis, cancer.
- Prognosis: 1. 50% close spontaneously; 2. if surgery is needed, morbidity over 50% and mortality 10-15%; 3. causes of death: 3.1 most common is sepsis; 3.2 next, electrolyte imbalance and malnutrition.
- Enteroatmospheric fistula: GI fistula with a large abdominal defect; difficult to treat.
- Clinical features: after surgery about D5 the wound develops SSI; on the first day the discharge may look like pus; the next day the color of the discharge turns brown like enteric content; the patient has fever, abdominal pain, ileus; try having the patient swallow methylene blue or carbon and look for colored discharge; think of enterocutaneous fistula.
- GI fistulas likely to close:
  1. Anatomic location: treat well, likely to close by themselves: oropharynx, esophagus, duodenal stump, jejunum, pancreas, biliary.
  2. Well nourished. 3. Absence of sepsis. 4. Cause is appendicitis or diverticulitis. 5. Bowel and adjacent tissue look healthy, with no inflamed disease. 6. Tract longer than 2 cm. 7. Small leaking defect, under 1 cm. 8. Transferrin over 200 mg/dL. 9. Postoperative fistula closes easily (memorize).
- Average time to closure: 1. oropharynx, esophagus, duodenum: 15-25 days; 2. ileum: 40-60 days; 3. colon: 30-40 days.
- GI fistulas unlikely to close: remember with the author's mnemonic ("High Society FRIEND"):
  - High: high output over 500 cc/day
  - So: Short fistulous tract
  - F: Foreign body in fistula tract
  - R: Radiation enteritis
  - I: Infection or inflammation at the fistula
  - E: Epithelialization
  - N: Neoplasm at the fistula origin
  - D: Distal obstruction
- Additional: these fistulas are also hard to close by themselves: 1. anatomic location: gastric fistula, lateral fistula of duodenum, ileal fistula (memorize); 2. poor nutritional status; 3. total disruption of bowel; 4. transferrin under 200 mg/dL; 5. tract shorter than 1 cm; 6. defect diameter larger than 1 cm; 7. high output fistula.
- Transferrin: a short half-life protein of the same group as retinol binding protein and thyroxine binding prealbumin; when hepatic protein synthesis is adequate, transferrin is high; value over 200 shows the liver is usable; under 200 shows liver function is still not good, so the fistula is hard to close.
- Problems in the patient with ECF:
  1. Fluid and electrolyte imbalance from loss of enteric fluid, much or little; which electrolyte is lost depends on volume and composition of that fluid; e.g. small bowel fistula loses more volume than pancreas.
  2. Malnutrition, the patient is very thin: 2.1 hypermetabolism; 2.2 cannot eat or eats insufficiently; 2.3 protein lost in the leaking fluid and protein drawn from muscle for use, causing muscle wasting.
  3. Sepsis from leakage of fluid and food residue left in the abdomen, forming abscess.
  4. Skin excoriation: if fluid leaking onto the skin is from the small bowel it is painful, like a dressed wound; if it is pancreatic fluid there is less skin excoriation because the juice is still in inactive form [as printed].

### Management of Enterocutaneous Fistula
- Goal: closure of the fistula and re-establishment of bowel continuity.
- Steps recommended by Josef E. Fischer (in the past 5 steps):
  1. Recognition and stabilization: within the first 24-72 hours.
  2. Investigation: after 7-10 days.
  3. Decision: during week 1-6, when the treatment plan is made.
  4. Definitive treatment: wait about 6-12 weeks; if the fistula still does not close, operate, as the abdomen has "settled".
  5. Postoperative healing: 5-10 days after the fistula closes; much emotional support is needed; patients often fast from food and water a long time, so are depressed; if arms and legs are weak, PT with rehabilitation.
- Figure/table (ECF management phases, as printed from Fischer):

| Phase | Description | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Stabilization | rehydration; correction of anemia; electrolyte repletion; drainage of sepsis; control of fistula drainage; local skin care; start nutritional support | 24-48 h |
| Investigation | fistulogram to define anatomy; CT/US/MRI to localize collections and guide drainage | 7-10 d |
| Decision | assess likelihood of nonoperative closure; plan therapeutic course; decide optimal surgical timing | 7-10 d to 4-6 wk |
| Definitive therapy | plan operative approach; bowel resection with end-to-end anastomosis; secure abdominal closure (+/- flap); gastrostomy; jejunostomy | more than 4 months if spontaneous closure unlikely |
| Healing | continue nutrition support; transition to total oral/enteral feeds; physical and emotional rehabilitation | 5-10 d after closure |

- Currently Fischer's steps have been expanded into 9 steps; reading on, the old ones were already fine, and the new update does not change much.

#### Recognition and stabilization in first 24 hours
- Done within the first 24 hours: sometimes extra fluid is given to fix fluid and electrolyte imbalance; early loss of much fluid: restore blood volume with crystalloid as needed, give albumin, give FFP for clotting factors; if blood or platelets must be given, they should be fresh.

#### Recognition and drainage of obvious abscess 24-48 hours
- ECF often comes with an abscess beforehand, with protein catabolism possibly up to 500 gm/day, making the ECF harder to close; suspect in a patient with high fever or sepsis that something hides in the abdomen; send CT with IV, oral and rectal contrast; if a localized collection is found, drain the abscess; choose percutaneous before open, and drain the abscess quickly before placing a central line for TPN; except in diffuse peritonitis, which requires going in: control contamination, resect the dead bowel (often an ostomy must be made) and wash the abdomen clean; use a soft latex catheter for the drain.
- Skin care: needs suction and protection; seal with karaya seal, or use vacuum dressing (VAC device). Figure: photos of karaya seal and VAC dressing for fistula (skip).

#### Nutritional assessment and starting nutritional supplementation 24-48 hours
- Practical nutritional treatment:
  1. Give as early as possible; in the first 1-2 weeks mainly TPN first.
  2. If remaining bowel is longer than 4 ft (120 cm), it can take enteral feeding after the bowel has rested from NPO and absorption is effective; when fistula output decreases, if possible start supplementary enteral within 48-72 hours.
  3. How much: 3.1 80-120 gm protein/day; 3.2 20% fat [as printed: "205 fat"]; 3.3 2,200-3,600 calories (depending on fever, sepsis); 3.4 adequate trace metals, vitamins, essential fatty acids.
  4. Assess nutritional status: 4.1 how much nutritional intake can be taken; 4.2 mid-arm and mid-leg circumference and body weight (if possible); 4.3 CBC, lymphocyte count; 4.4 check blood sugar periodically, avoid significant hyperglycemia; 4.5 serum albumin; 4.6 serum transferrin if possible.
- Medication:
  1. Somatostatin: helps reduce secretion volume but does not speed up overall closure rate; some advise it in pancreatic fistula; watch for the effect within 48 hr; if no effect, stop the drug.
  2. PPI: suits gastric fistula.
  3. Infliximab: suits Crohn fistula.
  4. Antibiotic: in theory give if frank sepsis, but in practice it is given to almost every patient.
- Text gives importance to albumin level: should be more than 3.3 gm/dL, because albumin promotes healing and bowel function returns faster.
- NG tube: whether to insert or not, from reading, most experts and the author do not advise much, because NG does not reduce fistula output but causes reflux esophagitis.
- Investigation: if no sepsis, do CT with triple contrast after 7 days, when the patient is stable enough.
- Information we want to know: 1. is there really a fistula? if so, what caused it, location, which organ; 2. continuity of the bowel: partial or complete disruption; 3. end or lateral fistula; 4. character of tract, e.g. length in cm; 5. any collection or abscess; 6. any condition that prevents closure, e.g. distal obstruction.
- CT scan with GI contrast: the most useful test and the initial test; shows contrast leakage, SBO, or a collection; if CT is unclear or not sure, do fistulogram or small bowel series next.
- Fistulogram: done after the tract has matured, injecting water soluble contrast into the tract through a No. 5 feeding tube.

#### Spontaneous closure up to 60 days
- The period for planning treatment: can it close spontaneously or not; 60-75% should close within 60 days, by caring for the surrounding skin so it is not wet or soiled by pus; put a soft sump drain with gentle suction.

#### Operation
- Operate when:
  5.1 No sign that it can close within 60 days; if it doesn't close, consider surgery; for the author, the patient is given as long as 6 months, and if it does not close, operate.
  5.2 Consider early operation, without waiting 60 days, if the cause is: 5.2.1 malignancy; 5.2.2 RT; 5.2.3 IBD such as Crohn; 5.2.4 fistula in a transplant patient.
- Remember: rushing the patient to exploration without trying conservative treatment first is wrong.
- Do not forget before operating to assess nutritional status and improve the patient's condition as much as possible; the most important cause of death is sepsis.
- Make the incision in a clean area; at the wound edges place antibiotic-soaked blue towels or plastic drapes.

#### Lysis of adhesion
- Use scissors to dissect and free tissue around the fistula first; if bowel is dense, the part can be cut out. Figure: divided adhesion (skip).

#### The anastomosis
- Best: segmental resection then end-to-end 2 layers.
- But if in a septic condition, resect and bring out an ostomy first.
- For duodenal fistula: resection is not advised; do bypass instead, e.g. gastrojejunostomy, gastrostomy, jejunostomy, like pyloric exclusion in trauma.

#### 8. Postoperative care
- Let the patient ambulate but do not hurry feeding; feed enteral diet together with TPN first, maintaining calories and protein until bowel function returns to normal, then slowly stop TPN.

#### 9. Maintain nutrition and start rehabilitation
- After being bedridden long, protein loss means the patient needs physical therapy to continue.
- Novel techniques:
  1. Percutaneous gelfoam pledgets plugging the fistula: reported success in 3 patients with duodenal fistula.
  2. Endoscopic closure with clip pinching the fistula hole, the clip passed via colonoscopy: reported success in 2 patients with colocutaneous fistula.
  3. Plugging with fibrin glue.
- Overall, novel techniques have low mortality and morbidity, but most experts do not support them because they cannot correct the underlying problem.

### Surgical tips and tactics (ECF)
1. This kind of case is very hard, eating the surgeon's strength; the operator must look in the mirror first: can they do it, and before operating improve the patient's status, especially nutritional status.
2. Open explore, because it is easier than lap.
3. Should open a new incision, but if the abdominal wall is all scarred, use the old incision extended; find a way into the abdomen at a virgin place; open wide.
4. Be calm and slow, explore and assess the entire bowel for distal obstruction or any condition that keeps the fistula from closing.
5. When lysing adhesion, do it gently; do not add more holes.
6. Bowel to be anastomosed must have good blood supply; when anastomosing the stitches must not be tight and there must be no tension.
7. Avoid contamination.
8. If a drain is placed, use a closed suction drain, brought out through a different incision, and place it away from the anastomosis.

## Blind Loop Syndrome
- A group of symptoms: B12 deficiency, steatorrhea, diarrhea, weight loss, abdominal pain, resulting from food not passing through part of the small bowel (blind loop). Causes:
  1. From surgery that separates part of the small bowel from the normal food path, e.g. gastrectomy with Billroth II reconstruction, or small bowel bypass.
  2. From patient's disease, e.g. Crohn, scleroderma.
- A defunctionalized or blind loop of small bowel has stagnation of bowel content, so bacterial overgrowth.
- Results from bacterial overgrowth:
  1. Disturbs conjugated bile salt: when bile salt falls, fat cannot be absorbed, causing steatorrhea.
  2. B12 deficiency from: 2.1 consumption of B12 by bacteria; 2.2 toxins produced by bacteria that disturb B12 absorption.
- Summary of main problems of blind loop syndrome: B12 deficiency and malabsorption of bile acid, fat, water [continues; the rest of the sentence is on the next page].
- Residents need to know: B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia or pernicious anemia; one must work out what causes this anemia: lack of intrinsic factor or a blind loop. Figure: B12 absorption: B12 binds intrinsic factor from the parietal cells of the stomach, B12-IF complex binds the IF receptor on the terminal ileum epithelial cells, then goes with transcobalamin II into the mesenteric veins.
- Schilling test:
  - Stage 1: tests whether B12 deficiency is from malabsorption of B12: give cobalt-labeled B12 orally, then measure urine B12 level; if urine B12 is under 6%, the patient truly has B12 malabsorption.
  - Stage 2: tests whether B12 deficiency is from lack of intrinsic factor: give oral cobalt B12 as before, then give IV intrinsic factor; if urine B12 is still under 6%, the B12 deficiency is not from intrinsic factor deficiency.
  - Stage 3: tests whether B12 malabsorption is from bacterial overgrowth in a blind loop: give cobalt B12 then IV antibiotic (in the past tetracycline; now should be a quinolone or metronidazole); if it is truly bacterial overgrowth, after the bacteria are killed B12 is absorbed well and urine B12 rises.
- Normal urine B12: 7-15%.
- Treatment:
  1. If the blind loop can be corrected by surgery, do it; but often it cannot be corrected, e.g. small bowel bypass in Crohn or radiation enteritis, so one must accept the blind loop condition.
  2. Give B12 and antibiotic about 7 days; repeat Schilling test, should be normal.
  3. Some cases are stuck and surgery is done if: 3.1 antibiotics must be given often; 3.2 antibiotics cannot be stopped at all.
- For a blind loop with no blind loop syndrome, no treatment is needed.

## Terminal Ileum Resection
- Reduced bile salt absorption causes problems:
  1. Malabsorption of bile acid leads to gallstone.
  2. Malabsorption of fat leads to steatorrhea, fat diarrhea.
  3. Oxalate renal stone as a result of poor fat absorption: much fat remains in the bowel, so the bowel takes calcium to bind fat, leaving too little calcium to bind oxalate, so oxalate is free; when oxalate passes into the colon, the colonic mucosa absorbs it well, causing hyperoxaluria and then oxalate stones in the kidney.
- B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia; beware the exam trap: Fe deficiency does not relate to ileal resection, because iron absorption is at the duodenum, not the ileum.

## Short Bowel Syndrome
- A syndrome of too-short remaining small bowel causing malnutrition, weight loss, dehydration, diarrhea and electrolyte abnormalities. In the past emphasis was on remaining bowel length (remaining small bowel under 200 cm diagnoses short bowel syndrome), but now emphasis is on function and adaptation of the remaining bowel more than length; so there is agreement that whenever so much small bowel is removed (a remaining length less than 100 cm) and loss of function in digestion and absorption results, we call it short bowel syndrome.
- Etiology:
  1. Adult: about 75% from acute mesenteric vascular occlusion; the rest are Crohn, cancer, RT, trauma, recurrent SBO.
  2. Child: from intestinal atresia, volvulus, NEC.
- Survival for a non-malignant etiology: 5-year survival about 45%.
- Basics:
  1. If no more than 50% of small bowel is resected, the remaining bowel is still tolerated.
  2. If resection is over 50-80%, there is a chance of short bowel.
  3. If the IC valve and terminal ileum can be preserved, we can resect up to 70%.
- Consider not only residual bowel length; other factors:
  1. Whether the colon remains: if colon remains it helps a lot because colon absorbs water, electrolyte and medium chain triglyceride.
  2. IC valve acts like a brake, keeping food in the small bowel longer, giving more time to absorb, reducing diarrhea.
  3. Remaining small bowel: ileum is better than jejunum because ileum adapts better, and a remaining terminal ileum helps reduce steatorrhea.
  4. Whether the remaining small bowel is diseased: if the remaining part is bowel that had RT, or is Crohn, this is not good.
- If the jejunum is all removed, try to keep as much ileum as possible.
- After a lot of bowel is removed, risk increases for gallstone, renal stone, liver disease and gastric hypersecretion.
- Adaptation compensation:
  1. Within the first 24 hours: epithelial cellular hyperplasia.
  2. Then villi lengthen, increasing mucosal surface absorption about 200-300 times.
- Remember: adaptation is in the size of cells and the length of villi; the number of cells and number of villi do not increase (memorize).
- Lifelong TPN dependence (until death):
  1. Remaining small bowel under 100 cm with poor function of the colon.
  2. Remaining small bowel under 60 cm even if the colon still functions.
  3. Infant with remaining small bowel under 10 cm.
- Bowel remaining over 120 cm: can give enteral nutrition; do not forbid enteral by mistake.
- Bowel remaining 60-120 cm: in the first year give TPN first, then follow with enteral nutrition.
- In the first 1-2 years the remaining bowel keeps adapting all the time, not just 2 months (memorize).
- In the first 1-2 years there is hypergastrinemia and high acid; after 1 year the bowel adjusts and acid decreases by itself.
- Treatment:
  1. The heart of treatment, the single most important in management, is optimizing nutritional status.
  2. Early phase: correct fluid and electrolytes; give anti-acid PPI to reduce acid secretion; give an antidiarrheal such as loperamide; for electrolytes do not forget to check calcium and magnesium.
  3. If there is steatorrhea and the colon is still present, give cholestyramine.
  4. In acute phase give antibiotic such as flagyl to reduce bacterial overgrowth.
  5. Somatostatin should be used in cases where fluid loss is large or diarrhea does not respond to antidiarrheal drugs.
  6. Nutrition: in the early period give TPN first, then slowly add enteral feeding, which helps intestinal adaptation; if diarrhea is not much, keep adding enteral, give small amounts often; high protein, high carbohydrate; restrict fat, lactose and oxalate (cola, tea, vegetables like [unclear]).
  7. Fat diet to restrict: medium chain triglyceride can be given because medium chain does not need bile salt to form micelles first.
  8. Novel drug therapy: growth hormone, or more recently glucagon-like peptide (GLP-2), hoped to increase villus adaptation so villi lengthen; currently in phase 3 trial; glutamine seems to have a better response, since studies found it increases intestinal absorption efficiency.
- Figure/table (Table 1, management strategies for short bowel syndrome):
  1. Acute phase: a. treat postoperative complications; b. maintain full support via the parenteral route; c. initiate low-rate trophic enteral feeds; d. document amount and site of remaining bowel and underlying disease.
  2. Early adaptation (up to 1 year postsurgery): a. increase enteral nutrition to tolerance, supplement with glutamine; b. achieve permanent parenteral access if indicated; c. maximize antiperistaltic agents; d. octreotide for high output ostomy or fistula; e. dietary counseling; f. clinical trials of trophic growth factors.
  3. Long-term adaptation (more than 1 year postsurgery): a. recruit bypassed bowel; b. bowel-lengthening procedure (Bianchi or STEP); c. monitor for development of TPN-associated complications, and refer for transplant before recurrent sepsis, thrombosis, or end-stage liver disease.
- Surgery:
  1. Non-transplantation procedures, 2 groups:
     - 1.1 Slow intestinal transit, e.g. electrical pacing, intestinal valve or segmental reverse peristalsis. Figure: valve and reversed segment drawings (skip).
     - 1.2 Lengthening procedures to increase absorbing surface: LILT (longitudinal intestinal lengthening and tailoring) and STEP (serial transverse enteroplasty); not explained, since they do not give good results and are unlikely to be asked.
  2. Transplantation procedure: suits patients with life-threatening complications of short bowel, e.g. liver failure from cholestasis (the most common indication); next, patients with TPN line problems: cannot place a line from central vein thrombosis of at least 2 veins, or recurrent sepsis from line infection more than 2 times per year, or the patient is repeatedly admitted from short bowel problems, e.g. volume depletion or repeated episodes of pseudo obstruction.
- Normally, the length of remaining bowel alone is not an indication for intestinal transplantation, except that in an adult with remaining bowel under 20 cm there will be volume depletion and electrolyte imbalance that are hard to correct.

## Small Bowel Diverticula
### GI diverticulum
- From most common to least common: colon first, then Meckel, duodenum, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, jejunum, appendix, and least is the ileum; most are false diverticula (guess if asked).
- Remember: true diverticulum of the GI tract is found at 4 places: 1. Meckel diverticulum; 2. traction diverticulum of the esophagus; 3. duodenal diverticulum; 4. cecal diverticulum.
- Most diverticula are located at the mesenteric border, except Meckel, which is at the antimesenteric border. Duodenal and cecal diverticula can be both true and false.

### Small bowel diverticulum
- Most are incidental findings, with no symptoms; about 10-20% have symptoms from inflammation or bleeding.
- Principle of treatment: no symptoms, no treatment.

### Duodenal diverticulum
- Found in 15% of small bowel diverticula; most often at the medial wall at the 2nd part near the ampulla; most are asymptomatic except a few with biliary obstruction. Figure: duodenal parts I-IV with the number of diverticula seen at each site (more near the ampulla in part II).
- Treatment:
  1. 90% are found incidentally, no symptoms, do nothing; remember only about 10% have symptoms.
  2. If infection occurs (diverticulitis), give antibiotic.
  3. Bleeding or obstruction from a lateral duodenal diverticulum: advised diverticulectomy only.
  4. Medial duodenal diverticulum, where the diverticulum is buried in pancreatic tissue: diverticulectomy has a lot of morbidity, so treatment is gentler (soft):
     - 4.1 Endoscopic treatment is advised more than opening the abdomen.
     - 4.2 Bleeding: endoscopic clip or suture to stop it is enough.
     - 4.3 Perforation: if contained, wide drainage is advised rather than excising the diverticulum; but if free perforation and the diverticulum is stuck to the ampulla, cannulate the ampulla first to prevent bile duct injury, then Kocherize, do diverticulectomy, and close the duodenum in 2 layers.

### Jejunoileal diverticulum
- Found in 1-5%; most are multiple, which have more symptoms than single ones.
- 80% at the jejunum, 15% at the ileum, 5% jejunoileum; most have no symptoms, only about 10% need surgery, the most common indication being diverticulitis with or without perforation, found in 50% (figure: jejunal diverticulum with perforation); bleeding found in 5-33%.
- Diagnosis is often made at operation for peritonitis, then finding it.
- Treatment:
  1. No symptoms, no treatment (the main thing to remember, has been warned many times).
  2. If symptoms, whether inflammation, bleeding or perforation, segmental resection is advised over diverticulectomy.
  3. Remember: in perforation, the experts do not accept simple closure of the hole; they must resect and then anastomose.

### Meckel's diverticulum
- A congenital true diverticulum from failure of closure of the omphalomesenteric or vitelline duct, which normally obliterates from week 6 of pregnancy; if it does not close, Meckel results. Tumors in Meckel, e.g. carcinoid, are very rare.
- Rule of two:
  1. Found in 2% of the general population.
  2. 2 feet from the IC valve, on the antimesenteric border.
  3. 2 inches long.
  4. Two kinds of cells: 60% gastric mucosa, and 40% pancreatic tissue.
  5. Symptoms from Meckel in 2%.
  6. Symptoms usually occur in the first 2 years.
- Symptoms of Meckel: most have none; symptoms usually occur before age 2; over 18 years, symptoms from Meckel are few; lifetime complication is no more than 4%, which is less than the chance of getting appendicitis; so from the data the management trend is: if a normal Meckel is found while operating for some other cause, as an incidental finding, nothing should be done to it. Figure: Meckel photos (skip).
- Those with symptoms:
  1. Bleeding: painless lower GI bleeding, usually in children under 10 years (as printed here), from an ileal mucosal ulcer beside the Meckel mouth, which has acid producing heterotopic gastric tissue; the bleeding ulcer is not inside the Meckel itself.
  2. Obstruction: mostly in adults, from a Meckel band attached to the abdominal wall acting as a point around which bowel twists, causing volvulus, or the band strangulates bowel, or is the leading point of intussusception.
  3. Diverticulitis: symptoms like appendicitis; inflammation is found less often, in both children and adults.
  4. Incidence of malignancy in Meckel: 0.5-3%, and the most common is carcinoid.
- Most accurate diagnostic imaging: 99m-Tc pertechnetate scan to identify ectopic gastric mucosa; Meckel scan gives positive result about 80-85%; if Meckel scan is negative, follow with laparoscopy. Figure: Meckel scan with stomach, Meckel's diverticulum and bladder labeled (skip).
- Treatment:
  1. Diverticulitis: diverticulectomy plus band removal from the diverticulum up to the abdominal wall.
  2. Bleeding (of interest): segmental ileal resection including the Meckel and the adjacent ileal ulcer.
  3. Perforation: segmental resection.
- Indication for incidental diverticulectomy:
  - Resecting a normal Meckel has no clear conclusion; depends on whose reports one believes; most advise doing nothing; but Sabiston 2017 p.1286 advises taking Meckel out, believing the lifetime complication rate is up to 6.4%, not under 2% as believed; also there are reports of malignant transformation of Meckel itself.
  - Acceptable features, per Cameron 9th p.138:
    1. Young child (Cameron): in the past resect at age under 18 years; those over 18 have less chance of Meckel problems; Shackelford 2019 uses age under 40; Schwartz 2019 uses under 50 for resection; the author leans toward Cameron.
    2. Signs of prior diverticulitis.
    3. Presence of a mesodiverticular band attachment.
    4. Presence of palpable heterotopic tissue or nodule intraoperatively.
    5. Diverticulum length over 2 cm.
    6. Narrow base.
  - When operating on a symptomatic Meckel diverticulum, also resect the adjacent ileum (Cameron 2017 p.130).

## Tuberculous Enteritis
- Found often at the terminal ileum and IC valve.
- Primary TB: mostly in developing countries; organisms come from drinking milk contaminated with TB; now found less; small bowel symptoms are obstruction more than ulceration.
- Secondary TB: over 90% are this type, from swallowing contaminated sputum; symptoms are mostly pain, diarrhea, ulceration more than obstruction.
- Besides infection at the bowel directly through the oral route, TB can come by blood, e.g. in miliary TB, or from the fallopian tube in women.
- Swallowed organisms cause bowel inflammation and spread to mesenteric lymph nodes, causing caseation necrosis.
- Pathology, at the bowel 3 forms: 1. ulcerative form (fever, abdominal pain, diarrhea); 2. hypertrophic form (some give SBO, may form a mass called tuberculoma); 3. ulcerohypertrophic form. Figure: ulcerative type with belt-like ulcer (skip).
- If severe, the patient has a fever, weight loss, ascites from peritoneal involvement.
- Diagnosis: chest film shows upper lobe infiltration in about 50%; most diagnoses of intestinal TB rely on BE, CT, and colonoscopy with intubation into the terminal ileum for biopsy; also collect sputum for AFB; skin test positive 55%.
- Treatment:
  1. Main treatment: anti-TB drugs, kind, dose and duration as for pulmonary TB, plus recovery of health, fattening up the patient.
  2. Surgery indications: 2.1 obstruction; 2.2 perforation; 2.3 fistula; 2.4 bleeding; 2.5 can't separate from cancer, especially a patient with obstruction or a mass; the classic appearance at operation is the whole bowel studded with tiny millet-seed-like nodules (remember it as it appears). Figure: intraoperative photo (skip).
- If surgery is needed, do segmental resection including the mesenteric lymph nodes, then anastomose.
- Avoid bypass, because a blind loop problem will follow.

## Typhoid Enteritis
- Caused by Salmonella typhosa, which causes acute systemic infection; organisms enter the body by food contamination via the mouth, resist stomach acid and run past the stomach to the small bowel.
- At the small bowel there is hyperplasia and ulceration of Peyer's patches; the patient has fever, abdominal pain, maculopapular rash; CBC shows normal WBC count; diagnosis by hemoculture or stool culture; Widal test with high titers of O and H antigens is highly (quite) sensitive [as printed: "มีความผิดพลาดสูง", i.e. high error rate].
- Complications usually occur in the 3rd week:
  1. Bleeding from ulceration: small, multiple ulcers with friable mucosa; luckily most typhoid ulcer bleeding stops by conservative treatment.
  2. Perforation: less common; from a single ulcer, usually at the terminal ileum. Figure: ulcer drawings and photo (skip).
  - Treatment: open the abdomen, look; if the hole can be sutured, suture it, so treatment of choice is closure of the hole; but if tissue looks edematous and soft, or there are multiple holes, resect that part and anastomose.

## CMV Enterocolitis
- Common in HIV patients, with inflammation at the distal ileum and colon.
- Symptoms: fever, abdominal pain, diarrhea.
- CMV enteritis has a high chance of perforation, so it is the most common infection needing exploration in HIV patients.
- Diagnosis: positive titer of antibody to CMV.
- The treatment drug line is a joke aside, not a clinical statement; no drug is named in the book here.

## Acute Segmental Enteritis
- Has many names; some call it acute necrotizing enteritis or acute necrotizing jejunitis.
- Seen in children aged 6-10 years; seen often in Thailand around the northeast (Isan).
- The causative organism is not clearly known; some blame the toxin of Clostridium welchii.
- Inflammation of the small bowel in patches; the bowel segments between are normal.
- Symptoms: abdominal pain, vomiting, stool is dark bloody with a bad smell.
- Treatment: 1. NPO, IV, IV antibiotic covering gram negative and anaerobe. 2. If symptoms worsen, abdominal tenderness increases, open and resect the inflamed part and then anastomose directly.

## Radiation Enteritis
- The small bowel is a radiation-sensitive organ; when irradiated it becomes inflamed, dose dependent: the higher the dose, the more severe.
- There may be a latent period from irradiation of many years before symptoms appear; the usual injury site is the terminal ileum, because it is a low-lying position.
- Clinical, 2 phases:
  1. Acute: usually found 2-4 weeks; symptoms from mucosal injury cause abdominal pain, vomiting, nausea, but usually a transient injury; once RT stops, the symptoms of pain and vomiting get better; diagnosis from clinical symptoms, plain film showing plain ileus.
  2. Chronic: symptoms from progressive obliterative vasculitis, usually present within 2 years (6-12 months) after RT, since the bowel wall suffers from decreased blood supply. Figure: irradiated bowel photo (skip).
     - Symptoms: partial SBO; obstruction site is often the terminal ileum, from stricture with adhesion; fistula and hemorrhage can also be found but less; in the chronic group send CT to rule out tumor recurrence.
- Treatment: whether acute or chronic, try to be as conservative as possible; the surgeon must endure the pressure from the patient's complaints, including the radiation oncologist, who will consult to push; remember: if not necessary, do not touch, since operating on these cases has morbidity of 30-50%, especially leak, and mortality of operation about 5-15%.
- Indication for surgery: 1. high grade obstruction; 2. perforation; 3. abscess; 4. hemorrhage; 5. fistula.
- Surgical tactics:
  1. Improve the patient's nutrition before operating.
  2. Ask the urologist to help place ureteral stents.
  3. Lysis of adhesion as little as possible, since it leaks easily.
  4. If possible choose a good case: resect the affected bowel and anastomose straight away, taking as little bowel as possible.
  5. If that segment of bowel is fixed in the pelvis and there is extensive distal disease, do not struggle: do a bypass, but accept that the problem after bypass is blind loop syndrome.
  6. Whether resection or bypass, for the distal anastomosis it is advised to join to the transverse colon, which is usually disease-free bowel.
  7. Do not do stricturoplasty.
  8. The surgeon operating on these cases must have the art of communicating with the patient and relatives, explaining the good and bad outcomes that may occur; speak often; do not give excessive hope; if after surgery the patient still has abdominal pain, or stool comes out through the drain, they will understand and not get angry or sue.
- Prevention:
  1. During irradiation, make the patient lie in head-down Trendelenburg position and do not use a dose over 5,000 cGy.
  2. During operation, close the pelvic peritoneum at the level of the sacral mesentery to prevent small bowel from sliding down to pile up in the pelvis, or place absorbable mesh to sling the small bowel so it does not fall down.

## Small Bowel Tumors
- Although the small bowel is about 70% of the length of the GI tract and has 90% of the mucosal surface, small bowel tumors are found rarely, especially cancer, only 1-2%.
- Symptoms: rather insidious and nonspecific, especially if cancer; by the time symptoms are many, it is late; suspect small bowel tumor in cases of unexplained or obscure GI bleeding, or unexplained chronic abdominal pain.
- Investigation (written only as needed; SBO and obscure GI bleeding are covered in the GI bleeding chapter):
  1. Enteroclysis: the method is a bit complicated: put an NJ tube advanced past the ligament of Treitz, then push in barium; takes no more than 1 hour; picture clearer than small bowel follow through for seeing mucosal lesion.
  2. CT enteroclysis: even more advanced, since extramural lesions can also be seen; but GI follow through, enteroclysis and CT are all similar: sensitivity and specificity for intraluminal lesions are low.
  3. In this hi-tech era, wireless capsule enteroscopy is used in obscure GI bleeding and chronic abdominal pain, so small bowel tumors are found more; disadvantages: expensive, many pictures, cannot control the capsule's movement, the images are not very clear and telling the exact location is rather hard.
  4. Endoscopy: push enteroscopy sees proximal jejunum about 60 cm; if lucky and found, jackpot: biopsy right there; for longer reach use double balloon enteroscopy, which sees nearly all small bowel mucosa.

### Benign Small Bowel Tumors
- Found less, but still more than malignant small bowel tumors.
- Leiomyoma: found mostly at the jejunum, usually extraluminal mass; the benign tumor with the most symptoms compared with other benign small bowel tumors; the most common benign tumor in some series.
- Adenoma: also the most common benign tumor in some series; seen often in the past; now since GIST is found more, we must look for a new answer. Figure: adenoma at the duodenum (skip).
- Benign duodenal tumor: written because the treatment is a common question: found incidentally at EGD, take a biopsy.
  - Indication for surgery: 1. all adenomatous polyps, because of malignant potential; 2. symptomatic cases.
  - Treatment depends on size:
    1. Under 1 cm: endoscopic polypectomy.
    2. Over 2 cm: hard to resect endoscopically; should open; several ways: 2.1 transduodenal polypectomy; 2.2 segmental duodenal resection; 2.3 if at the ampulla, ampullectomy; 2.4 if a polyp in an FAP patient with severe dysplasia, finish with standard PD.
    3. 1-2 cm: EUS first: 3.1 if the lesion is limited to the mucosa, endoscopic polypectomy; 3.2 if deeper than the mucosa, treat as for size over 2 cm.
- Hemangioma: found as both capillary and cavernous types; seen often at the jejunum and ileum; if inherited, it is called Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome; the prominent symptom is bleeding; look at the lips, skin, fingers and hands for hemangioma.
- Hamartoma: classic is the polyp in Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.
- Peutz-Jeghers syndrome: autosomal dominant; classic: a patient aged 20-30 with mucocutaneous melanotic skin pigmentation as dark spots on lips, palms, soles. Figure: lips and polyps (skip).
  - GI polyps are found mostly at the jejunum and ileum and are hamartoma with low malignancy potential; from polyps in the bowel, patients may present with small bowel obstruction from intussusception and obscure GI bleeding.
  - Treatment: try to take out as many polyps as possible before problems occur, by sending the patient for EGD, enteroscopy and colonoscopy, and polypectomy to remove all if possible.
  - For small bowel polyps that are quite numerous and extensive, if symptoms occur, limited segmental resection is advised, only the segment of bowel that produces the problem.

### Malignant Small Bowel Tumors
- Overall rare and usually secondary more than primary cancer.
- Adenocarcinoma: the most common malignant tumor of the small bowel; found mostly at the duodenum, especially the 2nd part. Figure: endoscopic photo (skip).
  - Risk factors are many, like CRC; oddly, in IBD, Crohn has a 2% risk of developing cancer, while ulcerative colitis has no relation with malignant small bowel tumor (think about it, it is right, since UC involves only colon and rectum).
  - Diagnosis is late because symptoms are not clear, so prognosis is rather poor.
  - Treatment:
    1. Duodenum, 1st part, 2nd part or proximal 3rd part: PD or PPPD; or resection plus regional lymphadenectomy for the 3rd part, for which free margin can be obtained, and the 4th part.
    2. Jejunum and ileum: segmental resection to get a margin of about 10 cm with lymphadenectomy. Figure: wedge resection of mesentery with 10 cm margin on each side of tumor, then anastomosis.
    3. Distal ileum: do right hemicolectomy.
    4. Chemo or chemoRT: does not work well.
- Carcinoid, lymphoma, GIST, non-GIST: written separately as their own chapters.
- Kaposi sarcoma: appears as raised, sessile, reddish nodules, seen often in AIDS patients; in AIDS patients Kaposi is found more than lymphoma; if Kaposi is in the small bowel, there is usually Kaposi on the skin too. Figure: bowel with nodules (skip).
  - An AIDS patient who comes with GI bleed usually bleeds from peptic ulcer, but an AIDS patient with small bowel Kaposi who comes with GI bleed usually bleeds from Kaposi more than from PU.
  - Treatment: localized tumor is difficult; radiation of the bowel is not tolerated; if surgery is needed, segmental resection is usually necessary.
- Metastasis disease of the small bowel: found more than primary. Figure: photos (skip).
  - Rank 1 from colorectal cancer (Cameron 9th p.136); rank 2 from melanoma (in the past rank 1); rank 3 from pancreas, lung, ovaries.
  - Treatment: resection for palliative purpose only.
- Most common, concerning small bowel tumors:
  1. Benign: smooth muscle tumor (GIST).
  2. Symptomatic benign: leiomyoma, found mostly at the jejunum.
  3. Malignant primary: number one is adenocarcinoma.
  4. Secondary: CRC.
  5. Age under 10 years: lymphoma, found mostly at the ileum.
  6. Presentation: obstruction.
  7. Sarcoma: the answer for GIST in the past was leiomyosarcoma.

## Small Bowel Perforation
- Etiology:
  1. Currently most common: iatrogenic injury, during GI endoscopy.
  2. DU perforation in the USA is found as rank 2, probably because treating H. pylori is widespread; but the author's personal view is that in developing or developing-stage countries, including Thailand, DU perforation is probably still number 1 as before.
  3. Miscellaneous: 3.1 infection or inflammation from TB, typhoid, CMV, Crohn; 3.2 ischemia from RT; 3.3 tumor; 3.4 FB; 3.5 drugs, e.g. potassium or NSAIDs.

### Iatrogenic Duodenal Perforation
- Most commonly from ERCP with endoscopic sphincterotomy.
- If free perforation, symptoms are severe; retroperitoneal perforation has few symptoms.
- CT is the most sensitive test.
- Management:
  1. Nonoperative management suits: 1.1 iatrogenic retroperitoneal duodenal perforation; 1.2 absence of peritonitis or sepsis.
  2. Operative management is used in: 2.1 free perforation; 2.2 clinically deteriorating patients.

## Pneumatosis Intestinalis
- Means finding gas at the bowel wall; found in both small and large bowel, but seen most often at the jejunum.
- This is not a disease but a sign, found on CT or plain abdomen.
- Divided into primary and secondary.
- Primary or idiopathic: i.e. gas is in the bowel wall generally but mostly in the colon; 15% still nobody knows how it occurs; the group with no symptoms need not be treated; so the primary group must not be treated, every one (do not make this mistake).
- Secondary:
  1. Not serious group: occurs after endoscopy, jejunostomy, or in DM, COPD, asthma patients; no treatment needed.
  2. Serious group: found in patients with bowel ischemia and infection; air in the bowel wall in these cases indicates that the bowel is already necrotic.
     - Treatment: hurry, explore laparotomy quickly, with the family told that this must be done, since the trend is death [as printed: "แนวโน้มตายแน่" - likely death].
