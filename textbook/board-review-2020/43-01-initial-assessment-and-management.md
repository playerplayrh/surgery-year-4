# Initial Assessment and Management
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 8-58 (PDF pages 2142-2192) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 12 มกราคม 2559)

## Introduction
- Correct, good-quality assessment and resuscitation of severe trauma patients can cut deaths by up to 35%.
- Care follows ATLS; current at time of writing was the 9th edition (2012); the 10th edition was expected around October 2016. Changes from the 9th edition are added in this book; several recommendations are hard to apply in Thai practice (น้องๆต้องทำใจ).
- **Ideal trauma care has 3 phases**
  1. Prehospital phase: preparation and triage
  2. Hospital phase: from primary survey to definitive treatment
  3. Rehabilitation phase

## ATLS Initial Assessment and Management
- System approach of the ATLS guideline:
  1. Prehospital trauma care — mainly preparation & triage
  2. Primary survey (ABCDE) & resuscitation, done simultaneously
  3. Adjuncts to the primary survey
  4. Secondary survey (head-to-toe evaluation)
  5. Adjuncts to the secondary survey
  6. Continued postresuscitation monitoring and reevaluation
  7. Definitive care
- In practice the steps may not run in strict order; some are done together, or you may need to start over.
- With many departments/specialties helping, set up a clear system: no fighting over the patient, and more worrying, no pushing the patient away as "not my case"; as printed, the general surgery resident should step in first.

## Prehospital Trauma Life Support (PHTLS)
- The standardized system of pre-hospital care (preparation & triage), with formal training: give only the basic care needed, then rush the patient to a hospital able to treat severe injury.
- Outline of PHTLS care:
  1. Assess scene safety for rescuers.
  2. Assess whether the scene needs help from other units (e.g. more transport vehicles).
  3. Understand the mechanism of injury and assess life-threatening conditions; manage airway and C-spine; support ventilation with bag-mask device on oxygen, FiO2 > 0.85, keep SpO2 > 95%.
  4. Control significant external hemorrhage, external splinting & maintain manual spine stabilization, keep warm, then transfer quickly.

## Preparation
- Preparation and care starting at the scene before transport, usually by medical staff or foundation (rescue) staff with some training.
- Principle in trauma: **no delay trauma care** — get the patient from the scene to hospital as fast as possible; better than treating at the scene ("stay and play").
- Key factors for survival found in studies:
  1. Severity of injury
  2. Time from injury to definitive treatment
- **In practice, do only:**
  1. Airway management — position head and neck so the tongue does not block the upper airway
     - 1.1 Conscious, answers questions, voice clear, no hoarseness → no airway problem
     - 1.2 Unconscious → chin lift and jaw thrust; **no head tilt** (dangerous if there is C-spine injury)
     - 1.3 To keep the tongue off the upper airway → oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway
     - 1.4 ET tube is too much for the scene; laryngeal mask airway or esophagotracheal Combitube should be enough
  2. After an upper artificial airway (e.g. oropharyngeal airway) is in, assist with bag valve mask; ET tube depends on indication and the operator's ability.
  3. External bleeding: direct pressure with cloth, by hand or elastic bandage.
  4. IV from the scene does not reduce mortality; if travel time to hospital ≤ 30 min, IV is not needed. If IV is necessary, use LRS and keep BP about 80 mmHg.
  5. Arrest at the scene → may need ET tube with external cardiac massage; IV access and IV drugs are done at hospital.
  6. Refer: inform the receiving hospital in advance; position the patient to avoid airway obstruction.
     - 6.1 Drowsy patient after oropharyngeal airway: supine on a board or rigid stretcher, head held still with sandbags on both sides of the neck or a hard collar.
     - 6.2 Conscious patient with heavy bleeding in the mouth or parasymphysis mandible fracture: sit leaning forward or lie on the side.
     - 6.3 Pregnancy, especially third trimester: left lateral decubitus so the uterus does not compress the IVC.
- In-hospital preparation: set up area, equipment and team; decide who to treat here and who to refer to a hospital with a surgeon.
- When referring, tell the receiving hospital the patient's state and initial treatment using **MIST**:
  - M = mechanism of injury
  - I = injuries found or suspected
  - S = symptoms and signs
  - T = treatment already given

## Triage
- From French "Trier" = to sort.
- Trauma triage sorts mass-casualty patients into groups by severity; about 10-15% need referral to a trauma center.
- **Triage decision criteria suggesting severe injury**
  1. Physiologic criteria: consciousness, vital signs; unconscious or shock → likely severe
  2. Anatomic criteria: injury to brain, neck, chest → often severe
  3. Mechanism of injury: high-speed driving, falls from height > 6 m → often severe (as printed, includes "พวกแว้น พวกสะก๊อย")
  4. Patient criteria: elderly, children, many comorbidities → often severe
- **Simple triage and rapid transport (START)** — 4 groups:

| Group | Colour | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate | Red | Treat at once |
| Urgent | Yellow | Can wait, but not > 30 min |
| Delay | Green | Can wait (walking wounded); often the loudest, most demanding group (as printed) |
| Expectant | Black | Certain to die |

- In mass casualties, first priority = the patient with the greatest chance of survival (as printed, in Thailand one also has to check whose child the patient is).
- Referral from a small/level 1 hospital to a big hospital: CT before transfer is not needed — it delays transfer by at least 2 h, and receiving hospitals repeat CT in almost 60%.
- Injury in children is more severe than in adults because:
  1. Small body → injury spreads widely, multiple injury is common
  2. Very elastic ribs → blunt upper-abdominal trauma transmits severe force to liver, spleen, kidney
  3. Thin abdominal wall and fat layer → internal organ injury is often severe

## Field triage
Figure: Field triage algorithm, as steps —
1. **Step one** — measure vital signs and level of consciousness: GCS < 14, SBP < 90 mmHg, RR < 10 or > 29/min (< 20 in infant < 1 year).
   - Yes → take to a trauma center (steps 1 and 2 identify the most seriously injured; transport preferentially to the highest level of care in the trauma system).
   - No → step two.
2. **Step two** — assess anatomy of injury: all penetrating injuries to head, neck, torso and extremities proximal to elbow and knee; flail chest; two or more proximal long-bone fractures; crushed, degloved or mangled extremity; amputation proximal to wrist and ankle; pelvic fractures; open or depressed skull fracture; paralysis.
   - Yes → trauma center (as step one).
   - No → step three.
3. **Step three** — assess mechanism of injury / evidence of high-energy impact:
   - Falls: adults > 20 feet (one story = 10 feet); children > 10 feet or 2-3 times the child's height
   - High-risk auto crash: intrusion > 12 inches occupant site, > 18 inches any site; ejection (partial or complete) from automobile; death in same passenger compartment; vehicle telemetry data consistent with high risk of injury
   - Auto vs pedestrian/bicyclist thrown, run over, or with significant (> 20 mph) impact
   - Motorcycle crash > 20 mph
   - Yes → transport to the closest appropriate trauma center (need not be the highest level).
   - No → step four.
4. **Step four** — special patient or system considerations:
   - Age: older adults — risk of injury/death rises after age 55; children — triage preferentially to pediatric-capable trauma centers
   - Anticoagulation and bleeding disorders
   - Burns: without other trauma mechanism → burn facility; with trauma mechanism → trauma center
   - Time-sensitive extremity injury
   - End-stage renal disease requiring dialysis
   - Pregnancy > 20 weeks
   - EMS provider judgment
   - Yes → contact medical control and consider transport to a trauma center or specific resource hospital.
   - No → transport according to protocol.
- When in doubt, transport to a trauma center.

## Primary Survey
- Aim: identify immediately life-threatening conditions and treat them first. Work as a team with a team leader; speak one at a time, repeat back and confirm orders; assess, then quickly consult the relevant departments.
- Primary survey = **ABCDE**
  1. Airway maintenance with cervical spine protection
  2. Breathing and ventilation
  3. Circulation and bleeding control
  4. Disability or neurological evaluation
  5. Exposure and environmental (temperature) control

## Airway Management
- Start by assessing responsiveness — simply talk to the patient. Talking sensibly with a clear voice = normal response; conscious and no definitive airway needed yet.
- Hoarseness or stridor suggests > 70% airway obstruction; agitation, confusion, hypoxia → needs advanced/definitive airway (ET tube or surgical airway).
- Begin with basic airway maintenance: appropriate position, suction secretions and blood clot from the mouth.
- Chin lift, jaw thrust, oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway, while keeping the cervical spine still (cervical in-line immobilization).
- **Chin lift and jaw thrust: yes; head tilt: no.**
- **Oropharyngeal airway**: not for conscious patients — irritating, triggers gagging/vomiting.
- **Nasopharyngeal airway**: suits conscious patients (better tolerated than oral), and patients with seizures or trismus. Contraindication: fracture of base of skull or midface fracture.
- **Caution with bag valve mask ventilation** (ระวัง): conscious patients find it irritating; air into the stomach → distension and vomiting. Not usable in severe maxillofacial injury or cribriform plate fracture (bagging can induce pneumocephalus). Bagging too hard/fast → respiratory alkalosis → O2 held tightly by Hb and not released to tissue (as printed: the more you bag, the bluer they get).
- Then decide whether the patient needs a definitive airway.
- **Decision-making questions**
  1. Does the patient need to be intubated?
  2. How rapidly does the patient need to be intubated?
  3. Will the intubation be difficult?
  4. What is the chosen method to control the airway?
  5. What are my back-up plans?
- Figure: Airway algorithm, as steps —
  1. Patent airway? Inadequate breathing? GCS < 9? — No → adjunctive airway measures → continue resuscitation. Yes → step 2.
  2. Age < 8 years with laryngeal injury? — Yes → tracheostomy. No → assume cervical spine injury → step 3.
  3. Severe maxillofacial injury? — Yes → cricothyroidotomy. No → rapid sequence induction → orotracheal intubation.
  4. Success? — Yes → continue resuscitation. No → cricothyroidotomy.
- **Indications for definitive airway**
  1. Respiratory insufficiency
  2. Airway obstruction
  3. Glasgow Coma Scale score ≤ 8
  4. Severe maxillofacial injury
  5. Thermal airway injury
  6. Persistent agitation
  7. Large and/or expanding neck hematoma
  8. Penetrating airway injury
  9. Sustained seizure
  10. Protect aspiration
  11. Inability to maintain oxygenation with face mask oxygen supplementation
- After the definitive airway is secured, assess breathing and look for urgent problems that must be treated during the primary survey; treat at once if present.
- **Urgent problems treated in the primary survey** (details in the chest injury chapter)
  1. Tension pneumothorax
  2. Open pneumothorax
  3. Severe flail chest
  4. Massive hemothorax
  5. Cardiac tamponade

## ATLS Airway Decision
Figure: ATLS airway decision algorithm, as steps —
1. **Be prepared** — equipment: suction, O2, oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways, bag-mask, laryngoscope, gum elastic bougie (GEB), extra-glottic devices, surgical or needle cricothyroidotomy kit, endotracheal tube, pulse oximetry, CO2 detection device, drugs. **Protect C-spine.**
2. **Preoxygenate** — O2 ± bag-mask ± oral airway ± nasal airway.
3. Able to oxygenate? — No → definitive airway / surgical airway. Yes → step 4.
4. Assess airway anatomy, predict ease of intubation (LEMON).
   - Easy → intubation ± drug-assisted intubation, cricoid pressure → if unsuccessful → consider adjunct (e.g. GEB/LMA/LTA) → definitive airway / surgical airway.
   - Difficult → call for assistance if available → consider awake intubation → consider adjunct (GEB/LMA/LTA) → definitive airway / surgical airway.

## Airway Management in Suspected C-spine Injury
- Always think of possible C-spine injury in: coma; severe neck pain or unable to turn the neck; arm/leg weakness; head, maxillofacial or neck injury; and small children.
- If the patient wears a helmet, do not remove it until C-spine injury is excluded.
- C-spine protection is very important at this stage:
  1. Best and most workable protection: sandbags on both sides of the neck, then tape across the forehead to the bed.
  2. Philadelphia collar or hard collar still allows neck flexion and extension ~30%, and rotation and lateral movement 40-60%.
  3. Soft collar is unacceptable (wrong) — neck movement still almost 100%.
- Lateral C-spine film with both arms pulled down, to see C7 and T1 (10% of injuries are here). If the patient is conscious, add AP and odontoid views; these alone give diagnosis in 92%.
- For exam answers, choose spiral CT over plain lateral film, per ATLS 9th ed.
- Intubation: do not extend the neck (technique under orotracheal intubation).

## Airway Management in Maxillofacial Injury
- Airway obstruction is common, from blood or broken teeth blocking the upper airway.
- Conscious patient → lie on the side; supine position lets blood and the tongue fall back and block the airway.
- Maxillary fractures bleed a lot → posterior and anterior nasal packing to stop bleeding.
- Ragged bleeding facial wounds with no clear bleeding point: do not blind clamp; pack with open gauze and wrap with elastic bandage first.
- Definitive airway: may try orotracheal tube 1-2 times; if it clearly will not go in, do cricothyroidotomy.

## Airway Management in Laryngotracheal Injury
- Suspect laryngeal injury with the triad:
  1. Neck bruising/hematoma with subcutaneous emphysema
  2. Hoarseness
  3. Palpable fracture
- Should not intubate orally or nasally, or do cricothyroidotomy (as printed).
- **Partial laryngeal tear**: early on may have only mild hoarseness and tiredness, palpable subcutaneous emphysema; later the neck swells and breathing becomes difficult. Intubation must be careful — it may turn a partial into a complete tear; safe option is fiberoptic bronchoscope as guide; if that fails, do tracheostomy.
- **Complete laryngeal tear**: no choice — tracheostomy only.
- Laryngeal fracture: fix with miniplate.

## Airway Management in Children
- Children have a large tongue, floppy epiglottis, and lymphoid tissue in the pharynx → airway obstruction occurs easily.
- As in adults, the first choice to secure the airway in pediatric trauma is ET tube with rapid sequence intubation. If ET intubation fails, next is laryngeal mask airway as temporary airway management, or the more invasive needle cricothyroidotomy.
- ET tube size ≈ size of the child's nostril or little finger; insertion depth (cm) = multiply by 3.
- Children < 8 years may not need a cuffed tube because the cricoid ring seals; if cuffed, use low-pressure cuff and keep cuff pressure ≤ 30 mmHg.
- If orotracheal intubation fails in a child → needle cricothyroidotomy with a 14 G needle connected to jet insufflation.

## Techniques of Definitive Airway
1. Direct orotracheal intubation
2. Blind nasotracheal intubation
3. Surgical airway
   - 3.1 Cricothyroidotomy
   - 3.2 Tracheostomy
4. Other airway control
   - 4.1 Laryngeal mask airway (LMA)
   - 4.2 Esophageal tracheal Combitube (ETT)
   - 4.3 Transtracheal jet ventilation (TTJV)

## Orotracheal Intubation
- Most commonly used; gold standard of definitive airway; easy; can be done in apneic patients.
- In trauma, intubation is best with about 2 assistants:
  1. First: manual in-line immobilization to protect against C-spine injury
  2. Second: Sellick's maneuver — left hand cradles under the neck, right thumb and index finger press the cricoid cartilage so the cricoid compresses the esophagus against the vertebral column, preventing aspiration
- Even with C-spine injury, oral intubation is possible — but no head tilt (no neck extension).
- After intubation always check tube position: auscultate both lungs and the epigastrium, to avoid one-lung intubation (usually right side) or esophageal intubation.
- If the ER is ready, rapid sequence intubation with sedative and muscle relaxant makes intubation easier.

## Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI)
- Standard airway management in trauma: faster ET intubation and lower aspiration risk, using drugs to ease intubation.
- First-choice drug: succinylcholine; if contraindicated, alternatives such as rocuronium, etomidate, propofol.
- **Technique**
  1. Preoxygenation with 100% oxygen bag valve mask ventilation for 3 min
  2. Maintain in-line cervical spine
  3. Apply cricoid pressure (Sellick's maneuver)
  4. Agitated, uncooperative, clenching patient → awake intubation is not possible (may bite the doctor's finger off); give an induction agent such as etomidate (non-barbiturate hypnotic, rapid onset, rapidly eliminated) IV to sedate; if still not controlled, add succinylcholine (ATLS gives both together)
  5. Propofol: same group as etomidate, shorter duration of action, but profound hemodynamic effect → unsuitable for labile hemodynamic status
  6. Laryngoscopy, then insert the endotracheal tube
  7. Confirm tube position; if OK, release cricoid pressure and continue ventilation
- **Notes** (หมายเหตุ)
  1. Succinylcholine — short-acting muscle relaxant, dose 1-1.5 mg/kg, onset within 30-60 นาที (as printed), lasts 3-10 นาที. Cautions:
     - 1.1 May cause early abdominal wall rigidity → raises gastric pressure → aspiration
     - 1.2 Avoid > 24 h post-injury — causes hyperkalemia; unsuitable for burn or spinal cord injury
     - 1.3 Avoid in long-standing myopathies, myasthenia gravis, or history of malignant hyperthermia
     - 1.4 When succinylcholine is contraindicated, use rocuronium (nondepolarizing neuromuscular paralytic agent) instead — but it lasts longer, so not suitable when neurological evaluation is needed
  2. During RSI, keep a cricothyroidotomy tray at the bedside for emergency use.

## Nasotracheal Intubation
- Blind technique; needs a skilled operator.
- Patient may be conscious or not; what matters is spontaneous breathing — breath sounds guide the tube.
- Suited for:
  1. Suspected C-spine injury (no neck extension needed)
  2. Clenching patient who will not open the mouth
- **Disadvantages**
  1. Tube size usually ≤ 7.5, smaller than the usual size 8 orotracheal tube → harder to suction secretions
  2. Heavy-handed insertion can perforate into the retropharyngeal space
- **Contraindications** (จำ)
  1. Fracture base of skull (e.g. raccoon eye)
  2. Fracture midface or maxilla
  3. Penetrating wound at hard palate
  4. Unconscious patient with apnea
  5. Children < 12 years (bone and soft tissue not fully developed)

## Cricothyroidotomy
- Surgical airway used when a definitive airway is needed quickly but intubation fails — usually severe or extensive maxillofacial injury.
- **Technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. Thumb and middle finger of the left hand hold the larynx still
  2. Left index finger palpates the cricothyroid membrane
  3. Vertical skin incision — a transverse one may cut the anterior jugular vein and bleed, obscuring the field
  4. Incise the cricothyroid membrane transversely, spread with a clamp, insert tracheostomy tube size 5 or 6
- Cricothyroidotomy is temporary — use ≤ 24 h, then convert to tracheostomy, because the tube is small and long-term placement inflames the cartilage → subglottic stenosis.
- **Difficult cricothyroidotomy (SHORT)**
  1. Surgical scar in the neck
  2. Hematoma
  3. Obesity
  4. Radiation therapy
  5. Trauma to the larynx with disrupted landmarks
- **Contraindications**
  1. Children < 12 years — small cricothyroid membrane, thick soft tissue, technically hard; the cricoid supports and maintains tracheal patency, so cutting through the membrane causes subglottic stenosis
  2. Laryngeal injury, e.g. severe fracture of laryngeal cartilage
  3. Tracheal transection/separation from the larynx

## Transtracheal Jet Ventilation (TTJV)
- Children ≥ 12 years (as printed) should have tracheostomy; when a temporary airway is needed, do not do surgical cricothyroidotomy — puncture the cricothyroid membrane with an 18 G needle (adults 14 G), then connect to jet insufflation with high-pressure oxygen = **needle cricothyroidotomy with jet insufflation**. Buys 30-45 min; longer is dangerous from progressive CO2 retention, especially in head injury (raised ICP worsens the patient).
- **Indications** (suits children)
  1. Emergency when intubation fails and open cricothyroidotomy is also not possible
  2. Fracture larynx
  3. Laryngeal edema
- **Technique**
  1. 14 G needle through the cricothyroid membrane into the tracheal lumen
  2. Cannulate with plastic catheter
  3. Connect to a 3-way or Y-connector: one limb to a jet ventilator delivering 100% oxygen, the other limb as the exhalation route; 1 second on, 4 seconds off
- **Disadvantages**
  1. Barotrauma from high-frequency ventilation
  2. Air embolism
  3. Subcutaneous or mediastinal emphysema
  4. Esophageal perforation

## Tracheostomy
- Usually a poor choice in trauma: deep, technically hard, needs many instruments, too slow for emergencies. Even percutaneous tracheostomy is not OK in trauma because the neck must be extended.
- **Indications**
  1. Children < 12 years
  2. Acute laryngeal injury
  3. Open wound with visible tracheal laceration — push a tube in to solve the problem first

## Laryngeal Mask Airway (LMA)
- For difficult airway when ET intubation fails; LMA is a temporary solution, then find a way to place an ET tube.
- Insertion: blind technique; tube tip passes along the posterior pharynx and sits above the epiglottis.
- **Advantages**
  1. Better oxygenation than face mask
  2. Blind insertion with head in neutral position → suitable for C-spine injury
  3. Prevents aspiration from full stomach, though not 100%
  4. Does not pass the vocal cords → less hoarseness, sore throat, cough or laryngospasm than ET tube
  5. Insertion success is similar for LMA and ET intubation
- **Disadvantages**
  1. Needs a skilled operator; hard in obese patients
  2. Cuff malposition
  3. Aspiration protection not 100%
  4. Unsuitable when gag reflex is present or high inspiratory pressure is needed
- Should be used in the field, like Combitube. Newer type: Intubating Laryngeal Mask Airway (ILMA) — no need to remove it to intubate; a dedicated ET tube designed for ILMA passes through it (ordinary ET tubes cannot be used).

## Esophageal Tracheal Combitube
- Designed for difficult airway; also called multilumen esophageal airway device.
- Two cuffs: pharyngeal cuff and distal cuff. Air passes through the upper port between the two cuffs into the trachea; the lower cuff blocks the esophagus to prevent aspiration.
- Tip lies in the esophagus → a suction catheter can be passed to aspirate air and gastric content.
- **Advantage**: no neck extension during insertion.
- **Disadvantages** (many, so few people recommend it)
  1. Esophageal perforation and gastric rupture
  2. Needs a skilled operator — correct position is hard to achieve
  3. Unsuitable for children, patients < 16 years, or height < 5 feet
  4. Unsuitable for unconscious patients

## Circulation with Hemorrhage Control
- After A (airway) and B (breathing) comes C (circulation).
- Assess circulatory status:
  1. Weak, rapid pulse → major blood loss
  2. Strong but slow pulse → likely brain problem
- **Key principles**
  1. Stop bleeding (stress on hemorrhage control more than IV)
  2. Give IV
  3. Assess response to IV
- After temporary control of external bleeding, give fluid:
  1. Antecubital veins with 18 G needles (ATLS 10th ed says smaller than 18 G is acceptable), at least 2 lines
  2. Draw 20 cc blood for lab and cross match; request blood = 2 × estimated blood loss
  3. Fluid: balanced salt solution (LRS or Acetar) first; 0.9% NSS is acceptable as a substitute. Old teaching: 3-in-1 rule, because only 25-30% of 1000 cc crystalloid stays intravascular, so give ~3 × blood loss to maintain BP. Current concept: give fluid only to keep BP about 80 mmHg = **permissive hypotension**, no longer 3:1.
  4. Always warm fluid and blood
  5. Early fluid should not contain dextrose — exacerbates physiologic hyperglycemia → osmotic diuresis
  6. Colloid: too expensive and no better than crystalloid
  7. Old teaching: give blood if IV > 50 cc/kg or blood loss class 3 or more; with massive transfusion, add plasma and platelets. Now: give blood, FFP and platelets together from the start, without waiting for INR, to reduce bleeding — this integrated approach is better than blood alone; give FFP 1 unit and Plt 1 unit for every 1-2 units of pRBCs.

## New Concept of Fluid Resuscitation
- In the past, hemorrhagic traumatic shock in the ER got large fluid boluses, then response was assessed.
- Problems with this:
  1. Rising BP dislodges clot, and large volumes dilute clotting factors and platelets → more bleeding
  2. ARDS
  3. Compartment syndrome, bowel edema, ACS
- Overall, aggressive fluid loading gave higher mortality — too little fluid → shock, too much → bleeding; balance is needed.
- Now IV in shock is no longer 2 L boluses; use **balanced resuscitation** (permissive hypotensive resuscitation): keep BP about 80 mmHg — enough to perfuse brain, heart and kidney — until the bleeding source is surgically controlled, then give fluid to restore normal BP.
- In practice give 500 cc at a time until the radial pulse is palpable; a palpable radial pulse means BP is probably > 80 mmHg.

## Estimated Blood Loss Based on Patient's Initial Presentation

| | Class I | Class II | Class III | Class IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood loss (ml) | Up to 750 | 750-1500 | 1500-2000 | > 2000 |
| Blood loss (% blood volume) | Up to 15% | 15%-30% | 30%-40% | > 40% |
| Pulse rate | < 100 | 100-120 | 120-140 | > 140 |
| Blood pressure | Normal | Normal | Decreased | Decreased |
| Pulse pressure (mmHg) | Normal or increased | Decreased | Decreased | Decreased |
| Respiratory rate | 14-20 | 20-30 | 30-40 | > 35 |
| Urine output (mL/hr) | > 30 | 20-30 | 5-15 | Negligible |
| CNS/mental status | Slightly anxious | Mildly anxious | Anxious, confused | Confused, lethargic |
| Fluid replacement | Crystalloid | Crystalloid | Crystalloid and blood | Crystalloid and blood |

(Table prints "Deceased" for the decreased cells.)
- Familiar guideline classifying hypovolemic shock into 4 classes.
- **Blood volume**
  1. Children ≈ 8% of BW; age ≤ 1 year use 80 cc/kg, > 1 year use 70 cc/kg
  2. Adults ≈ 7% of BW or 70 cc/kg — a 70 kg adult has ~5,000 cc
- Quickest estimate of blood loss: level of consciousness and heart rate.
- First sign of hypovolemia in children: tachycardia.
- Loss 20% or 750-1,500 cc → HR starts > 100/min.
- Loss 30% or 1,500-2,000 cc → HR > 120 and BP starts to drop.
- Loss 40% or > 2,000 cc → HR > 140 and BP drops.

## Responses to Initial Fluid Resuscitation

| | Rapid response | Transient response | Minimal or no response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vital signs | Return to normal | Transient improvement, recurrence of decreased BP and increased HR | Remain abnormal |
| Estimated blood loss | Minimal (10%-20%) | Moderate and ongoing (20%-40%) | Severe (> 40%) |
| Need for more crystalloid | Low | High | High |
| Need for blood | Low | Moderate to high | Immediate |
| Blood preparation | Type and crossmatch | Type-specific | Emergency blood release |
| Need for operative intervention | Possibly | Likely | Highly likely |
| Early presence of surgeon | Yes | Yes | Yes |

- Rapid responders (vital signs normal after IV) → go on to secondary survey.
- Transient or non-responders → worrying; redo the primary survey, as there is ongoing hidden bleeding to find or to operate on.
- Best way to assess adequate resuscitation: hourly urine output.
- Class 3 bleeding or transient responders need blood.
- ATLS 10th edition recommends early use of TXA.

## Neck Vein
- **Distended** → think of:
  1. Tension pneumothorax
  2. Cardiac failure, e.g. tamponade or myocardial injury
- **Collapsed** → think of hypovolemia.

## Pulse and BP
- Minimum SBP from palpable pulses:
  1. Radial pulse palpable → BP likely > 80 mmHg
  2. Femoral pulse palpable → BP likely > 70 mmHg
  3. Carotid pulse palpable → BP likely > 60 mmHg
- Shock with no palpable carotid pulse → SBP certainly < 60.
- Simple bedside check if the ER is not very cold: capillary refill — press the nail bed; colour returns within 2 s → SBP still normal; > 2 s with cold pale fingers → true shock.
- Class 2 shock: HR rises but BP not yet dropped; a narrowing pulse pressure signals impending class 3 — narrow pulse pressure means hypovolemia is still present.
- ATLS definition of hypotension in children: systolic BP < 70 + (2 × age in years) and diastolic BP < 2/3 of systolic BP → shock.
- Beware in children: less physiologic reserve than adults; by the time hypovolemia shows, the child is already in bad shape.
- Beware in athletes, boxers, or patients on beta-blockers: low HR makes shock harder to assess.

| Tibia | 500 cc |
| Femur | 1,000 cc |
| Pelvis | 1,000-2,000 cc |

## Potential Sources of Occult Blood Loss
- Hypovolemic shock from internal bleeding may be in:
  1. Pleural space — history, physical exam, ICD, film chest
  2. Abdominal cavity — history, physical exam, FAST, DPL, CT
  3. Retroperitoneal space — CT
  4. Bony fracture (pelvic or long bone) — PE and film are enough
- Severe head injury with shock → beware intraabdominal hemorrhage or intrathoracic vascular injury.

## External Bleeding Control
- External wound bleeding → manual compression with cloth, then elastic bandage — best.
- Long bone fracture bleeding → splint. Open fracture with a mangled leg may be tourniqueted, but try other methods first; if truly uncontrolled, apply for ≤ 2 h each time, and only if the patient can reach hospital within 4 h — except an already amputated leg, which can be tourniqueted straight away.
- Pelvic fracture → pelvic binding as a temporary fix.
- Bleeding from the face or nose: after intubation, pack with gauze or Foley size 24 into the nostril as anterior and posterior nasal packing, then wrap with elastic bandage.
- Deep wound spurting from the neck or thoracic inlet: push a Foley into the wound and inflate the balloon. Never wrap/tie the neck to stop bleeding (strongly worded in the book).
- MAST (military antishock trouser): now much less used; only for pelvic fracture bleeding needing long-distance referral. Do not use MAST in major thoracoabdominal injury or suspected diaphragmatic injury.

## Venous Access
- **Emergency trauma**
  1. Antecubital vein first — but not in an injured arm
  2. If 1 fails → cut down
     - 2.1 Primary site: greater saphenous vein at the ankle, 2 cm anterior and superior to the medial malleolus (จำ)
     - 2.2 Secondary site: cut down basilic vein 2.5 cm above the medial epicondyle of the humerus
  3. Last resort: common femoral vein is acceptable; avoid subclavian (bleeding or pneumothorax) and avoid internal jugular (needs neck turning — not safe)
- **Central line routes**: better placed later than in the ER. Options: subclavian, internal jugular, femoral vein. Main concern is infection — highest with femoral, lowest with subclavian.
- Central line should be in a central vein rather than peripheral, and single lumen is better than double lumen.
- **Intraosseous catheter** in children < 6 years: proximal tibia (not distal tibia) as temporary fluid route; other sites: lateral aspect of distal femur, humerus; some recommend the sternum.
- Figure: Proximal tibial intraosseous insertion — needle below the tibial tuberosity, angled 0-30° from vertical, away from the epiphyseal plate.
- Intraosseous infusion in adults: favoured by emergency physicians — during CPR, drugs via this route work as well as central or peripheral lines; blood can be drawn for electrolytes and cross match; infection only ~1%.
- Newer intraosseous devices make insertion easier and safer; IV rate up to 165 cc/min; faster to place than a C line, failure rate 15% vs 40% for C line.
- Intraosseous infusion in adults is now theoretically popular, but in oral exams answer that it is not necessary (Mattox strongly dislikes it).
- **Children**
  1. First venous access: IV in the arm
  2. Second venous access: cut down distal saphenous vein at the ankle

## Fluid Resuscitation in Trauma
- Only commonly used fluids are covered, for an idea.
- **Crystalloid** — isotonic; during resuscitation up to 2/3 moves across the vascular membrane into the interstitial compartment, hence the old 3 × blood loss volume.
- Crystalloid — 3 types:
  1. LRS — most used; electrolytes closest to blood
  2. Acetate Ringer solution (Acetar) — acetate (a ketone) is converted to bicarbonate by tissues other than liver → helps reduce acidosis
  3. 0.9% NSS — second to LRS; large NSS loads cause chloride overload → acidosis
- **Colloid** — no salts; large molecules not excreted by kidney → maintains intravascular oncotic pressure better than crystalloid; stays intravascular longer, so less volume is needed. Common: albumin, dextran, gelatin (Hemaccel), starch.
  - Disadvantages (ข้อเสีย)
    1. Expensive
    2. Dextran: anaphylaxis, interferes with coagulation and cross matching
    3. Stays in the body longer than crystalloid → pulmonary edema
  - Studies: colloid gives no better outcome than crystalloid → cheaper crystalloid is the fluid of choice in trauma.
- **Hypertonic saline solution** — advantage: high Na, so less volume than crystalloid maintains intravascular volume; less tissue edema → suits children or elderly, especially head injury or burn where large IV loads are not possible.
- Overall, however, hypertonic saline does not improve outcome — same as colloid — so it is not popular for trauma resuscitation.
- **Blood and blood components** — from donors or from the patient (autotransfusion): blood from the patient's abdomen or chest is filtered and returned, but only if not contaminated with feces or food (as printed: no ส้มตำ, crab or ปลาร้า mixed in).
- If type-specific blood is unavailable, use **universal donor PRC** (group O — no A and B antigen). Rh: men may receive Rh +ve universal donor blood; women of reproductive age or pregnant should preferably get Rh -ve.
- **RBC substitution** — synthetic oxygen carriers with perfluorocarbon and hemoglobin; appeared in SESAP questions ~10-15 years earlier then disappeared; the author does not know whether it failed or for commercial reasons.

## Disability
- Once ABC is done and the patient is reasonably stable, do a baseline neurological evaluation to detect CNS injury quickly, using simple quick methods: GCS, consciousness, pupils, motor, cranial nerves, roughly. Detailed exam belongs to the secondary survey.
- If there is traumatic brain injury (TBI), manage it now: avoid shock, ensure good oxygenation, prevent brain anoxia (which worsens brain swelling).
- TBI treatment is covered in its own chapter.

## Exposure and Environmental Control
- Remove all clothing and expose the whole body, especially back, axillae, groin, perineum; turn the patient by log rolling.
- Beware hypothermia while exposed: warm the room, warm IV (39°), or warm blanket.

## Adjuncts to Primary Survey and Resuscitation
- Additional monitoring, investigations and supportive treatment:
  1. Monitor vital signs, GCS, pulse oximetry, ABG, EKG
  2. Foley; NG or OG (orogastric tube)
  3. Blood for lab and cross match
  4. Plain film "big three" for major trauma: chest AP, pelvis AP and lateral C-spine. C-spine film has sensitivity only 60% vs CT C-spine 90% → use CT in the secondary survey.
  5. FAST or DPL can be done at this stage
- If the patient worsens or acutely deteriorates after the primary survey → re-evaluate, restart the primary survey.
- X-ray and diagnostic studies must not interrupt resuscitation.
- Again: fracture base of skull and midface fracture → no NG tube; use OG tube.

## Secondary Survey
- Done after the primary survey once the patient is more stable: detailed history and head-to-toe examination to find other injuries.
- Take more history, e.g. mechanism of injury, from those who brought the patient or witnesses.
- If relatives are present or the patient can talk, take an **AMPLE** history:

| Letter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Allergy — beware drug allergy and x-ray contrast allergy |
| M | Medication currently used — cardiac drugs or beta-blockers → poor cardiac response |
| P | Past illness, e.g. DM, HT |
| L | Last meal |
| E | Event or environment related to injury — e.g. seat belt worn or not, burn in closed space or not |

- After AMPLE, examine head to toe as summarized below.

## Adjuncts to Secondary Survey
- The patient is now reasonably stable: time-consuming investigations or procedures (e.g. DPL) can be done.
  1. More x-rays, e.g. arms, legs, spine
  2. CT scan
  3. IVP
  4. DPL
  5. Scope
  6. Angiogram

## Postresuscitation Monitoring and Re-evaluation
- After the critical phase, reassess repeatedly; may need to go back to the primary survey. Monitor:
  1. Vital signs, GCS
  2. Foley catheter — urine output
  3. NG tube — gastric decompression
  4. ABG, pulse oximetry
  5. EKG

## Definitive Care
- Decide: observe, intervention, or operation.
- Operate yourself or hand over to a subspecialty — decide case by case.
- General surgeons, especially in provincial hospitals, may have to open the head, chest and abdomen themselves since off-hours help is scarce; so as a resident, rotate to centers with many trauma cases rather than idling (as printed, a long joke about residents on phones/social media).

## Rehabilitation
- Restore physical and mental function of both the patient and the family.

## Algorithm: primary survey overview
Figure: Primary survey algorithm, as steps —
1. Assess airway (elicit verbal response, protect cervical spine).
   - Unstable → establish stable airway: endotracheal intubation; consider airway adjuncts, gum-elastic bougie, laryngeal airway, surgical airway → reassess.
   - Stable → step 2.
2. Assess breathing (physical exam, pulse oximetry).
   - Inadequate → supplemental oxygen, assisted ventilation, consider tube thoracostomy → reassess.
   - Adequate → step 3.
3. Assess circulation — shock? (physical exam; vital signs BP, HR, RR).
   - Patient in shock → 2 large-caliber IVs; 1-2 L warm crystalloid infusion; consider tension pneumothorax; immediate evaluation for bleeding: physical exam (external loss, multiple long bones), chest x-ray (intra-thoracic loss), pelvic x-ray (retroperitoneal loss), FAST (intra-abdominal loss) → specific hemorrhage control: control external loss, tube thoracostomy, splint/stabilize fractures, decrease pelvic volume — wrap, operating room, angiography.
   - Patient perfusing well → step 4.
4. Assess disability/neurologic condition (Glasgow coma scale, moving all extremities).
5. Expose entire patient, prevent hypothermia (warm blankets, warm fluids).
6. Secondary survey: radiographic evaluation, laboratory studies, urinary/gastric catheters.
