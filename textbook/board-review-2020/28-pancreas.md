## Introduction
- The organs the author most fears operating on are the esophagus and the pancreas: once complications, especially leakage, occur, both patient and surgeon suffer.
- A teaching from his residency (session trauma), from อาจารย์ ปิยะสกล สกลสัตยาทร: "Bleeding pancreas not primarily controlled is never controlled" — whoever operates on the pancreas and gets post-op bleeding is guaranteed trouble (รับรองเสียศูนย์).
- Over the last 2-3 years much has changed (new consensus from the pancreas experts); treatment has moved from open surgery toward MIS and robot. Main sources: John L. Cameron's group and Shackelford.

## Anatomy
- Retroperitoneal organ: length 15-20 cm, thickness 1-1.5 cm, width (upper to lower border) 3 cm, weight 75-100 gm.
- Lies at T12-L2; divided into 5 parts:
  1. **Head** — to the right of SMA and SMV, at L2
  2. **Uncinate process** — lower part of the head, hook-shaped; derived from the ventral pancreas; lies in front of aorta and IVC but behind SMV, SMA, PV
  3. **Neck** — in front of the PV; the PV has no branches here, so in Whipple one tunnels here and divides at the neck; at L1; length 1.5-2 cm
  4. **Body** — in front of the aorta and L1
  5. **Tail** — at about T12; ends at the splenic hilum

## Pancreatic duct (PD)
1. **Main PD or duct of Wirsung** — drains pancreatic enzyme from tail to head; lies below the CBD; usually joins the CBD as a common channel before opening into the second part of the duodenum. Only 10% have PD and CBD opening separately into the duodenum.
   - Normal main PD size: about 4 mm at the head, tapering to 1-2 cm (as printed) at the tail.
   - In the pancreas the main PD usually lies about 0.7-1 cm deep from the pancreatic surface, but sometimes more superficial — so be careful when suturing the pancreas.
   - The main PD gives off about 20 branches; pressure in the PD is about 2 times that in the CBD, preventing bile reflux into the PD.
2. **Accessory duct of Santorini** — arises from the dorsal pancreas, usually joins the duct of Wirsung; drains the anterior and upper part of the pancreatic head; opens at the minor papilla about 2 cm above the ampulla of Vater.
- **Ampulla of Vater and sphincter of Oddi** — muscular fibers around the CBD and PD; control flow of bile and pancreatic juice into the duodenum and prevent reflux of duodenal content and bile into the PD. Sphincter action is controlled by neural and hormonal factors.

## Blood supply
1. **Head** — superior (from GDA) and inferior (from SMA) pancreaticoduodenal A.
2. **Body & tail** — branches of the splenic A.
- **Venous drainage** — into the portal system.
- **Lymphatic** — to SMA nodes and celiac nodes.
- **Nerve supply** (focus on the sympathetic):
  1. **Sympathetic nerve** — from greater and lesser splanchnic nerves, starting from preganglionic fibers of the spinal cord at T5-T9; in the abdomen they join the celiac ganglion beside the aorta. Pain fibers travel with the sympathetics — so neurolytic block for pain is done at the sympathetics.
  2. **Parasympathetic nerve** — from the vagus nerve (CN 10).

## Physiology
- Two main functions: 1) exocrine, 2) endocrine. (Kept brief, as printed.)

## Exocrine Function
1. Pancreatic juice 500-800 cc/day; clear, colourless, alkaline pH 7.6-9, isoosmotic; main anion HCO3−.
2. Many enzymes; 3 of interest:
   - 2.1 Amylase — digests carbohydrate
   - 2.2 Trypsinogen — digests protein
   - 2.3 Lipase — digests fat
- Most pancreatic enzymes are secreted as inactive proenzymes; in the alkaline duodenum they are activated with help of enterokinase from the duodenum. **Exception: amylase and lipase are secreted already active** (จำ — has been examined; if only one choice, answer amylase).
- Chronic pancreatitis → fat malabsorption (จำ).
- **Enzyme secretion phases**
  1. Cephalic phase — sight, smell, wanting to eat, via the vagus nerve
  2. Gastric phase — gastric distension stimulates secretin
  3. Intestinal phase — food in the duodenum stimulates hormones for much more secretion
- **Enzyme secretion control**
  1. CCK and vagal parasympathetic response control enzyme secretion, especially proteolytic enzymes, in the cephalic and gastric phases
  2. VIP and secretin (stimulated by HCl) control water and electrolyte secretion, especially bicarbonate; the major stimulant is secretin

## Endocrine Function
- Hormone-producing cells = islets of Langerhans, 2% of pancreatic tissue. Main 4:
  1. **Alpha cell** — mostly in body & tail; secretes glucagon, action opposite to insulin
  2. **Beta cell** — throughout the pancreas; secretes insulin, amylin, pancreatostatin; "breaks down glycogen, raising blood sugar" (as printed)
  3. **Delta cell** — throughout the pancreas; secretes somatostatin; inhibits pancreatic secretion and gut peptides
  4. **PP or F cell** — mostly in the uncinate process; secretes pancreatic polypeptide; inhibits pancreatic exocrine secretion

## Embryology
- Know a little to understand congenital abnormalities of the pancreas (formation 5 weeks, rotation 6-7 weeks, fusion 8 weeks).
  1. The pancreas comes from fusion of the ventral and dorsal buds.
  2. The ventral bud develops together with the CBD and forms the head and uncinate process; the dorsal bud becomes body & tail.
  3. Rotation brings ventral and dorsal buds together to fuse; the ventral duct (Wirsung), the main pancreatic duct, joins the dorsal duct (duct of Santorini, minor or accessory duct).
  4. Pancreatic duct openings — 2 sites:
     - 4.1 Wirsung (main duct) drains juice to the major papilla (ampulla of Vater)
     - 4.2 Santorini (minor duct) drains body & tail juice, opening at both major and minor papillae

## Heterotopic Pancreas
- Rest of pancreatic tissue in an abnormal location.
- Common in stomach and duodenum (duodenum more), also Meckel's, colon, appendix.
- EGD: submucosal lesion, yellowish firm nodule with central umbilication.
- Histology: both exocrine and endocrine glands (จำ).
- Mostly asymptomatic, found incidentally on upper endoscopy; no cancer risk.
- **Indications for resection**
  1. Symptomatic, e.g. bleeding, obstruction, ulceration
  2. Incidental intraoperative finding — stomach opened for any reason and a yellow umbilicated nodule found → excise it

## Annular Pancreas
- Congenital anomaly: a band of ventral pancreas rotates around and encircles the 2nd part of the duodenum.
- 40% have duodenal atresia or stenosis; also seen in Down syndrome.
- Two age peaks: 50% in infancy, 50% at age > 40 years.
- Mostly asymptomatic; if symptomatic → upper gut obstruction.
- Plain abdomen: may show double bubble sign like duodenal atresia.
- Upper GI: smooth eccentric indentation along lateral portion of descending duodenum.
- CT: ring of pancreatic tissue around the duodenum with narrowed duodenal lumen.
- EGD: narrowed duodenal lumen without mucosal abnormality.
- **Treatment** — relieve duodenal obstruction:
  1. Duodenoduodenostomy — 1st choice
  2. Duodenojejunostomy — 2nd choice
  3. Gastrojejunostomy — last choice
- Never try to divide the band (จำ) — causes pancreatic fistula, can be fatal.

## Pancreas Divisum
- Failure of fusion of ventral and dorsal pancreas: the ventral duct (Wirsung) opening at the ampulla drains only the uncinate process, while the dorsal duct (Santorini) — the minor duct opening at the small minor papilla — must drain almost all the juice → stasis, raised duct pressure → pancreatitis.
- Found in 4-14% of the population.
- Diagnosis: MRCP or ERCP — contrast at the major papilla opacifies only the uncinate; contrast at the minor papilla shows Santorini draining most of the gland.
- Now popular: **stimulated secretin MRCP** — IV bolus secretin to stimulate secretion; if open, enzyme flows out; with minor papilla stenosis almost no flow, plus persistent duct dilation > 3 mm.
- Key: a young patient with recurrent acute pancreatitis → think of it and send for MRCP or ERCP.
- **Treatment**
  1. ES or stent at the minor papilla; if this fails → open sphincteroplasty.
  2. The accessory papilla orifice is very small, hard to find (even for pros); look 2 cm above the major papilla and about 1 cm medial to it. Success cannulating Santorini 90-95%.
  3. Open minor duct sphincterotomy for failed endoscopic treatment.
  3. (numbered 3 again, as printed) If long-standing and chronic pancreatitis (CP) has developed → treat as CP.

## Pancreatic Rules
- Rules a surgeon must know before treating, especially operating on, the pancreas:
  1. One can live without a pancreas, but quality of life is poor:
     - 1.1 Exocrine — oral enzyme replacement
     - 1.2 Endocrine — e.g. DM controlled with insulin
  2. 20% of normal pancreas is enough to function — a normal pancreas can be resected up to 80%.
  3. An abnormal pancreas (e.g. chronic pancreatitis) — even a small resection causes both exocrine and endocrine insufficiency.

## ERCP
- Consider when intervention or endoscopic treatment is needed:
  1. Pseudocyst failing conservative treatment — to see where the duct is disrupted
  2. Pancreas divisum
  3. Chronic pancreatitis — guides treatment: big duct → drain; small duct → resect
  4. CA head — to help diagnosis and place a stent
- **Classic ERCP findings that may be examined**
  1. Pancreatic neoplasm — "double duct" sign: dilated CBD above an abruptly cut-off pancreatic duct. Besides cancer, also seen in CP and autoimmune pancreatitis; CBD stone does not give this sign.
  2. Chronic pancreatitis — double duct sign with irregular beading of the pancreatic duct.
  3. Cholangiocarcinoma — stenosis of the bile duct with normal pancreatic duct.

## Acute Pancreatitis (AP)
- Definition (international meeting, Atlanta, USA): acute inflammation of the pancreas including surrounding regional tissue; may be severe enough to affect many body systems.
- Two phases: early (within 2 weeks, with SIRS) and late (2-4 weeks).
- Severity is graded by clinical features plus radiologic evaluation. The older, widely used **Atlanta classification (1992)**:
  1. **Mild AP** (edematous pancreatitis) — mild inflammation, minimal organ effect, usually recovers fully
  2. **Severe AP** (necrotizing pancreatitis) — organ failure or local complication such as necrosis, abscess or pseudocyst
  3. **Acute fluid collection** — early; fluid near the pancreas without a wall; often multiple, high amylase; often resolves spontaneously
  4. **Acute pseudocyst** — as 3 but with a wall of fibrosis or granulation tissue, usually 4-6 weeks after onset
  5. **Chronic pseudocyst** — pseudocyst following CP
  5. (numbered 5 again, as printed) **Pancreatic necrosis** — area of nonviable pancreatic parenchyma and peripancreatic fat necrosis
  6. **Pancreatic abscess** — collection of pus near the pancreas without pancreatic necrosis
- 2009 update of the Atlanta classification (more detailed, to guide treatment):
  1. **Weeks 1-2 (early phase)** — mainly clinical, using organ failure and SIRS:
     - 1.1 Organ failure > 48 hr (respiratory, CVS, renal)
     - 1.2 Persistent SIRS > 48 hr; Marshall score helps assess organ failure
     - 1.3 Severity parameters
  2. **After 2-4 wk (late phase)** — mainly morphology, focusing on local complications:
     - 2.1 Peripancreatic necrosis and acute postnecrotic necrosis added
     - 2.2 After 4 weeks, the term pancreatic abscess is dropped and renamed walled-off necrosis
- In 2012 a revision of the Atlanta classification was held, published 2013 (as printed, a joke about the stress of keeping up).
- **Revised Atlanta 2013** — severity from 2 to 3 groups (mild, moderate, severe) by local and systemic complications plus organ failure assessed with the modified Marshall score.

| Atlanta criteria (1993) | Atlanta Revision (2013) |
|---|---|
| **Mild AP**: absence of organ failure; absence of local complications | **Mild AP**: absence of organ failure; absence of local complications |
| **Severe AP**: 1. local complications AND/OR 2. organ failure | **Moderately severe AP**: 1. local complications AND/OR 2. transient organ failure (< 48 h) |
| GI bleeding (> 500 cc/24 hr) | **Severe AP**: persistent organ failure > 48 h (now defined by Modified Marshall Score) |
| Shock — SBP ≤ 90 mmHg | |
| PaO2 ≤ 60% | |
| Creatinine ≥ 2 mg/dl | |

- **2013 definitions of local complications** (clearer; may change again):
  1. **Acute pancreatic fluid collection (APFC)** — homogeneous fluid around a swollen pancreas within 4 weeks of onset of edematous pancreatitis, without features of pseudocyst or necrosis. APFC occurs in 40-60% of AP; about 90% resolve spontaneously in 7-10 days; 10% persist as pseudocyst.
  2. **Pancreatic pseudocyst** — after 4 weeks from onset of edematous pancreatitis; well and completely defined wall; clear fluid, no necrotic tissue.
  3. **Acute necrotic collection (ANC)** — within 4 weeks of onset of necrotizing pancreatitis; pancreatic or peripancreatic necrosis; turbid heterogeneous fluid density, no wall yet. In the first 2 weeks ANC is sterile; with full conservative treatment 41% resolve and 49% progress to WON.
  4. **Walled-off necrosis (WON)** — after 4 weeks from onset of necrotizing pancreatitis; mature encapsulated inflammatory wall around pancreatic and peripancreatic necrosis, often loculated. WON can still be managed conservatively, but once IPN (infected pancreatic necrosis) occurs → intervention is certain.
  - Note (ข้อสังเกต): turbid intrapancreatic fluid means necrosis is present; you must state whether the necrosis is infected or not.

## Modified Marshall score (Atlanta 2013, for organ failure)

| Organ system | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory (PO2/FiO2) | > 400 | 301-400 | 201-300 | 101-200 | ≤ 100 |
| Renal — serum creatinine, mg/dl | < 1.4 | 1.4-1.8 | 1.9-3.6 | 3.6-4.9 | > 4.9 |
| Cardiovascular — SBP, mmHg | > 90 | < 90, fluid responsive | < 90, not fluid responsive | < 90, pH < 7.3 | < 90, pH < 7.2 |

- Score > 2 for more than 2 hours (as printed) = persistent organ failure; score > 2 for not more than 2 hours (as printed) = transient organ failure.
- In the first 2 weeks death is from SIRS then MOF; after 2 weeks death is from infection and necrosis.
- **SIRS (2013 definition)** — at least 2 of:
  1. Heart rate > 90 beats/min
  2. Temp < 36 or > 38
  3. WBC < 4000 or > 12000
  4. Respiratory rate > 20/min or PCO2 < 32 mmHg
- Radiologists adapted Atlanta 2012 for imaging, with conclusions in 2016 and 2018; more changes expected in 1-2 years.
- **Mechanism of AP**: premature activation of pancreatic enzymes inside the acinar cell (normally activated only in the duodenum). Trypsinogen autoactivates to trypsin, which activates other proenzymes → acinar cell destruction → inflammation, release of many cytokines → SIRS.
- **Causes** (สาเหตุ)
  1. Alcohol — 25-30%; thought to reduce pancreatic blood flow
  2. Biliary stone — now more common than alcohol, 35-50%; stone impacted at, or passing through, the ampulla of Vater (swollen ampulla narrows) → back flow of bile into the pancreas
  3. Pancreatic injury, ERCP
  4. Drugs, e.g. sulfa, steroid, lasix, MO., H2 block
  5. Infection: mumps (คางทูม), measles (หัด), salmonella, mycoplasma
  6. Hereditary pancreatitis
  7. Pancreas divisum
  8. Hyperlipidemia, especially triglyceride
  9. Toxin
  10. Postop, e.g. GB or bile duct surgery, sphincterotomy, even PO. open heart
  11. Idiopathic — a cause exists but has not been found
- **Phases of acute pancreatitis**
  1. Early phase — first 2 weeks; host response and local pancreatic injury
  2. Late phase — systemic or local complications
- **Symptoms** (อาการ)
  1. Pain — constant epigastric pain radiating (knifing) to the back; with this "stabbed through to the back" pain, think of ruptured AAA in the diff. diag. Pancreatic pain may last for days; patients often sit or lie curled up to keep the pancreas off the TL-spine.
  2. Ileus — dull, tight pain, decreased bowel sounds; this helps distinguish from SBO, which has colicky pain and very active bowel sounds.
  3. Hemorrhagic pancreatitis may show bruising: Grey Turner sign (flank ecchymosis) and Cullen sign (periumbilical ecchymosis); less common Fox sign at the groin. Any of these signs means severe disease, mortality > 50%.
- **Diagnosis** — now 2 of 3 positive criteria:
  1. Abdominal pain consistent with the disease, often radiating to the back
  2. Serum amylase or lipase ≥ 3 times the upper limit of normal
  3. Imaging (CT, MRI or transabdominal US) consistent with AP
- **Serum amylase** — rises within 24 hours, stays only 3-5 days then normalises; persistently high → suspect a complication such as pseudocyst.
  - > 300 or > 3 times: sensitive 91% but specific 71%
  - > 1000: specific for AP almost 100%
  - Regular drinkers or high blood lipids may have normal amylase in AP, because repeated inflammation has destroyed the gland — normal amylase does not rule out AP. Urine amylase stays elevated longer than serum. Formerly amylase : creatinine ratio > 5 suggested AP.
  - Severity of AP does not correlate with amylase level (see Ranson).
  - Amylase: 40% from pancreas (P-type amylase rises), 60% from salivary gland (S-type amylase rises).
- **Serum lipase** — made and stored in acinar cells; rises 4-8 hr after onset of AP, peaks about 2-4 hr (as printed), stays high 7-14 days. Produced only by the pancreas → more sensitive and specific than amylase.
  - Lipase > 3 times normal → almost 100% sure of AP.
- **Procalcitonin** — new marker to predict SAP.
- **C reactive protein (CRP)** — > 150 suggests progression to SAP.
- **Imaging studies** — plain film, US and CT are routine; MRI is not better than CT, so less needed.
- **Plain abdomen** findings:
  1. **Sentinel loop** — localized ileus of bowel near the pancreas
  2. **Colon cut-off sign** — spasm of the transverse colon near the pancreas (abrupt termination of gas in the proximal colon at the splenic flexure, with decompressed distal colon)
  3. Others: increased epigastric soft tissue density, loss of pancreatic shadow, pancreatic calcification, opaque GS, left pleural effusion
- **US** — may be hard to see because of bowel gas; may show parenchymal edema or peripancreatic fluid. Indication for US: suspected AP of biliary cause — GS is the no. 1 cause (30-60%), so texts advise US once the patient improves. Contrast enhanced US (ceUS) is now used, but ceCT is more accurate for severity and necrosis.
- **CT with pancreatic protocol** — gold standard for diagnosis and severity, and to differentiate necrosis from abscess; sees the pancreas even with much bowel gas. Triphasic pancreatic protocol (arterial, late arterial, venous phases) with thin cuts is best. CT sensitivity and specificity > 90%.
- **Indications for CT** (only when indicated, not routine):
  1. AP not improving within 48-72 hr
  2. Suspected complication — persistent or recurrent abdominal pain, signs of sepsis (fever, rising white count), organ dysfunction, persistently high enzymes, or elevated CRP
  3. Suspected pancreatic necrosis — nonperfusion (< 30 HU) after IV contrast; necrosis of 3 cm suggests about 33% necrosis; necrosis > 50% → higher infection risk
  4. Suspected bowel ischemia or perforation
  5. Suspected abdominal compartment syndrome
  6. Unclear diagnosis
- In theory, CT is not needed in mild AP or in the early phase — finding necrosis does not change treatment or prognosis.

## Assessment of severity
- Severity ranges from mild swelling to the worst necrosis or bleeding. Now 3 levels, not mild/severe as before:
  1. **Mild AP** — 80%; no organ failure, no local or systemic complications; easy to treat, no CT needed, discharged quickly.
  2. **Moderately severe AP** — transient organ failure, e.g. fever, abdominal pain, rising white count, dyspnoea, tachycardia, rising creatinine, but resolving within 48 hours; some progress to severe.
  3. **Severe AP** — everyone struggles (patient and team); persistent organ failure (> 48 hours), one or several organs, from unchecked cytokines, with persistent SIRS; high chance of death.
- So assess the patient well, especially those tending to severe AP. Organ failure criteria: modified Marshall scoring system (kidney, heart, lung) — as printed, complicated and does not work well. Simpler severity assessment:
  1. **Clinical signs** — shock or multiorgan failure means bad. Also age > 55, obesity (BMI > 30), comorbidity, SIRS, SBP < 90 mmHg, Hct > 44%, GI bleeding > 500 cc/24 hours, pleural effusion or pulmonary infiltration, PaO2 < 60, BUN > 20, creatinine > 2 mg/dl — beware, as these may relate to necrosis and MOF.
  2. **Scoring systems** — residents must know Ranson score and APACHE-2. Glasgow and Bank scoring are not of interest. APACHE-2 is somewhat complex, but can assess severity within 24 hours and at any time; Ranson needs 48 hours to score, but is still better than Glasgow or Bank score.
     - Ranson > 3 or APACHE-2 > 8 → severe AP (SAP).
     - **BISAP Score** (Bedside Index for SAP) — clinical plus simple labs, 5 items, 1 point each; > 3 (as printed) = SAP:
       1. Age > 60 years
       2. Altered mental status
       3. Presence of SIRS
       4. BUN > 25
       5. Pleural effusion
     - BISAP is easy and can be done in the first 24 hours, but sensitivity is low.
  3. **Biological markers** — mostly cytokine measurements in SIRS; complex and not widely available. Residents should know:
     - 3.1 CRP — now standard; > 150 = SAP, but needs 48 hr to rise and be interpretable
     - 3.2 Others: IL-6, PMN elastase, trypsinogen activation peptide (TAP) — limited availability
  4. **CT severity index**

|---|---|---|

| Age | > 55 | > 70 |
| WBC | > 16,000 | > 18,000 |
| Blood glucose | > 200 | > 220 |
| AST | > 250 | > 250 |
| LDH | > 350 | > 400 |
| **At 48 hours** | | |
| Hct | > 10 (as printed) | > 10 (as printed) |
| BUN increase | > 5 | > 2 |
| Base deficit | > 4 | > 5 |

| Calcium | < 8 | < 8 |

- **Interpretation**: 1-2 criteria MTR < 1%; 3 criteria MTR 10%; 4 criteria MTR 15%; 5-6 criteria MTR 40%; > 7 criteria MTR > 50%.
- **Notes** (หมายเหตุ)
  1. Needs 48 hr; does not work well if AP presents after 48 hr.
  2. Biliary AP criteria are mostly higher than nonbiliary.
  3. SAP has criteria ≥ 3 — in the board exam answer ICU immediately.
  4. Beware distractors that are not criteria: amylase, lipase, bilirubin.
  5. Calcium is hypo-, not hyper-.

## Acute Physiology And Chronic Health Evaluation-2
- Abbreviated APACHE-2.
- Scoring based on 3 main factors:
  1. Physiological status — V/S, ABG, electrolytes, Hct, WBC count, GCS
  2. Age — older is worse
  3. Chronic health status
- Score ≥ 8 = severe.
- Notes for APACHE-2:
  1. Can be assessed at any time and in detail; a good guide for treatment, especially better than Ranson for biliary AP.
  2. Complicated, confusing, too much data — the person filling in the checklist gets bored and slow (as printed).

## CT Severity Index (Balthazar Score)
- From CT, score the inflammation of the pancreas and the amount of necrosis, then add; higher score = more severe.

| CT grade | Score |
|---|---|
| (A) Normal pancreas | 0 |
| (B) Oedematous pancreatitis | 1 |
| (C) B plus mild extrapancreatic changes | 2 |
| (D) Severe extrapancreatic changes including one fluid collection | 3 |
| (E) Multiple or extensive extrapancreatic collections | 4 |
| **Necrosis** | |
| None | 0 |
| < One third | 2 |
| > One third, < one half | 4 |
| > Half | 6 |

| CT severity index = CT grade + necrosis score | Complications | Deaths |
|---|---|---|
| 0-3 | 8% | 3% |
| 4-6 | 35% | 6% |
| 7-10 | 92% | 17% |

(Modified from the World Association guidelines, based on Balthazar and colleagues.)
- Example: no necrosis → 0; < 50% → score 4; > 50% → score 6. Peripancreatic fluid collection on CT → score ≥ 3.
- Total of necrosis score and CT score predicts MBR and MTR:
  1. Score 0-3 (mild AP): MBR 8%, MTR < 3%
  2. Score 4-6 (moderate AP): MBR 35%, MTR 8% (as printed)
  3. Score 7-10 (severe AP): MBR 92%, MTR 17%
- Advantage: shows local complications clearly; disadvantage: clear images need 72-96 hours.

## Management of Acute Pancreatitis
- Recommended reading: Shackelford 2019 (very clear); Schwartz 2019 is only fair.
- Principle: do everything to stop the mild form progressing to severe.
  1. NPO — until ileus resolves, pain gone and amylase normal.
  2. Correct fluid and electrolyte abnormalities — early on the patient needs a lot of fluid because of third-space loss, fever, vomiting. IAP guideline recommends RLS (reduces SIRS and organ failure), starting at 5-10 up to 20 cc/kg/hr; give fluid fast until resuscitation goal is reached, then taper, guided by vital signs and urine output. When loading fluid, beware abdominal compartment syndrome, pulmonary edema and ARDS.
  3. Monitor — MAP > 65 mmHg, heart rate < 100/min, urine output > 0.5 cc/kg/hour. SAP should have a CVP line; with hypoxia beware ARDS, especially if not responding to 100% oxygen → add PEEP.
  4. Pain relief — mild pain: NSAID; severe: meperidine (pethidine). M.O. was once thought contraindicated, but now is not forbidden to the point of death.
  5. In practice give enteral nutrition as early as possible, unless ileus persists; if feeding is still impossible by day 4, give parenteral nutrition/TPN first, then switch to enteral.
     - Enteral advantages: cheaper, fewer complications than TPN, maintains intestinal mucosal integrity and prevents bacterial translocation.
     - Formerly early enteral feeding was via NJ tube; RCTs later showed that without gastric stasis, NG feeding is as effective as NJ.
  6. Still debated (give or not):
     - 6.1 **Antibiotic** — old belief: reduces sepsis in SAP. Current data: give antibiotic only for infected pancreatic necrosis; not for sterile necrosis, nor as prophylaxis in SAP. If needed, use drugs that penetrate the pancreas: carbapenem, e.g. Imipenem 500 mg IV TID for 2 wk, or Ciproflox (quinolones), or high dose cephalosporin + flagyl. Avoid aminoglycosides, e.g. gentamicin — low concentration in pancreatic juice and bile.
     - 6.2 **Somatostatin** — to reduce exocrine secretion; worked only in animals, not in humans.
     - 6.3 **PAF antagonist** — PAF is a key cytokine in SIRS, but the PAF antagonist lexipatant did not work, so it is not in the guidelines.
     - 6.4 **Secretin inhibitory**, e.g. calcitonin, glucagon — no use.
     - 6.5 **Protease inhibitory**, e.g. aprotinin or Gabexate mesylate — slightly better than nothing.
     - 6.6 **Acid reducing** — to reduce duodenal acidity (duodenal alkalinization) and so secretin release; no real help.
     - 6.7 **NG tube** — not routine; use for marked abdominal distension or drowsy patients, to decompress the stomach and prevent aspiration.
  7. Peritoneal lavage — may help in worsening cases, especially respiratory failure or shock; some breathe better, but overall no mortality reduction.
- In summary, apart from antibiotics, most of these are no use.
- So once AP is diagnosed: maximize tissue oxygen perfusion with volume and oxygen support, plus pain control.
- When the patient improves, send US to check for GS as the cause.

## Severe Acute Pancreatitis (SAP)
- 20% of AP.
- Suspect in AP in the first 2-3 days if, despite adequate fluid, there is persistent SIRS and rising BUN/Cr.
- **Natural progression of SAP**
  1. First 1-2 wk — cytokine problem causing SIRS and MOF; mainly the physicians' (Med.) role; surgeons can only give support and pray (as printed).
  2. > 2 wk — necrosis may occur; if bacteria translocate, physicians may need surgeons for intervention or surgery or CPC (as printed).
- **Summary — SAP criteria**
  1. Ranson score ≥ 3
  2. APACHE-2 ≥ 8
  3. MOF, or complication such as pseudocyst, necrosis, abscess
  4. ceCT necrosis score > 33%
- Treatment of SAP starts with resuscitation: third-space loss needs adequate fluid, especially in the first 24 hours, to prevent hypoperfusion of pancreas and organs; ringer lactate solution is preferred.
- Generally the surgeon's role comes after 2 weeks, but stay alert in week 1, especially in a patient not improving in 2-3 days → CT; besides the pancreas, look for abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS).
- Normally in the 1st wk death is from respiratory failure & MOF; after the 1st wk, from infection.
- **Abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS)** — intra-abdominal pressure > 20 mmHg with new onset of organ failure, e.g. reduced urine, high peak airway pressure, falling cardiac output, BP drop. ACS with IAP ≥ 25 mmHg may cause death in SAP in the 1st wk. Cause in early SAP: over-resuscitation → swollen, edematous bowel and ascites → raised IAP. So measure bladder pressure.
- **CT suggesting ACS**
  1. Compressed, flattened kidney and IVC
  2. Round belly sign — ratio of AP to transverse abdominal diameter; a bulging round belly suggests high pressure
- With ACS, next step: bedside US first; often much ascites → try percutaneous drain placement. If IAP falls → observe; if IAP does not fall after draining ascites → laparotomy decompression — so the surgeon may be involved in the 1st wk.
- So fluid resuscitation in AP: just enough to keep BP and urine output — do not overdo.
- **Role of ERCP + ES in SAP** — case by case:
  1. Obstructive jaundice with cholangitis → within the first 24 hr
  2. Severe case within 72 hr with suspected bile duct stone
- **Must remember** (ต้องจำ): do not do ERCP + ES in SAP without cholangitis or suspected CBD stone, nor in acute alcoholic pancreatitis — alcoholic AP is from reduced pancreatic blood flow, not outflow obstruction.
- **Severe hemorrhage** — high mortality; usually rupture of a pseudoaneurysm, especially splenic A. Treatment:
  1. Intervention first — embolization stops bleeding in 80%.
  2. 20% need surgery — a nightmare:
     - 2.1 Suture-ligate if possible, but tissue is friable and hard to stop
     - 2.2 If distal → distal pancreatectomy
     - 2.3 If not ready or nothing works → swab packing, tell relatives the hospital lacks equipment, and refer to another hospital (as printed)
- **Indications for surgery in SAP**
  1. Questionable diagnosis — is it really SAP?
  2. Abdominal compartment syndrome, bowel ischemia, bowel perforation, gastric outlet obstruction
  2. (numbered 2 again, as printed) Persistent biliary pancreatitis, especially impacted stone at the ampulla that cannot be removed endoscopically
  3. Infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN) that cannot be drained percutaneously or endoscopically
  4. Very thick abscess
- **Controversy** — current trend favours maximal conservative care rather than operating:
  1. Sterile pancreatic necrosis > 50%
  2. Deterioration & MOF
- Remember (จำไว้): surgeons should not meddle in mild AP or sterile necrosis.

## Pancreatic Necrosis
- Death (necrosis) of pancreatic tissue — a severe complication of AP, especially if infected.
- **Sterile pancreatic necrosis (SPN)** — mostly conservative: resuscitation, NPO, NG, improve nutrition, organ support; usually manageable; MTR 9%; intervention rarely needed. Antibiotic prophylaxis is now not recommended in sterile necrosis.
- **Indications for intervention in sterile necrosis**
  1. Gastric outlet, intestinal and biliary obstruction from mass effect of large walled-off necrosis
  2. Persistent symptoms, especially pain
  3. Full transection of the pancreatic duct in pancreatic necrosis
- About 1/3 of sterile necrosis develops infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN), which may need drainage or debridement. Suspect IPN when the patient worsens after 7-10 days of hospital treatment: organisms mostly gut flora; spiking fever, rising WBC, clinical sepsis, falling BP, MOF — usually around week 2. Send contrast enhanced CT (Cameron favours MRI) to look for acute necrotic collection or walled-off necrosis.
- CT: nonperfused area, heterogeneous collection and debris with rim enhancement in the pancreas → pancreatic necrosis; then decide sterile vs infected. Air bubbles in the necrosis with hypotension → think IPN; confirm with FNA under CT guide — fluid for gram stain and culture; +ve bacteria usually needs drainage. FNA result is the most accurate indicator for diagnosing IPN.
- FNA is not routine — do it when IPN is suspected or the patient is worsening.

## Infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN)
- About 40% of sterile necrosis becomes IPN; IPN mortality 20-30%.
- Suspect IPN with persistent fever, tachycardia, adynamic ileus, worsening organ dysfunction, developing sepsis → CT.
- CECT showing extraluminal gas in or around the pancreas fits IPN.
- Formerly IPN meant open debridement with adequate drainage — very few survived without drainage. Later reports: antibiotics alone (stubborn) gave survival up to 60%, but mostly in patients without sepsis who responded to ATB and could still eat; if worsening → necrosectomy promptly.
- Necrosectomy now favours minimally invasive approaches over open; open necrosectomy should be the last resort.
- So if asked or faced with IPN, the single best answer: **minimal invasive necrosectomy + drain**, whether percutaneous or transluminal endoscopic drainage. The route — laparoscopic transperitoneal, transluminal endoscopic necrosectomy (DEN) or video-assisted retroperitoneal debridement (VARD) — depends on location and features of the IPN and the surgeon's expertise. IPN cases are hard and need a lot of skill.
- IPN patients are often critically ill and may not tolerate straight necrosectomy. In 2010 the **step-up approach** was recommended: CT-guided percutaneous retroperitoneal catheter drainage or endoscopic transluminal drainage first as source control, then endoscopic or video-assisted necrosectomy. Reports show the step-up approach has fewer short- and long-term complications and lower hospital cost than going straight to necrosectomy, so it is increasingly popular.
- Now in tertiary centers (US experts), step up approach: first peritoneal or endoscopic drainage, leave the drain, then MIS necrosectomy if needed — via VATS, laparoscope or endoscope.

## Minimally invasive necrosectomy
- Includes VATS, laparoscope or endoscope necrosectomy; MIS is now strong and standard, likely to replace open treatment, which has high mortality.
- Most popular: endoscope and VATS; which is better depends on the institution, surgeon's skill and IPN location. America leans to endoscope, Europe to VATS (as printed; VARD intended).
- **Endoscopic necrosectomy** — suits WON ≤ 1 cm from the gastric or duodenal wall, at most 2 cm.
  - Technique: endoscope into the stomach (posterior wall) or duodenum (medial wall); EUS guide to see the necrotic collection and nearby vessels; electrocautery puncture through the stomach into the pancreatic collection; dilate tract with balloon (up to 8 mm); place a nasocystic catheter and double pigtail catheter (2 tubes) to keep the cavity open; then NSS irrigation — that is all at first. After about 72 hours, re-scope for necrosectomy with biopsy forcep, snare, basket — scrape, wash and suction out as much necrotic tissue as possible; leave a pigtail catheter or self expanding metallic stent with the nasocystic tube for continuous irrigation. Then repeated gastroscope to dilate the tract and wash out necrotic tissue every 2-3 days until clear. Now fully covered expandable metal stent is preferred — the large lumen drains tissue debris well.
- **Video-assisted retroperitoneal debridement (VARD)** (also called sinus tract endoscopic necrosectomy) — suits WON > 1 cm from the gastric or duodenal wall, unsuitable for endoscopic necrosectomy. Starts with initial CT guided puncture drainage; about 72 hours later dilate the tract, insert a small scope (video-assisted), follow with a large catheter for irrigation, and remove necrosis with forceps under vision.
- **Laparoscopic necrosectomy** — use Hasson technique (adhesions are dense); scope through a large port to debride, suction and wash out liquefied material. Approach transperitoneal or retroperitoneal; transperitoneal is used less because of risk of contamination to sterile space.

## Open necrosectomy
- Now used little since minimally invasive approaches. Indication in Schwartz 2019: failed step-up approach. As printed, the author quietly advises not to open if not confident — very dense adhesions and alarming bleeding.
- If open, wait well beyond 2 wk: pancreatic necrosis is mostly on the surface rather than deep, and the line of demarcation between viable and dead tissue needs > 2 wk to be clear. So avoid necrosectomy in the first 2 wk; the longer the delay (> 4 wk, into walled-off necrosis), the more liquefied the necrosis — easier to suction and wash.
- Do not try to complete the necrosectomy in one go — risk of bleeding and of removing viable pancreatic tissue unnecessarily. Principle: remove as much dead tissue as possible while keeping as much viable tissue as possible.
- Technique: avoid sharp dissection (bleeding, pancreatic duct injury); author recommends strong water jet with gentle suction, alternating with finger fracture; bleeding points sutured with nylon.
- Opening within the first 2-3 days: mortality up to 65% — demarcation cannot yet be separated; rushing in is certain trouble.
- **Open necrosectomy techniques** must still be known: in Thailand MIS necrosectomy is done only in big centers; provincial referral is hard, so most surgeons still operate open.
  1. **Pancreatic necrosectomy with stuffed penrose drain** — gauze packed inside penrose drains, with small suction drains alongside; penrose drains removed about 6-10 days postop, suction drains later.
     - Main proponent Warshaw (closed packing) reports MTR ≤ 6%, but others doing it report > 30% deaths, and 1/3 need reoperation.
  2. **Necrosectomy & laparostomy** (open packing) — Bradley's technique: necrosectomy, abdomen left open; packing cavity with swab, then reopen, wash and debride every 24-48 hr in OR, healing by granulation.
     - MTR & MBR high, 20-30%, from fistula, bleeding, hernia.
  3. **Pancreatic necrosectomy + closed continuous lavage** — the open method most favoured: MTR 7-15%. Bilateral subcostal incision; finger or forcep necrosectomy; hemostasis until dry; large bore silicone tubes 28-32 F via the lesser sac with tips behind head and tail; brought out through each flank; abdomen closed; NSS irrigation 6-10 L/day until clear; very turbid cases may need up to 35 L/day for the first 2-3 days; lavage usually 2-3 wk.
     - Fistula only 19%, reoperation 17% — fewer than other methods.
  4. **Siriraj technique** — uses a "coconut" model (ของมะละกอ as printed): bilateral subcostal incision, a single opening into the pancreas to suction out dead tissue, then 2 drains left for postop irrigation. Heard presented at the Royal College of Surgeons meeting at Pattaya; impressive results for an open approach, but unclear what happened to it — not seen since.
- Figure: Pancreatic necrosis with suspected infection, as steps —
  1. Obtain CT-guided FNA (or empiric use of necrosis-penetrating antibiotics).
     - Negative gram stain and culture → sterile necrosis: supportive care; consider repeat FNA every 5-7 days if clinically indicated.
     - Positive gram stain and/or culture → infected necrosis → step 2.
  2. Infected necrosis:
     - Clinically stable → continue antibiotics and observe; delayed minimally invasive surgical, endoscopic or radiologic debridement; if asymptomatic consider no debridement.
     - Clinically unstable → prompt surgical debridement.

## Nutrition support
- If the bowel works, consider early feeding. Mild AP: when pain and vomiting stop, start low residue, low fat diet and increase gradually. SAP without pain or vomiting and with good bowel sounds: most experts also recommend feeding now, mainly enteral via NG or NJ; early enteral nutrition within 48-96 hours of admission reduces overall infectious complications and pancreatic inflammation. Enteral nutrition is given as trophic rate (small, frequent amounts). Early TPN raised infection rate and mortality. Current reports (Cochrane 2012) favour early enteral > TPN because:
  1. TPN causes gut mucosal atrophy and more bacterial translocation → more sepsis; enteral preserves gut integrity.
  2. TPN has more complications — multiorgan failure, systemic infection, longer stay, more intervention.
  3. Enteral is cheaper than TPN.
  4. Formerly high fat was avoided (thought to stimulate enzyme secretion); now not forbidden.
  5. NG feed is acceptable; NJ is not necessary — equally effective and safe.

## Summary: Management of Acute Pancreatitis (สรุปแนวทางการ Management)
1. **Confirmation of diagnosis**
   - 1.1 Clinical
   - 1.2 Lipase, amylase
2. Once AP is diagnosed → **assessment of severity** (mild or severe):
   - 2.1 Clinical signs
   - 2.2 Scoring system
   - 2.3 Cytokine, e.g. CRP, LDH, IL6, TAP
   - 2.4 CT scan
3. **Mild AP** → continue conservative treatment.
4. **Severe AP** → admit ICU, full conservative care; if improving continue conservative.
5. If SAP worsens or sepsis → FNA under CT or US guide for bacteriology, to separate sterile from IPN.
6. **Sterile PN** → continue conservative.
7. **IPN** → indication for drainage + necrosectomy.
8. Do not do necrosectomy within 14 days (demarcation unclear); wait at least 4 weeks.
9. At emergency laparotomy (e.g. peritonitis) finding an inflamed swollen pancreas: some surgeons place drains around head and tail, but many do not — benefit unclear and may add infection risk.

## Pancreatic Pseudocyst
- Localized fluid collection in or adjacent to the pancreas, surrounded by non-epithelial granulation tissue; after 4 weeks from onset of edematous pancreatitis; clear fluid, no necrotic tissue.
- Differs from acute pancreatic fluid collection (APFC): fluid around a swollen pancreas early (within 4 weeks of edematous pancreatitis), may be multiple, no wall; most resolve in 7-10 days; 10% persist as pseudocyst.
- Acute pseudocyst = after AP or trauma with pancreatic duct disruption. Chronic pseudocyst = after CP.
- **Investigation**
  1. **CT** — more useful than US (screening only); CT in every case to:
     - 1.1 Locate it in the pancreas and its relation to nearby organs
     - 1.2 Size, wall thickness, varices or pseudoaneurysm in the wall — puncturing one is a disaster (conference, everyone piles on — as printed)
     - 1.3 Content: necrotic tissue, debris or tumor
     - 1.4 Modern CT separates pseudocyst from acute fluid collection
  2. **MRCP** — evaluates whether the PD communicates with the pseudocyst; shows the duct above a stricture.
  3. **ERCP** — to see:
     - 3.1 Bulging area
     - 3.2 Abnormal vessels — heavy drinkers often have gastric varices
     - 3.3 PD leak, stricture, CP or not
  4. **EUS** — diagnostically similar to CT; if CT shows everything clearly, especially the wall, EUS is not needed. EUS is needed when aspirating cyst fluid or choosing a stent site.
     - Indications for EUS:
       1. No bulging on CT — EUS helps choose the puncture site and avoid vessels in the wall
       2. CT shows very little room for puncture → use EUS
- **Concept of treatment**
  1. Past: size > 6 cm or present > 6 wk → unlikely to resolve, more complications (infection, bleeding) → operate.
  2. Now: pseudocysts can resolve spontaneously even > 6 cm or > 6 wk, and complications are fewer than once feared. Recent study: pseudocyst 9.7 cm still resolved in 84% on follow-up.
- **Management of pseudocyst — indications for treatment**
  1. Symptomatic pseudocyst (persistent symptom, e.g. chronic pain) — the most important indication
  2. Pseudocyst with complications: infection, GOO, biliary obstruction, bleeding, leak into pleural space or pericardium
  3. Pseudocyst with ductal communication
  4. Pseudocyst continues to increase in size on surveillance imaging
  5. Persistent pseudocyst > 6 cm in size
  6. Suspicious of malignancy
- **4 treatment methods**
  1. Conservative treatment
  2. Endoscopic drainage — emphasised: most widely used; first consideration when drainage is needed
  3. Percutaneous drainage
  4. Surgical drainage

## Conservative Treatment
- Always used alongside any other method:
  1. Stop alcohol, low fat diet, multivitamins especially vitamin B
  2. Analgesics
  3. Octreotide (hoping to reduce pancreatic secretion) — no RCT support, uncertain results; in summary, no effect
- Choice of treatment depends on whether the pseudocyst communicates with the duct, whether the main duct is disrupted, and where any stricture is.
- Nealon and Walser (pseudocyst experts) classified pseudocysts by pancreatic duct anatomy into 4 types, each with subgroups A and B. Purpose:
  1. Decide which pseudocysts resolve on their own and which need intervention
  2. If intervention is needed, choose endoscopic or percutaneous drainage — e.g. a pseudocyst communicating with the main pancreatic duct should have internal drainage (endoscopic first); percutaneous drainage often leads to pancreaticocutaneous fistula. Grasp the principle and it is easy.
- **Nealon and Walser classification**
- Figure: Categories of ductal anatomy — type I normal duct; type II duct stricture; type III duct disruption/cut-off (disconnected); type IV chronic pancreatitis duct; in each type, "a" = pseudocyst not communicating with the duct, "b" = pseudocyst communicating with the duct.
- Nealon type 1: spontaneous resolution 87%.
- Nealon types 2, 3, 4: unlikely to resolve, often need intervention. Formerly the gold standard was open surgical cystoenterostomy; now endoscopic or laparoscopic treatment is as effective as open, with much lower morbidity and mortality.
- Nealon types 3a, 3b: pseudocyst in the tail; the duct to the left of the pseudocyst is not connected to the duct on the right = disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome.
- Simple summary — Nealon classified the main pancreatic duct into 4 patterns to choose treatment of pancreatic pseudocyst:
  1. Type 1 = normal duct — resolves without surgical intervention
  2. Type 2 = duct stricture — observation/follow-up alone fails in about 25%, needing intervention
  3. Type 3 = ductal disruption, i.e. disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome — usually needs lateral pancreaticojejunostomy; if not possible, distal pancreatectomy
  4. Type 4 = associated with chronic pancreatitis (CP) — treat the CP

## Endoscopic Drainage
- Success 85-90%, complication 5%, recurrence 4%.
- No PD communication or PD stricture → transmural drainage.
- PD communication → transpapillary drainage.
- ERCP + CT ± EUS should be done before treatment.
- **Transmural drainage** — drain between cyst and GI wall, through the stomach or the duodenum.
- **Transpapillary drainage** — drain via the main PD.

## Transmural drainage
- Two methods: cystogastrostomy and cystoduodenostomy — endoscopic puncture creates a fistula between cyst and GI tract (transgastric needle puncture → balloon dilatation → double pigtail stent over guide wire → cyst drains into stomach), then a stent is left.
- **Selection criteria**
  1. Cyst adherent to stomach or duodenum, or < 1 cm from the GI wall
  2. Clear bulging seen on endoscopy in stomach or duodenum
  3. Cyst wall thickness < 1 cm, no vessels in the wall
  4. Content not too viscous; necrosis or debris in the cyst is not a contraindication
- Place 10F stents, about 2; thick content or debris → add a nasocystic tube for irrigation with NSS 200 cc every 4 hr.
- Use EUS to find the puncture site and check for interposed vessels.
- Reminder: endoscopic drainage should not be used with big vessels in the wall — often alcoholics with portal hypertension or splenic vein thrombosis with gastric varices. When reviewing CT, check for perigastric varices, not just the cyst wall; endoscopic puncture through the stomach here is a guaranteed disaster — open drainage is safer.
- Follow up with CT 4-6 wk after transmural drainage:
  1. Cyst resolved → endoscope to remove the stent
  2. Cyst not resolved, or necrosis → another method
- **Complications**
  1. Hemorrhage — 6-10%; bad luck; try endoscopic hemostasis; if still bleeding → embolization; open as last resort
  2. Infection — inadequate drainage, much debris in content, or necrosis
  3. Perforation — wall > 1 cm thick needing several punctures

## Transpapillary Drainage
- **Selection criteria**
  1. Pseudocyst with PD communication
  2. No necrotic tissue in the cyst — only 1 stent can be placed, so the tube blocks easily
  3. Partial disruption seen on ERCP
- **Technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. Stent via the PD opening/papilla into the main PD
  2. Stent through the pseudocyst communication into the cyst; if it cannot enter the cyst, try to bridge the PD disruption site
  3. 7F stent; thick content or infection → nasocyst stent for irrigation first, then switch to a normal stent once improved
  4. With PD stricture, dilate first, then stent
- Success 55%; more likely when:
  1. Stent bridges the leak
  2. Partial disruption
  3. Disruption at the body
- Failure — usually complete disruption of the PD.

## Percutaneous Drainage
- **Indications**
  1. Infected pancreatic pseudocyst or infected pancreatic fluid collection (จำ) — systemic sepsis, worsening, CT shows turbid fluid or intracystic air → certainly infected
  2. Rapid enlargement of immature symptomatic cyst
  3. Critically ill or high operative risk, unsuitable for a more extensive procedure
- **Technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. CT or US guidance
  2. Puncture and place a pigtail catheter 7-12 F via transperitoneal, retroperitoneal or transgastric route

## Surgical Treatment
- Open or laparoscopic; for patients unsuitable for endoscopic drainage, e.g. EUS, CT or MRI shows varices or big vessels in the cyst wall.
- 3 groups:
  1. Internal drainage of the cyst to stomach, duodenum or jejunum (cystoenterostomy) — wait for the cyst wall to mature, > 6 weeks; important: always biopsy the cyst wall to rule out malignancy
  2. External drainage — wall not yet mature, or infected pseudocyst
  3. Resection — lesion in body or tail where duct anatomy does not allow bypass; Nealon types 3a and 3b, the disconnected pancreatic duct syndrome

## Pancreatic Fistula
- Passage of pancreatic juice through a pancreatic ductal disruption.
- 2 types:
  1. **External fistula** (pancreaticocutaneous fistula) — usually after a percutaneous drain
  2. **Internal fistula** — leak into the peritoneal cavity (pancreatic ascites) or into the retroperitoneal space; a less common internal form is leakage into the GI tract = pancreatic-enteric fistula
- **Causes** (สาเหตุ)
  1. Acute or chronic pancreatitis
  2. Pancreatic trauma
  3. Anastomotic leakage

## Pancreaticocutaneous fistula
- **Diagnostic criteria**
  1. Output > 50 cc per day
  2. Fluid amylase > 3 times serum amylase
  3. Leakage > 7 days
- Low output fistula: drain < 200 cc/day. High output fistula: drain > 200 cc/day.
- Fortunately most close spontaneously, about 90%.
- **Treatment principle — 5D**: "Drainage ... Diagnosis ... Decompression ... Drug ... Definitive Surgery"
- **Drainage** — of the fluid coming out:
  1. Is it pancreatic juice? — clear, alkaline, high protein and high amylase
  2. Record fluid volume & correct fluid electrolyte
  3. Send fluid for culture
  4. Keep checking that drainage of pancreatic juice is adequate; do not forget skin protection
- **Diagnosis** — which part of the pancreas leaks, any abscess or pseudocyst, state of the main PD; map the fistula with suitable investigations:
  1. MRCP or ERCP (ERCP shows contrast leakage from the main PD)
  2. CT
  3. Fistulogram
  4. Fistuloscope — wait > 3 wk for the tract to mature, then gently insert the scope
- **Decompression** — normally the ampulla is a semiclosed system with basal pressure about 5-15 mmHg; with secretion, pressure exceeds basal. A leak from the main duct is hard to close because juice flows out through the leak.
- **Drug** — somatostatin or its analog octreotide; very useful: a study showed NPO + TPN + somatostatin clearly reduces pancreatic secretion; if no TPN, enteral can be used.
  - Low output fistula, or fistula after distal pancreatectomy in trauma, usually closes on its own. If the patient has no fever, juice drains well into the duodenum and nothing else impedes closure, the patient can eat a low fat diet or have enteral diet via NJ tube.
- **Definitive surgery** — if full conservative treatment fails to close it → ERCP: side leak with PD communication → stent across the leak.
  - End leak from the main PD without PD communication usually ends in surgery:
    1. Distal lesion → distal pancreatectomy
    2. Head lesion → wait about 6-12 weeks for the fistula tract to mature, then Roux-en-Y jejunum over the leak = fistula tract-jejunostomy

## Pancreatic Ascites
- Collection of pancreatic juice from a pancreatic ductal disruption in the peritoneal cavity, not walled off; cause is pancreatic duct disruption.
- Leak from the anterior pancreas spreading into the peritoneal cavity → pancreatic ascites.
- Leak from the posterior pancreas → juice tracks into the retroperitoneal space through the pleuroperitoneal foramen (aortic or esophageal hiatus) into the chest → pancreatic pleural effusion.
  - In the chest: if mediastinal pleura intact → presents as mediastinal pseudocyst; if pleura breached → pleural effusion.
- **Pancreatic juice**
  1. Clear, odourless
  2. Alkaline
  3. Protein > 2.5 (cirrhotic ascites < 2.5)
  4. Amylase > 3 times serum amylase
- **Treatment**
  1. NPO + TPN + somatostatin — > 50% close spontaneously
  2. ICD if pleural effusion
  3. Conservative > 3 wk without improvement → consider intervention or surgery; first find the leak with ERCP, then treat like external fistula

## Pancreatic-Enteric Fistula
- Usually a pseudocyst pressing on adjacent GI tract.
- Erosion into stomach and duodenum may close.
- If it does not close, or erodes into colon (often bleeding or sepsis) → operate.

## Pseudoaneurysm
- Usually in pseudocysts with severe inflammation; problem is bleeding from branches of splenic, GDA, superior or inferior pancreaticoduodenal A.
- Bleeding into the peritoneal cavity → hemoperitoneum.
- **Hemosuccus pancreaticus** — bleeding into the PD and out through the ampulla of Vater; very rare.
- **Investigation** — mesenteric angiogram +ve 94%.
- **Treatment**
  1. Angiographic embolization — treatment of choice
  2. Surgical resection — last resort; as printed, such a case is like sleeping on a bomb: refer to a senior who likes challenges

## Chronic Pancreatitis (CP)
- Incurable chronic inflammation causing permanent damage, leading to exocrine and endocrine insufficiency.
- **Histology** — increased connective tissue, loss of acinar cells, perineural damage. Remember (จำไว้): normal tissue & islet cells remain.
- Causes: alcohol 75%, idiopathic 20%; the rest GS, hypercalcemia, pancreatic divisum, hyperlipidemia, hereditary pancreatitis, etc.
- **Pathogenesis** — now mostly SAPE theory, then Necrosis-Fibrosis theory (from repeated AP attacks; printed "Neurosis-Fibrosis").
  - SAPE theory (Sentinel Acute Pancreatitis Event Theory): pancreatic stellate cells, normally dormant, are woken by AP or toxins from alcohol → activated stellate cells → fibrosis, the key cause of CP.
- **Problems of CP**
  1. Chronic incurable disease; early cases hard to diagnose
  2. Mimics cancer
  3. Difficult to treat
  4. Shorter life expectancy, from cancer, diabetes, cirrhosis
- **Classification** — many, each school its own; 4 popular ones:
  1. **Cambridge classification** (1984) — by gross and imaging (ERCP findings); morphology often does not correlate with clinical symptoms.

| Group | MPD | Side branches |
|---|---|---|
| Equivocal | Normal | < 3 abnormal side branches |
| Mild | Normal | ≥ 3 abnormal side branches |
| Moderate | Abnormal | ≥ 3 abnormal side branches |
| Marked | Abnormal | Moderate disease with one of: large cavities (> 1 cm); intraductal filling defects or calculi; ductal obstruction or strictures; gross irregularity; severe dilatation |

  2. **M-ANNHEIM** (2007) — by cause:
     - M = Multiple risk factor
     - A = Alcohol
     - N = Nicotine
     - N = Nutrition
     - H = Hereditary
     - E = Efferent duct factor
     - I = Immunology
     - M = Miscellaneous
  3. **TIGAR-O** (2001) — Whitcomb et al.; also by cause; still cited in Shackelford 2019; the author prefers it (easy to remember):
     - 3.1 **T** (Toxic-metabolic group) — alcohol, smoking, high serum calcium, high blood lipids, protein deficiency, chronic renal failure, drugs. Alcohol is the main cause of CP in 70-90%, but its mechanism is unclear, as with smoking and hypercalcemia. Reported drugs: phenacetin, carbamazepine and some antihypertensives.
     - 3.2 **I** (Idiopathic group) — cause not found; one of interest is tropical CP (below).
     - 3.3 **G** (Genetic) — hereditary, from mutation of certain genes (below).
     - 3.4 **A** (Autoimmune)
       - 3.4.1 Primary — pancreas only
       - 3.4.2 Secondary — less common; pancreatitis with other diseases, e.g. inflammatory bowel disease, primary biliary cirrhosis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, Sjogren syndrome
     - 3.5 **R** (Recurrent SAP induced CP) — repeated SAP, or vascular disease reducing pancreatic blood supply, or after RT
     - 3.6 **O** (Obstruction) — stone, tumor, or pancreatic duct stricture; pancreas divisum belongs here
  4. **Margumder and Chari** (2016) (as printed):
     - 4.1 Chronic calcified pancreatitis — usually after recurrent AP
     - 4.2 Chronic obstructive pancreatitis — obstruction from stone, tumor, trauma
     - 4.3 Steroid-responsive pancreatitis or autoimmune pancreatitis

## Tropical (Nutritional) CP
- Idiopathic group, but probably related to malnutrition, especially protein, fat and trace element deficiency.
- Usually boys < 20 years; develop cancer 30-80%.
- Symptoms: abdominal pain, DM, frequent AP, ending in CP.
- Treat as CP.

## Hereditary CP
- Autosomal dominant.
- Mutation of the cationic trypsinogen gene → premature trypsinogen activation.
- Repeated pancreatitis from a young age → progressive pancreatic dysfunction and pancreatic calcification.
- Cancer risk about 50 times normal.

## Autoimmune CP
- Form of CP with obstructive jaundice from fibrosis narrowing the CBD, often with a pancreatic mass — confused with pancreatic lymphoma or adenocarcinoma.
- Patients are often diabetic.
- Gross: diffusely enlarged pancreas.
- Patho: lymphocyte and plasma cell infiltration.
- CT: enlarged hypoechoic pancreas (as printed); typical calcification of CP is uncommon.
- Lab: raised IgG4 with antibodies against acinar cell peptides.
- Treatment: steroid, very effective; duration not agreed — some give 2 weeks, some 3 years.

## Groove Pancreatitis
- Form of CP with scar in the groove between pancreatic head, duodenal wall and CBD. CT often shows normal enhancement of the pancreatic head; less often a poorly enhancing mass in the head with adjacent duodenal inflammation — this can be distinguished from pancreatic cancer: groove pancreatitis has smooth biliary duct narrowing.

## Clinical Manifestations of CP
- Cigarette smoking is a strong risk factor for both CP and CA exocrine pancreas (จำ).
- 90% of CP have abdominal pain; 10% painless.
- **Abdominal pain** — 95%; chronic epigastric pain radiating to the back; better sitting curled forward (knees bent, leaning forward) or lying curled on the right side. Worse after fatty food or alcohol; becomes more frequent and severe, many become morphine-dependent.
  - Causes of pain (สาเหตุของอาการปวด):
    1. PD obstruction from stones → increased intrapancreatic duct pressure and increased pancreatic parenchymal pressure
    2. Acute inflammation of the pancreas narrowing the duct
    3. Neural & perineural inflammation
- **Fat malabsorption** — although lipase falls later than amylase, it falls faster than proteolytic enzymes; loss of fat-digesting enzyme → fat not absorbed → reaches colon → frequent, very foul, fatty stools floating in the toilet = steatorrhae. This means the pancreas is already 90% damaged; chronic diarrhea.
- **Endocrine insufficiency** — 2/3 abnormal glucose tolerance, 50% diabetic.
- Weight loss — eating causes pain, so patients eat little; depressed.

## Investigation
- **Pancreatic function test** — complex, slow, expensive; indicated only when imaging tests are equivocal; of little therapeutic use.
- **Histology** — not workable.
- **Imaging studies**: plain abdomen, CT, MRCP.
- **Plain abdomen** — pancreatic calcification in 30-50%; if found, fairly definite diagnosis. Little diagnostic help; once the gold standard, now not, because of low sensitivity — calcification seen in only 30-40%.
- **CT** — most commonly used: clear view of pancreas, duct, pseudocyst or mass; very sensitive and specific, available, easy to interpret. CT shows calcification with sensitivity 50-80%.
- **ERCP** — once the gold standard for diagnosis, sensitivity 70-100%. Now its role is to assess pancreatic duct size to guide intervention or surgery.
- **MRCP** — non-invasive; for duct size, site of stricture, ductal disruption; limitation: diagnostic only, no intervention.
- **EUS** — increasingly used; thought to detect CP earlier than MRCP/ERCP. Diagnostic criteria for CP by Rosemont criteria.

## EUS criteria for diagnosis of CP
1. Ductal criteria
   - 1.1 Pancreatic stones
   - 1.2 Echogenic ductal walls
   - 1.3 Irregular ductal walls
   - 1.4 PD stricture
   - 1.5 Visible side branches
   - 1.6 Ductal dilatation
2. Parenchymal criteria
   - 2.1 Echogenic strands or foci
   - 2.2 Calcification
   - 2.3 Lobular contour
   - 2.4 Pancreatic cyst
- ≥ 3 positive items → early CP.

## Management of CP
- Principles: relieve pain, treat malabsorption & diabetes.
- **Steps**
  1. Conservative treatment about 1 month; if better, continue.
  2. Not better → assess anatomy of the pancreas and main PD, e.g. CT or MRI + MRCP.
  3. Consider appropriate treatment: continue medication, endoscopic treatment, celiac ganglion block, or surgery.
- Remember: a CP patient who has not yet had medical pain treatment → taking them to surgery is certainly wrong (จำ).
- **Conservative Treatment**
  1. Small frequent low fat meals; stop smoking, stop alcohol, control diabetes.
  2. Pancreatic enzyme with lipase 40,000-50,000 U — helps exocrine insufficiency and reduces pancreatic work.
  3. Somatostatin — role not clear.
  4. Analgesics, stepping from mild to strong; if morphine-dependent, add other aids such as nerve block, plus psychological support — CP patients with chronic pain are often depressed.
  6. (no item 5, as printed) Acid reducers, PPI, to raise duodenal pH → reduces pancreatic stimulation.
  7. Assess whether endoscopic treatment is needed.
- **Endoscopic treatment** — CP with stone in the main pancreatic duct (MPD) or proximal MPD stricture: scope intervention relieves pain — endoscopic sphincterotomy with stone extraction, or ESWL to break stones, then stent (usually changed every 3 months). Less invasive, repeatable, or a bridge to surgery when the patient is not fit. Endoscopic treatment relieves pain early in 60-80%, but at 2 years only 30%; multiple endoscopic treatments give pain free 50%.
- **Neurolytic treatment or denervation procedure** — pain fibers travel with sympathetics from T5-T9, forming the splanchnic nerves, which enter the abdomen to the celiac ganglion and supply the pain plexus of the pancreas.
  - Denervation techniques:
    1. Bilateral thoracoscopic splanchnicectomy — under GA, lateral thoracotomy position; VAT scope to cut the nerve one side at a time.
    2. Celiac plexus neurolysis — inject 90% absolute alcohol to block the celiac ganglion on both sides of the aorta, 10 cc each side.
       - Alcohol injection routes (วิธีการฉีด alcohol):
       - 2.1 Intraop — usually just before closing the abdomen
       - 2.2 Percutaneous — usually from the back; needs a precise, skilled operator, mostly anesthesiologists
       - 2.3 Endoscopic with EUS guide — needle through the stomach to block the nerve
  - Limitation of denervation: pain relief only about 6 months, then pain usually returns.
  - Complications (ภาวะแทรกซ้อน):
    1. Reduced sympathetic → parasympathetic dominance → temporary diarrhea
    2. Cutting nerves in the chest → paraplegia from spasm or thrombosis of the anterior spinal A. of Adamkiewicz

## Surgical Management of Chronic pancreatitis
- Last resort.
- **Indications for surgery**
  1. Chronic intractable abdominal pain unresponsive to nonsurgical treatment
  2. Suspicious of pancreatic cancer, especially inflammatory head mass
  3. Persistent MPD or CBD obstruction unresponsive to endoscopic treatment
  4. Duodenal obstruction
  5. Splenic vein thrombosis with bleeding gastric varice
  6. Symptomatic or enlarging pancreatic pseudocyst
  7. Persistent pancreatic ascites or fistula
- Before surgery, use CT plus ERCP to see whether the main pancreatic duct is large or small.
- **2 main operation types**
  1. Drainage procedure, e.g. Puestow, Frey
  2. Resection, e.g. Whipple, Beger, Bern
- **Drainage procedure** — for CP with a large main pancreatic duct. How large is "large"?
  - Normal main PD diameter about 3-5 mm. Large duct: Greenfield and Sabiston 2017 say PD diameter ≥ 7 mm; Schwartz ≥ 6 mm. Newly graduated surgeons with little experience should operate on ducts > 1 cm, reducing the size as experience grows.
  - Now the minimum accepted PD diameter is ≥ 5 mm; ducts < 5 mm can still be drained, but the duct must be opened wedge-shaped as a V-plasty.
- **Resection** — consider when:
  1. Duct < 5 mm (Greenfield: < 6 mm)
  2. CP with pancreatic head mass
  3. CP with distal CBD obstruction
- Operations: only those commonly used in Thailand and likely in exams are covered — far too many to write all.
- Author's principle: "big duct → drain; small duct → resection."
- Drainage: the author chooses LR-LPG (local pancreatic resection with lateral pancreaticojejunostomy).
- Resection:
  1. Head dominant → DPPHR (duodenal-preserving pancreatic head resection, Beger); if the duct is also big, Frey is acceptable
  2. With pancreatic head mass → Whipple; body or tail dominant → distal pancreatectomy
  3. Diffuse involvement with small duct → TPIAT (total pancreatectomy with islet autotransplantation)

## Modified Puestow's Operation
- Very popular 20-30 years ago for the large-duct group; Partington and Rochelle (printed "Parington and Rochelle") modified the original Puestow.
- Unlike the original Puestow, the tail of pancreas and spleen are not removed before the jejunum is placed over it.
- **Technique**
  1. Unroof the pancreatic duct anteriorly, opening from tail toward head, at least 8 cm; send pancreatic tissue for frozen section to rule out underlying malignancy
  2. Clear out pancreatic duct stones
  3. Roux-en-Y jejunum laid along the duct; mucosa to mucosa suture
  4. When suturing the pancreas, always use non-absorbable suture
- **Results**
  1. Pain gone 40%
  2. Pain reduced 32%
  3. Average pain reduction 75%
- Long-term pain relief is poor: 30% have pain again at 3 years, because Puestow reduces intraductal & intraparenchymal pressure only in body and tail; the proximal duct of Wirsung and tributary branches are not adequately drained, and pain from inflammation, mostly in the pancreatic head, is not addressed.
- Note: also called
  1. Lateral or longitudinal pancreaticojejunostomy
  2. Partington & Rochell's procedure

## Original Puestow's Operation
- The real original:
  1. Remove tail of pancreas and spleen; lift up the pancreas
  2. Open the pancreatic duct lengthwise
  3. Then pull a Roux-en-Y jejunum over the pancreas like a condom

## Frey's Operation
- Like modified Puestow, but Frey also cores out the head to remove inflamed tissue, leaving a rim of pancreatic tissue along the duodenal margin about 1 cm. This drains the head and uncinate, which contain both Wirsung and Santorini.
- Results: at 8 years 62% pain-free and 95% pain controlled with medication — better than modified Puestow.
- Very popular in the USA: loses less pancreas than resection; weight gain after surgery. Frey's proponents claim patients can keep drinking after surgery without more pain (take with a pinch of salt — as printed, this seems a bit much).
- **Resection Procedure** — for small pancreatic duct where bypass is impossible: Whipple, Beger, subtotal or distal pancreatectomy.
  - Advantage: good pain relief — less pancreas left, less pain.
  - Disadvantage: loses much pancreas → more exocrine and endocrine insufficiency than the drainage group.

## Beger's Operation
- Also Duodenal preserving pancreatic head resection (DPPHR), by Beger, a leading German (European) expert, who held that resecting the head above the portal vein almost completely, leaving only the posterior pancreas against the C-loop, removes the head — the "pacemaker" of pain (pacemaker theory); then a Roux-en-Y jejunum is joined to both proximal and distal pancreas.
- **Beger suits**
  1. Small duct CP with large hypertrophic pancreatic head
  2. Small duct CP with normal pancreatic head in pancreas divisum
- Beger's colleague Markus Büchler defended Beger strongly, believing drainage gives recurrence of pain up to 30-50% after 5 years (as printed, a mild jab).
- Results: Beger reduces pain up to 95%, but American reports favour Frey.

## CP with Benign Distal CBD Obstruction
- Local inflammation at the CBD: long smooth tapering stricture — unlike distal CBD cancer with abrupt cut off of the CBD.
- The stricture is long and extends into the pancreas, so ES alone rarely works.
- **Treatment**
  1. Normal or small pancreatic duct → choledochoduodenostomy
  2. CP needing treatment too → PPPD (pyloric preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy); if the patient cannot tolerate PPPD → double bypass of both CBD and pancreatic duct
- Stents in benign disease are not recommended — frequent blockage, high infection risk, constant re-stenting; tiresome.

## CP with Pancreatic Head Mass
- Large hypertrophic pancreatic head = AP diameter on cross-sectional imaging > 3 cm.
- Head mass in CP: 30%; mostly inflammatory mass; cancer only 3.7%. Problem: benign vs cancer is hard to tell, and chronic inflammation drives hyperplasia → dysplasia → cancer. There is still no single best investigation to tell whether a mass in CP is cancer.
- CT: focal fibrotic enlargement in CP is hard to distinguish from adenocarcinoma; a useful clue is the **penetrating sign** — in CP the duct passes through into the pancreatic tissue; in cancer the duct cannot pass through the mass.
- EUS: increasingly favoured; useful even for masses < 2 cm; for tiny 2-3 mm masses a small probe can do intraductal US; a clear mass can be FNA'd. Problem: a negative FNA does not rule out cancer.
- **Treatment**
  1. **Conservative** — few supporters; for high risk patients without GI or biliary complications and without pain. If not operating, follow up watching for cancer — CT & EUS yearly.
  2. **Aggressive** — more supporters: even if asymptomatic or EUS with FNA negative, do PPPD if the patient can tolerate it.
- Reasons to do PPPD:
  1. CP with head mass has cancer cells in 3.5-4%; if lucky and early, 5 year survival 10-20%
  2. Solves pain and pancreatic duct & CBD obstruction
  3. A skilled, expert surgeon has PPPD mortality 2-5% — acceptable
  - So whether FNA is +ve, -ve or inconclusive, if the patient and the surgical team are fit and ready → do PPPD.
- **Summary — indications for pancreaticoduodenectomy in CP** (สรุป)
  1. Non-dilated pancreatic duct
  2. Enlarged head of pancreas, often containing cysts and calcifications
  3. Previous ineffective ductal drainage procedure
  4. Possibility of malignancy in pancreatic head

## CP with Diffuse Sclerosing Pancreatitis
- Small duct CP: duct ≤ 2 mm with the whole main PD narrowed.
- Technique: **Izbicki V-plasty** — longitudinal opening of the PD as a V-shaped wedge (like a boat), then jejunum laid over it as in modified Puestow.
- Izbicki's success led the German CP group to accept that drainage can now be done even for ducts < 5 mm, but the duct must be opened wedge-shaped.

## Comparisons of the Three Procedures (PD, Beger, Frey)
- From Schwartz 2019 p.1478-1479, citing a level 1 study of 10 reports: with proper case selection, pain control is the same, but PD has higher morbidity and mortality than Beger and Frey.

## Future Trend
- **Autologous islet cell transplantation** — total pancreatectomy, then the patient's islets are separated from the resected pancreas and infused via the portal vein to lodge in the liver. Relieves pain in 85% and DM is easier to control. Most experience and best results from U. of Minnesota; reports elsewhere are no better than Beger.

## Laparoscopic Surgery for Chronic Pancreatitis
- Laparoscopic surgery is now possible — distal pancreatectomy, drainage procedure, PD — but operating time is long because of fibrotic gland, loss of tissue planes and distorted anatomy.

## Guideline in Management of Chronic Pancreatitis
Figure: CP management algorithm, as steps —
1. **Make a correct diagnosis**: appropriate history; corroborating imaging (MRI/MRCP, EUS, CT); functional tests if imaging equivocal (tube-based secretin test, endoscopic-based secretin test); assess for and treat alternative diseases and complications (pancreatic cancer or IPMN, pseudocyst, bile duct obstruction, duodenal obstruction).
2. **Medical therapy**: measure pain severity, character and impact on QOL; refer to structured smoking and alcohol cessation programs; counsel on nutrition, supplement vitamin D and calcium (baseline bone mineral density testing); information on support groups; analgesics starting with tramadol, increase dose and potency slowly; adjuncts for persistent pain or higher narcotic needs (pregabalin, gabapentin, SSRI, SSNRI, tricyclic antidepressants); assess and treat exocrine or endocrine insufficiency (fecal elastase or serum trypsin; HgbA1C or GTT); steroids if autoimmune pancreatitis.
3. Treatment effective? — Yes → continue with periodic re-assessment. No → assess anatomy of pancreas and pancreatic duct:
   - **Inflammatory mass in pancreatic head** (with or without dilated duct, duodenal or biliary obstruction) → surgical therapy in fit patients: DPPHR (Beger, Frey, Berne operations) or Whipple operation.
   - **Dilated pancreatic duct (≥ 6 mm)** → discuss options (data support superiority of surgery; surgery remains an option after failed endoscopic therapy): endoscopic therapy (pancreatic and biliary sphincterotomy, stricture dilation and stenting, lithotripsy, stone extraction) or surgical therapy (modified Puestow or Frey operation).
   - **Small duct disease (pancreatic duct ≤ 6 mm)** → continued medical therapy; surgical therapy: "V-plasty", or total pancreatectomy with islet cell autotransplantation.

## Periampullary Carcinoma
- **Anatomy of the ampulla**:
  1. Common channel of CBD and PD
  2. Intraduodenal portion of CBD
  3. Intraduodenal portion of PD
  4. Duodenal mucosa, with a duodenal fold covering the orifice
- So anything blocking near the ampulla, tumor or stone, gives 2 key symptoms:
  1. Bile cannot flow → jaundice
  2. Pancreatic juice cannot flow → pain
- **Periampullary cancer** — cancer within 2 cm of the ampulla of Vater; exact origin may be hard to tell because symptoms are similar: jaundice, abdominal pain, weight loss, anorexia. Treatment is mainly surgery, the same operation — Whipple.
- M : F = 1.1 : 1; age 60-70 years.
- 95% adenocarcinoma, then lymphoma and rare tumors.

| Periampullary cancer (Ref. Cameron 2017) | Incidence (%) | Resectability (%) | 5 year survival (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pancreatic head cancer | 75-85 | 10-20 | 20 |
| Distal CBD cancer | 9-14 | 40 | 24-39 |
| Ampullary cancer | 6-8 | 80 | 34-68 |
| Duodenal cancer | 4-7 | 46 | 45-71 |

- In Yeo's center distal CBD > ampulla, but elsewhere ampulla is more common.
- Japanese figures: CA head resectability up to 44% and 5 year survival 27-40% — take with caution; Japanese figures always look the best in the world (as printed).
- 5 year survival: some reports say ampulla is better than duodenum — not worth dying over (as printed).

## Pancreatic Cancer
- **Overall**
  1. **Exocrine** 99% — ductal 90%, acinar 9%; the remaining 1% lymphoma and rare tumors
  2. **Endocrine** 1% — functioning 70%, non-functioning 30%
- Exocrine cancer first; endocrine later in the chapter.
- **Risk factors**
  1. Cigarette smoking — strongest risk (จำ)
  2. Diet: high fat, high protein, low fiber, low fruit
  3. Chronic pancreatitis — risk 4-16 times
  4. Hereditary pancreatitis — risk 50 times
  5. Tropical pancreatitis
  6. K-ras gene mutation
- Coffee and RT do not increase risk; Greenfield holds that alcohol does not either.
- Solid 99%, cystic 1%.
- 2/3 are in the head → jaundice as the main symptom, presenting earlier than body & tail tumors, which are diagnosed late and already spread — body & tail prognosis is worse.
- Painful jaundice in 75%. New onset DM in the elderly + vague abdominal pain, weight loss, anorexia, weakness → beware cancer.
- **Pain in CA pancreas**
  1. Formerly "painless jaundice"; now less believed — most patients have pain, more or less.
  2. Vague epigastric pain radiating to the back; some have severe pain needing morphine.
  3. Pain is better sitting hunched.
  4. Pain is worse lying supine (จำ).
- **Surveillance in high risk patients** (especially HNPCC and hereditary pancreatitis):
  1. CT
  2. EUS
  3. CA 19-9
  4. K-ras gene mutation — very interesting, increasingly supported for diagnosing CA pancreas, +ve in about 90%; problem: normal people can be +ve too — not cancer-specific.

## CA 19-9
- Normal upper limit 37 unit/ml; cut off value 90.
- > 90: cancer probability 85%; > 100: 90%; > 200: 95%; > 1,000: usually unresectable cancer.
- CA 19-9 is raised in:
  1. CA pancreas — 75% of cases
  2. Cholangiocarcinoma
  3. Gastric cancer
  4. Severe hepatic injury
  5. Obstructive jaundice without malignancy — after bypass CA 19-9 should fall; if it does not, suspect hidden underlying CA
- Not specific → cannot be used for screening.
- Now used for prognosis and as a marker to monitor response during chemo.

## Diagnostic Studies
- Several investigations are combined to stage the disease and judge resectability — curative or palliative treatment.
- **Non invasive studies**: US, CT, MRI, MRCP, PET.
- **Invasive studies**: endoscope, EUS, IDUS, IVUS.
- Note (ข้อสังเกต): with such good non-invasive studies, ERCP, PTC and angiogram are rarely used now.

## Ultrasound (US)
- Safe, no radiation.
- Always available; popular as initial study — while waiting for other tests, a bedside probe gives a rough look.
- Problem: sensitivity and specificity fairly low.

## Pancreatic Protocol CT
- Thin slice CT of both abdomen and chest, cuts < 3 mm (NCCN: very thin 0.5-1 mm is even better — as printed, rather excessive), with IV contrast: non-contrast first, then arterial, pancreatic parenchymal and portovenous phases. Best seen in portovenous phase: hypovascular mass surrounded by normally enhancing pancreatic parenchyma.
- CT is the single best investigation for staging & resectability; endoscopy or ERCP may help if needed.
- **What we want from CT**
  1. Head mass or not
  2. Major vascular involvement — and degree of circumferential involvement of PV, SMV, SMA
  3. Liver metastasis or not
- Sensitivity 91%, specificity 85%. If CT reads unresectable, only 2% can be resected. If CT reads resectable, 90% are truly resectable.
- **Problems of CT**
  1. Hard to detect masses < 0.5 cm (formerly 1 cm)
  2. Limited for peritoneal carcinomatosis
  3. Radiologist must be highly skilled — a precise reader helps a lot
- **CT Criteria for Resectability**
  1. No extrapancreatic disease
  2. No evidence of tumor encasement of SMA and celiac axis
  3. PV and SMV invasion is no longer a contraindication, as long as venous reconstruction is possible
- Tip (ข้อแนะนำ): practise looking at the fat plane between SMV and SMA:
  1. Fat plane still present → probably resectable
  2. Tumor surrounding SMA, no periarterial plane → probably unresectable
- **CT Criteria for Unresectability**
  1. Extrapancreatic disease, e.g. liver, lung, peritoneal metastasis, ascites, or +ve lymph nodes outside the resection line
  2. Encasement of SMA or celiac axis ≥ 180 degrees
- **CT Criteria for Marginal or Borderline resectability** — cancer invading veins (SMV, PV) and arteries (HA, celiac, SMA). Several groups define borderline resectable criteria (AHPBA/SSAT, NCCN and 2-3 others); the author prefers **MD. Anderson Criteria**:
  1. **SMA**
     - 1.1 Resectable — normal tissue plane still visible between tumor and vessel
     - 1.2 Marginal — tumor involvement < 50% of SMA circumference, or abutment = tumor-vessel contact ≤ 180 degrees (everyone agrees)
     - 1.3 Locally advanced — tumor encasement > 50% or > 180° → palliative care is enough
  2. **Celiac axis & common hepatic artery**
     - 2.1 Resectable — normal tissue plane between tumor and vessel, no arterial abutment or contact with SMV/PV → usually Whipple
     - 2.2 Marginal or borderline — tumor abutment of SMA or celiac artery < 180 degrees, or short segment encasement or abutment of CHA > 180 degrees; for SMV/PV, tumor contact with SMV/PV > 180 degrees, or contour irregularity of SMV/PV even if < 180 degrees
     - 2.3 Locally advanced — encasement of SMA, celiac > 180 degrees or aorta, or SMV/PV occlusion not reconstructable, or metastasis → as printed, prepare the patient's body and testament
     - 2.4 Celiac-aortic involvement > 180 degrees — most experts call it unresectable
  3. **PV, SMV** — even with tumor infiltration or occlusion, if reconstruction is possible → go ahead; if not, abandon. (Figure caption: any venous involvement of the SMV or portal vein allows for safe resection and reconstruction.)

## MRI
- Advantage over CT: may detect small metastases to the liver better, or for contrast-allergic patients. NCCN 2016: slice thickness < 6 mm in both coronal and axial views.
- Overall MRI gives no more than CT (as printed, a joke about not doing it to save money).

## MRCP
- May be used if distal CBD cancer is suspected.

## PET SCAN
- Cannot yet replace CT, though gaining support; +ve sites show as bright areas.
- If CT is clear, PET is not needed; if CT is unclear, adding PET increases positivity (CT doubtful → PET shows a clear bright area).
- Yeo favours PET for suspected metastasis, assessing response to neoadjuvant treatment, and postresection surveillance.
- **Drawbacks of PET**
  1. Poor at separating inflammation from tumor
  2. Not for pregnant women
  3. Diabetics: control glucose before the test — DTX not > 150

## EUS
- Assesses the periampullary region: upper endoscope passed beyond the 2nd part of the duodenum, then slowly withdrawn to view the pancreatic head and PV, then back into the stomach to view body and tail.
- **Advantages**
  1. Assessment of T and N staging
  2. Detects small tumors < 2 cm better than CT
  3. Tissue diagnosis by tru-cut needle biopsy; NCCN 2016: when tissue diagnosis is needed, EUS-FNA is preferred to CT-guided FNA
  4. Evaluates vascular tumor invasion
- Still debated: a pancreatic head mass clearly resectable on ceCT — is EUS + biopsy needed? Whatever the result, Whipple is still done. NCCN 2016: EUS should not be routine staging.
  - Exception: tumor at the ampulla or duodenum — biopsy helps choose the operation; if benign, local excision may suffice.
- **Limitations of EUS**
  1. Operator dependent — a careless reader leads to wrong decisions; even experts may miss masses < 2 cm
  2. Limited availability — anywhere in the world, out-of-hours EUS is hard
  3. Needs sedation
  4. Limited penetration — cannot assess peritoneal seeding or distant metastasis
  5. Not possible with anatomical problems, e.g. gastric outlet obstruction
  6. Cannot separate benign inflammatory from malignant lesions — needs histology

## IDUS
- Intraductal US — new tool to show microinvasion of ampullary tumors not seen on EUS.
- Technique: during ERCP or PTC, a small US probe (2-2.4 mm) is passed into CBD and PD; can find small lesions.
- Overall accuracy 89%.
- **Advantages**
  1. Diagnoses intraductal tumors, e.g. IPMN
  2. Separates benign from malignant stricture better than EUS
  3. Shows longitudinal spread of bile duct CA along the CBD better than ERCP

## IVUS
- Intravenous US — new Japanese tool; used when 3D-helical CT is unclear about SMV and PV invasion; the author's teacher believes IVUS shows PV invasion best.
- Author's personal view: fancy, not practical in Thailand or outside Japan.

## Laparoscopy and LUS
- EUS has limits — masses < 5 mm are often missed; laparoscopy with LUS detects occult metastases, especially small ones on the liver surface or peritoneum, better than both CT and EUS. Some institutions do it in every case, some selectively.
- Advantages (printed "ข้อดีของ EUS", as printed — the context is laparoscopy/LUS):
  1. Peritoneal seeding — much better than CT; seen and biopsied; washing for cytology also possible
  2. Superficial liver metastasis, especially right lobe, seen more clearly — EUS often misses these
  3. LUS assesses vascular invasion better than EUS
  4. With long waits (too long waiting list > 6-8 weeks), new liver or peritoneal lesions may appear meanwhile
- Disadvantages: needs GA; a major operation with cost and risk.
- **Accuracy**
  1. Laparoscopy alone: 70% reliable — nodes and vascular invasion hard to assess
  2. With LUS: 90%
- Debate: routine or selective — both camps have strong supporters (as printed, a joke).
- **Criteria for selective use**
  1. Suspicious extrahepatic metastasis
  2. Questionable liver nodule or peritoneal seeding on CT
  3. Pancreatic head mass > 4 cm (T3)
  4. Body & tail mass — scope every case (จำ): asymptomatic, diagnosed late, often already spread
  5. The hospital can do biliary stenting
  6. Weight loss > 9 kg
  7. CA 19-9 > 700
  8. Minimal ascites
  9. Hypoalbuminemia
- Items 6-9 suggest metastasis is likely.
- Note: in Thailand, the senior experts say: if EUS is available, use it; if laparoscopy with LUS is available, use them — in short, use whatever you have.

## ERCP
- Role in pancreatic head mass has shrunk; 3 drawbacks:
  1. Cannot detect metastasis
  2. Small tumors are hard to diagnose
  3. Body & tail location — ERCP is blind there
- **Advantages of ERCP**
  1. Suspected CA head with negative CT — may be ampullary or small intraductal lesion such as IPMN; mucin may be seen flowing from the ampulla
  2. Double duct sign = cancer in 85%; benign disease like chronic pancreatitis can show it, but less often
  3. CA head on ERCP: abrupt obstruction of the duct
  4. Chronic pancreatitis on ERCP: tapering of the duct

## Approach For Pancreatic Cancer
- No clear consensus; depends on surgeon and institution. Agreed: thin cut (≤ 3 mm) pancreatic protocol CT may be the single investigation that gives all needed information.
  1. No evidence of metastasis or vascular involvement → likely resectable; fit patient → resect. Expert surgeons in high volume centers (e.g. John L. Cameron) do what they like and are not wrong — they go straight to resection. Ordinary surgeons should not imitate (Cameron) — use CT, and EUS and laparoscopy if available.
  2. Metastasis → biopsy, then non-operative palliation, e.g. stent or celiac ganglion block.
  3. Marginal or borderline resectable — CT unclear on node +ve or vascular involvement → EUS with laparoscopy & LUS. If vascular invasion, NCCN favours neoadjuvant chemo first to shrink the tumor and treat occult micrometastasis — other pancreatic surgeon groups do not yet fully agree.
  4. If even after all investigations (endoscopy, laparoscopy, LUS) it is still questionable → experts recommend exploring.
- Further considerations before OR (from the expert):
  1. Avoid operating on rapidly progressing disease (unfavorable tumor biology) — recurs fast after surgery
  2. CT resectable but patient not fit → no Whipple

## Preoperative and Intraoperative Biopsy
- For CA head, the decision to resect rests on preoperative clinical findings and assessment at laparotomy.
- Biopsy reading is hard: pancreatic cancer has few cells and much fibrous tissue; often read as chronic pancreatitis though cancer is present.
- Rough rule: for CA head and CA distal CBD that are resectable on investigation, percutaneous or endoscopic biopsy is probably unnecessary — Whipple is done whether +ve or -ve.
- But for borderline resectable with planned neoadjuvant therapy (mostly high volume centers), or lesions in duodenum or ampulla, preoperative biopsy helps — if benign or carcinoma in situ, local resection is curative without Whipple.
- **Indications for preoperative & intraoperative biopsy**
  1. Unresectable case
  2. Unfit for surgery
  3. Consider chemotherapy
  4. Doubtful diagnosis, e.g. suspected pancreatic lymphoma — pancreatic mass with many abdominal nodes, unlike usual CA pancreas; lymphoma should be biopsied, since chemotherapy and RT are the treatment of choice
  5. Also lesions of duodenum & ampulla
- **Biopsy** (จำ) — biopsy the metastatic site first; the pancreas last:
  1. Look for lymph nodes first, especially supraclavicular LN — biopsy under local, no laparoscopy or laparotomy needed.
  2. No supraclavicular node → biopsy in the abdomen, EUS guided first; if EUS finds nothing → laparoscopy & LUS, biopsy peritoneal seeding or omentum.
  3. Nothing in nodes, omentum or peritoneum → look at the liver surface and inside; if found, needle biopsy the liver.
  4. Nothing in the liver → biopsy the pancreas, 3 routes:
     - 4.1 Via EUS
     - 4.2 Via laparoscopy
     - 4.3 Open: Kocherize the duodenum, hold the pancreas in the left hand, and with the right pass a Trucut needle through the duodenum into the pancreas

## Preoperative Biliary Drainage
- For CA head, whether routine drainage should be done is still controversial.
- Downside of routine use: more infectious complications and intraabdominal abscess; older reports even show higher postoperative death.
- Japanese groups: ERCP with stent or PTBD for bilirubin > 20, 2-4 wk before surgery, reduced postop complications, relieved itch, improved sleep and well-being — better than not draining.
- US groups tried copying Japan with poor results, unlike the Japanese reports, so two standards arose. After review, 8 conclusion criteria — meeting any one justifies biliary drainage, whether preop or palliative:
- **Conclusion criteria**
  1. Unfit for surgery — keep the patient comfortable, no pain, no itch
  2. Patient suffers from cholangitis (จำ)
  3. Severe pruritus — scratched raw, cannot sleep
  4. No connections (ไม่มีเส้น) — waiting list for surgery > 6 wk (as printed)
  5. Drainage before referral to tertiary care — author finds this odd: a hospital able to do PTBD or stent should be able to do Whipple
  6. Patient in a protocol giving chemotherapy via PTBD into the bile duct before surgery
  7. Major nutritional deficit
  8. Serum bilirubin > 250 mol/L (as printed)
- Most reports show preoperative biliary drainage does not reduce complications or mortality, but NCCN recommends it only with cholangitis, using a short self expanding stent.
- Schwartz 2019 leans toward accepting endoscopic biliary stent before surgery.

## Tumor Staging
- AJCC TNM 2017 — like liver staging, not very useful and does not relate to treatment:
  - T1 < 2 cm
  - T2 > 2-4 cm (as printed)
  - T3 > 4 cm or extension to duodenum, bile duct and peripancreatic tissue without involvement of celiac & SMA
  - T4 involvement of celiac & SMA
- Practitioners prefer classification by treatment guideline, 3 groups:
  1. Resectable disease
  2. Locally advanced disease
  3. Disseminated disease

## Surgical Management of Resectable Disease
- Surgery is the only curative treatment; diagnosed but not operated → as printed, guaranteed death within 2 years.
- Whipple mortality has fallen from 25% to 2-3% thanks to surgeons' knowledge and skill — and luck matters too. (As printed, a long joke about surgeons who are skilled but unlucky, and advice to change careers.)
- Operative mortality is now low, but morbidity such as leak has not fallen, so be careful. (Figure A classic PD; B pyloric preserving PD.)
- **Contraindications specific to PPPD** — in these, only PD:
  1. Encroachment of neoplastic lesion on antrum, pylorus or duodenum
  2. Previous antrectomy or pyloroplasty for ulcer disease
  3. Involvement of duodenal bulb or pylorus
  4. Ischemic duodenal remnant found during PPPD
- **Indications specific to PPPD**
  1. Chronic pancreatitis with pancreatic head mass
  2. Periampullary carcinoma — for CA head, not larger than 2 cm
  3. PETs
- Head masses < 2 cm rarely spread to distant nodes.
- **Not contraindications to PD or PPPD**:
  1. Age
  2. Location of tumor within the Whipple area
  3. Peripancreatic lymph node involvement, or nodes along the resection line — these are swept down and come out with the specimen
  4. PV, SMV invasion
- **Hospital volume and surgeon experience**
  1. High volume center = PD or PPPD > 40 cases/year
  2. Surgeon experience:
     - 2.1 < 1 case/year — mortality 16%
     - 2.2 1-2 cases/year — mortality 12%
     - 2.3 > 5 cases/year — mortality 4%
  3. John L. Cameron's view: for low mortality, operate > 15 cases/year. Residents should read his paper in Annals of Surgery, July 2006, reporting 1000 PD and PPPD by one surgeon (himself) — when the text is out, photocopy it.
- **PPPD or classic PD** — still controversial.
  - Proponents of PPPD as 1st choice: better QoL and nutrition; fewer gastric complications — no alkaline reflux, no marginal ulcer, no vagotomy needed; shorter operation, less bleeding. The old drawback was delayed gastric emptying (DGE), but current data show PPPD has less DGE, or at least not more than classic PD.
  - Data favour PPPD, but long-term QoL is the same for both — choose as you like.
- Remember: neither PD nor PPPD needs vagotomy.

## Preoperative Management for PD & PPPD
- Day before surgery: clear liquid diet, DVT prophylaxis, CVP line (well-off centers add A line), preop antibiotic.
- Tell the patient to say goodbye to family, reconcile, and settle property and inheritance before entering the OR (as printed).
- Residents: no sleep, no Facebook, tell the family not to expect you tonight — tomorrow is a big case of retractor pulling (as printed, a joke).

## Steps of PD & PPPD
- The author trained at Phramongkutklao under อ.วิบุล สัจจกุล (HBP expert), so his technique largely follows อ.วิบุล's, with small personal modifications.
- 3 steps:
  1. Assessment
  2. Resection & frozen section at bile duct and pancreatic duct margins
  3. Reconstruction
- **Intraoperative assessment**
  1. Incision — author prefers bilateral subcostal over midline.
  2. Explore: ascites; palpate liver; look at peritoneum and omentum for seeding; nodes around the celiac axis; look and palpate around the ligament of Treitz, root of mesentery, mesentery; run the bowel.
  3. Anything suspicious of spread outside the pancreas → biopsy for F/S, especially liver and peritoneum:
     - 3.1 Frozen +ve → no PD or PPPD
     - 3.2 Frozen -ve and no contraindication → continue assessment at bile duct, duodenum and pancreas
  4. Take down the right colon to see the pancreatic head clearly.
  5. Kocherize the duodenum and pancreatic head; if the pancreas is not stuck to the IVC and the head lifts freely → OK, continue: sling the hepatic artery, CBD, PV.
  - (Figure caption: Intraoperative assessment of resectability is inaccurate; incomplete gross resection gives no survival benefit compared with chemoradiation without surgery.)
  6. Free the neck of the pancreas over the SMV and PV by clearing the surrounding fat plane:
     - 6.1 Author dissects from below upward (bending so long his back aches), tunnelling under the neck along the SMV up to the portosplenic junction.
     - 6.2 Others dissect the GDA from above the pancreas downward: sling the GDA under the CHA, test-clamp the GDA to confirm adequate perfusion above via the celiac axis. These surgeons often ligate the Rt. gastroepiploic A or the GDA itself to lift the distal stomach off the pancreas, without fear of losing duodenal and pancreatic blood supply, since supply still comes from the SMA via the arterial arcade; some also ligate the Rt. gastric A. to lift the stomach further. Then ligate and divide the GDA and free the pancreatic neck.
     - Tip: beware a replaced Rt. hepatic A.; if suspected, test-clamp — if the liver does not blanch, ligate and divide.
- **Resection & F/S at margins of bile duct & PD**
  1. Some divide the CHD first, then duodenum, pancreas last — dividing the CHD because its blood supply is better than the lower bile duct. The author prefers pancreas first, then CHD, stomach or duodenum last — not rigid; do whichever is easiest first.
  2. F/S at CHD and pancreas margins; cut back until clear margin.
  3. Transect the pancreas with a sharp knife slowly; author slips a penrose drain behind the pancreatic neck over the PV, shaving down gradually — the PV has no branch here. Bleeding points on the pancreatic cut face are sutured with prolene 4 or 5/0 until dry, no oozing.
  4. Lift the distal pancreas off the PV & SMV about 2-3 cm, ready for reconstruction.
  5. Dissect the proximal pancreas, especially the uncinate, off the SMV — troublesome oozing from many small branches.
  6. Clear lymph nodes to the right of SMA and IVC with the specimen; expert standard: > 12 nodes. Caution: do not clear nodes to the left of the SMA — nerve injury → postop diarrhea.
  7. For PPPD, keep 2-3 cm of 1st part duodenum.
- **Reconstruction**
  1. Divide jejunum 10-20 cm from the ligament of Treitz (printed "Trietz").
  2. Pancreas-jejunum first; 10-15 cm further, CHD to jejunum (end of CHD to side of jejunum); jejunum to stomach or duodenum last, about 25-30 cm from the hepaticojejunostomy.
  3. Two closed suction (JP) drains, one near the pancreaticojejunostomy and one near the hepaticojejunostomy.
  4. The author then leaves (prostate, coffee break) and lets assistant 2 close; if complications occur, blame the closer (as printed, a joke).

## Pancreaticoenteric Anastomosis
- **Pancreaticojejunostomy (PJ)**
  1. Anastomosis of end of pancreas to side of jejunum; duct to mucosa when duct > 5 mm and the pancreas is fairly firm from fibrosis, with little pancreatic juice.
     - Postoperative leakage closes easily; the pancreatic-enteric anastomosis can be fairly complete and secure because sutures and ties rarely cut through.
  2. **Invagination** — end to end, pushing the pancreatic stump into the jejunal lumen; suits a soft pancreas with a small duct.
     - 2.1 **Telescoping technique** — author's favourite; easy: mobilize the pancreas free about 2-3 cm, then slide the jejunum over it like a condom.
     - 2.2 **Dunking technique** (end-to-end dunking).
- **Pancreaticogastrostomy (PG)**
  - **Surgical technique**
    1. Free the distal pancreas 3-4 cm; open the posterior gastric wall to the width of the pancreas; insert the end of the pancreas into the stomach about 1 cm.
    2. Remember: PG can be done with either PPPD or PD.
    3. PG suits IPMN in the head treated by PPPD — IPMN often recurs, and with PG follow-up can be by gastroscope.
- **PJ vs PG**
  1. Remember: leak rates are similar — PG does not leak more or less than PJ.
  2. Remember: PJ leaks are deadlier than PG leaks — leaked pancreatic juice is converted from inactive to active form in an alkaline environment, so PJ leak causes severe inflammation.
  3. PG allows endoscopic assessment of the anastomosis and pancreatic duct; leaks can be reinforced.
  4. Blood supply of stomach is better than jejunum — in theory PG should leak less than PJ, and when it does leak, less harm.
  5. Every PG has an NG tube, so decompression is better than with PJ.
- **Summary** (สรุป)
  1. Leakage: PG no different from PJ.
  2. In theory invagination leaks more than duct to mucosa, but in expert hands no different; most texts favour duct to mucosa.
  3. The author uses dunking PJ only — as printed, his life is happy because his grandmother taught "know one thing, but know it well"; still, learn a few other methods as a reserve.
  4. Some use glue to seal the anastomosis plus PO octreotide to reduce leakage, but no reports show it truly reduces leakage (author does not use it).
- **Pancreatic duct stent** — stent in the pancreatic duct during PJ: with or without stent, leakage is the same.
  - The author likes a small feeding tube (as used for cut down), cut to about 6 cm, 3 cm in the pancreatic duct and 3 cm in the jejunum, fixed to the duct with chromic; within days the suture dissolves and the stent falls out and passes — residents need not search the stool for it.
- **Closed suction drain** — drain or not? In theory drains should not be routine: no reduction in morbidity, more intraperitoneal sepsis and fluid collection, and drains increase pancreatic fistula risk. Without a drain, a collection can still be drained percutaneously — the concept seems sound.
  - The author advises drains in every case (Schwartz also favours drains); he believes no one doing Whipple omits drains. In practice keep the drain until the patient eats normally and drain output < 20 cc/day; Schwartz removes about D5.
  - If omitting drains, the case should be low risk for postoperative pancreatic fistula (see complications of pancreatic surgery at chapter end).
- **Extended lymphadenectomy** — to improve survival: Japanese surgeons believe pancreatic cancer has skip metastases and poor results come from too little node clearance, so they defined node stations to memorize (like stomach), recommending clearance of retroperitoneal paraaortic nodes from diaphragm, celiac nodes, along both sides of the aorta down to IMA, plus tissue around the renal hilum — many nodes removed and, in Japanese hands, much better results without added operative mortality (as printed, sarcastic aside).
  - Nakao, a regular guest speaker at the RCST meeting at Pattaya, strongly favours wide node clearance even though his own reports do not show better survival.
  - Western experts, including JL. Cameron's school, disagree with the Japanese: skip metastasis is rare and CA head nodes do not spread along stations as in stomach.
  - Yeo, Cameron and Lillemoe recommend clearing nodes in the line of resection during pancreaticoduodenectomy:
    1. Bile duct nodes below cystic duct
    2. Cystic duct nodes
    3. Anterior and posterior pancreaticoduodenal lymph nodes
    4. SMV lymph nodes
    5. Lymph nodes to the right of the SMA
  - (As printed, the author asks residents to say quietly that the Japanese view seems dubious.)

## Summary of Yeo and JL. Cameron's views (สรุปความเห็นของ Yeo และ JL. Cameron)
1. Extended lymphadenectomy does not improve survival or quality of life.
2. What it certainly adds is morbidity, e.g. pancreatic fistula, DGE, longer hospital stay, longer operations — and for the surgeon, knee damage needing a wheelchair (as printed).
- So Yeo and Cameron conclude Japanese-style extended LND adds morbidity without survival benefit.

## SMV & PV resection
- PV & SMV resection with venous reconstruction, by an experienced surgeon in a high volume center with R0 achieved → survival certainly improves.
- **Venous reconstruction technique** (ISGPS meeting) — 4 methods:
  1. Partial venous excision with direct closure
  2. Partial venous excision with patch
  3. Segmental resection with end to end anastomosis
  4. Segmental resection with internal jugular vein or PTFE graft
- Nakao, after losing the extended LND debate, turned to SMV and PV invasion — a pioneer of venous reconstruction using IVUS and bypass. Heard at Pattaya 2-3 years earlier; impressive, and Western experts seem to accept his methods here.
- **Non-vascular invasion** — CA head often invades Rt. colon, kidney or liver; if en bloc R0 is feasible, go ahead.
- **Arterial resection** — tumor on CHA, celiac, SMA, especially CHA: some try arterial reconstruction as in veins; feasible, but operative mortality high and survival not better — early death as before. As of 2020, arterial reconstruction shows no benefit.
- **TPN & feeding jejunostomy** — case by case, not mandatory. If the patient is very wasted before surgery, TPN or feeding tube to improve nutrition preop is probably better than nothing.
- The best and safest Whipple technique is the one you are most familiar with; operate with confidence and adequate experience — if unsure, do not rush in.
- **Multivisceral resection** — reported in locally advanced pancreatic cancer invading hepatic flexure and transverse colon: PD with colectomy improves survival (Shackelford 2019 p.1188).

## Management of Unresectable Disease
- CT shows metastasis before surgery → non-operative palliative treatment; avoid surgery.
- If laparotomy for intended resection finds contraindications to Whipple (about 20%) → surgical palliation then:
  1. **Obstructive jaundice** — bypass Roux en Y hepatico- or choledochojejunostomy with a wide 2-2.5 cm anastomosis; avoid choledochoduodenostomy — lasts only briefly, may block before the patient dies.
  2. **Duodenal obstruction** — preop nausea, vomiting, early satiety, distended stomach → bypass gastrojejunostomy. Without evidence of duodenal obstruction, prophylactic gastrojejunostomy is not clearly settled, though it seems not to add risk.
  3. Before closing — chemical splanchnicectomy with 50% alcohol diluted in NSS to 40 cc, 20 cc each side of the aorta, to reduce pain.

## Palliative Treatment
- Aim: reduce suffering when curative resection is impossible. Current trend: non-operative (endoscopic) treatment; operate only after failed endoscopic treatment or when unresectable disease is found at surgery.
- **Obstruction jaundice**
  1. Best is no surgery — stent; endoscopic stent first, PTBD only after failed endoscopic drainage; choose metal stents, which last longer than plastic.
  2. Preop expected resectable but PD impossible at surgery → Roux-en-Y hepatico- or choledochojejunostomy before closing (author prefers CHD — further from the tumor than CBD).
  3. Bypass to CHD or CBD is better than to GB, but GB may be needed:
     - 3.1 Portal hypertension — GB bleeds less than CHD
     - 3.2 PV & SMV occlusion — many collaterals around CHD, heavy bleeding
     - 3.3 Laparoscopic surgery — GB or CBD easier to join than CHD
     - 3.4 If using GB, do IOC first to prove cystic duct patency
- **Duodenal obstruction**
  1. Distended stomach from duodenal obstruction → bypass gastrojejunostomy, no question.
  2. Prophylactic GJ is still debated — only 19% need GJ before death.
  3. When operative biliary decompression is done anyway, prophylactic GJ does not add postop complications or hospital stay; Shackelford 2019 advises prophylactic GJ; Cameron leans that way too.
  4. Considerations for prophylactic GJ:
     - 4.1 Local invasion of duodenum even without obstruction → do it
     - 4.2 Multiple liver metastases or peritoneal carcinomatosis — survival usually ≤ 3 months → do not
     - 4.3 If possible, laparoscopic before open
  5. Metallic antral & duodenal stents are now used, with decent results; Shackelford 2019 and Cameron 2017 recommend stent before surgery, as equally effective; the author expects surgery will likely become unnecessary.
- **Pain palliation**
  1. NSAID
  2. Morphine
  3. Celiac plexus block under EUS or CT guide: 50% alcohol beside the aorta, 20 cc each side, total 40 cc (surgical approach: bilateral injection of 50% ethanol in region of celiac plexus)
- **Laparoscopic approaches for palliation** — skilled surgeons can do biliary-enteric bypass, gastrojejunostomy and celiac plexus neurolysis together laparoscopically.
- **Chemoradiation**
  1. Adjuvant after surgery or PO.RT alone — no clear supporting reports yet.
  2. Gemcitabine, FDA approved for unresectable cases, raises survival from 6-10 months to 9-13 months — very expensive, about 20,000 baht/month.
- New trend: few cases are resectable and good chemo drugs, including targeted drugs, keep appearing; chemo is used to improve survival. NCCN favours chemo for unresectable cases and also before surgery to down-size tumors, especially marginal resection — not universally accepted, since a patient who progresses to unresectable during chemo is unlucky.

## Palliative PD & PPPD
1. In principle should not be done — 99% wrong idea; a comparison of palliative resection vs bypass found nearly equal survival (8 vs 6.7 months); even neoadjuvant chemoradiation cannot convert unresectable to resectable tumor.
2. Palliative Whipple is still needed when:
   - 2.1 Preoperative tumor bleeding that cannot be stopped non-operatively
   - 2.2 Unresectability found after the point of no return in Whipple, e.g. pancreas already divided — must continue to the end

## Ampullectomy or Papillectomy
- **Indications**
  1. Benign ampullary tumor
  2. Carcinoma in situ or T1 lesion not invading muscle, with well differentiated histology
  3. Patient refuses major operation such as PD or PPPD
  4. High risk patient
- Before ampullectomy: preop EUS for tumor thickness (IDUS if available), then tru-cut biopsy of the ampullary mass for benign vs malignant; if no clear lesion to biopsy, use dye staining technique.
- **Ampullectomy technique** (วิธีการทำ)
  1. Try endoscopic first, especially polypoid lesions; open if scope fails.
  2. Steps: see the pictures (as printed, the teacher won't ask).
  3. After ampullectomy, leave 7Fr stents in CBD and pancreatic duct about 8 weeks to prevent pancreatitis and cholangitis.
- **Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)** — because T1 has node +ve < 5%; Japanese report success in 35 cases — lucky to find early lesions.
- **Pancreas sparing duodenectomy (PSD)** — for CA duodenum in the second part, which usually needs PD or PPPD; PSD resects only duodenum and ampulla without removing the pancreas.

## Minimally Invasive Pancreaticoduodenectomy
- Increasingly done; results no different from open surgery.
- Two MIS techniques: laparoscopic PD and robotic PD.
- **Laparoscopic PD & PPPD** — expert laparoscopists in high volume centers can do Whipple laparoscopically in about 5-7 hours. Current data comparing laparoscopic vs open: R0 resection, lymph node count, hospital stay, morbidity and mortality, and overall survival are no different.
- **Lap PD & PPPD criteria**
  1. Healthy patient, not obese (cancer patients are rarely obese anyway), no previous upper abdominal surgery, no lung or heart disease
  2. Early ampullary or distal bile duct cancer without vascular invasion
  3. Pancreatic head mass not > 3 cm with only minimal inflammatory change
  4. Preop imaging without SMV or PV involvement — laparoscopic experts say this is not a contraindication (as printed: don't argue with them)
  5. Firm pancreas, large duct
- **Robot PD & PPPD** — increasingly done; results not inferior to open; awaiting more numbers and cases. (As printed, a joke about the robot taking jobs.)

## Ampullary Cancer
- 2nd most common periampullary cancer.
- Highest resectability, some report up to 80%.
- Fluctuating jaundice & intermittent cholangitis with anemia from repeated necrosis and sloughing of the tumor — the ampullary opening reopens intermittently.
- CT: nodular mass or irregular periampullary thickening, clearly enhancing in portovenous phase.
- Early cases are now found more often, so EMR or ampullectomy may suffice; if extensive → PD or PPPD.

## Distal CBD Cancer
- 20% of cholangiocarcinoma.
- Prognosis better than hilar lesions.
- Progressive jaundice; CT shows no head mass but a short segment of asymmetric CBD wall thickening or abrupt luminal obliteration with abnormal enhancement in portovenous phase.
- Start with MRCP for level of obstruction, then ERCP for scrape or brush biopsy or bile cytology.
- Treatment: PD with LND.

## Duodenal Cancer
- Japan: mostly 2nd part; USA: more in 3rd and 4th parts.
- Symptoms: GI bleeding, gut obstruction, anemia, jaundice and weight loss.
- CT: mass-like thickening of the duodenal wall or heterogeneous polypoid mass with enhancement.
- Diagnosis by endoscope and biopsy.
- **Treatment**
  1. Early case → EMR
  2. PD, PPPD, with PSD as an option for the 2nd part
  3. 3rd & 4th part lesions → segmental resection possible

## Metastatic Cancer to the Pancreas
- Most common: renal cell CA; then melanoma, breast, lung, colon, gynecologic cancer.
- From the kidney often metachronous — some patients had nephrectomy more than 10 years earlier and then present with pancreatic metastasis.
- Treatment: mostly poor prognosis; an isolated pancreatic lesion → resect; the patient may live a while longer.

## Pancreatic Neuroendocrine Tumors (PNETs)
- 1% of all pancreatic tumors; part of gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NETs); slow course, especially well differentiated tumors, where histology can hardly separate benign from malignant.
- **Criteria of malignancy** (จำ) — evidence of local invasion or metastasis to node or liver (best); histopathology cannot tell — cells look like normal cells under the microscope. Features suggesting malignancy: size > 2 cm, mitotic rate > 20 per 10 HPF, necrosis or atypia, wall invasion to submucosa, Ki 67 (proliferation marker) > 20% → likely malignant behaviour.
- Symptoms usually from hormonal excess. In real life PNETs are rare, but common in the Board exam.
- When a PET is found, establish:
  1. Functioning or non-functioning PNET
  2. Benign or malignant
  3. Familial or not
  4. Part of MEN or not
- 2 main groups:
  1. **Functioning tumor** — 10-50% depending on report.
     - 1.1 May secrete several hormones, but symptoms come from one, and the tumor is named for the dominant hormone. Most common functioning PNET: insulinoma; most common PNET in MEN: gastrinoma.
     - 1.2 Can also secrete peptides & amines: serotonin, ACTH, GH, PTH and calcitonin.
  2. **Non-functioning tumor** — more common than functioning. They still secrete hormone, but not enough for symptoms; mostly present with pressure symptoms. Key: malignant > 99%, and about 60% have metastasis at diagnosis; fortunately less aggressive than exocrine cancer — so resect locoregional disease if possible; if not resectable, chemo.
- **Multidetector CT** — best initial imaging for PNETs: hypervascular lesion in arterial phase, whereas pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is hypovascular in arterial phase.
- EUS is useful for localizing tumors, especially insulinoma and gastrinoma.
- All PNETs produce **chromogranin A (CGA)** → used as tumor marker; higher CGA = higher tumor burden.
- **Classification and staging** — TNM staging often does not correlate with prognosis, making treatment choice hard; WHO recommended WHO classification 2010 based on cell differentiation, tumor grading, mitotic count, Ki 67 index.
- WHO revised again in 2017: Ki 67 for low NETs changed from < 2 to < 3, and high NETs, formerly all poorly differentiated, now include well differentiated; the poorly differentiated group = high NET carcinoma G3 small cell and large cell.

## Grades of Neuroendocrine Neoplasms – WHO 2017

| Grade | Differentiation | Mitotic count/Ki-67 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (low) - NET | Well differentiated | < 2 mitoses/10 HPF; < 3% Ki-67 index |
| 2 (intermediate) - NET | Well differentiated | 2-20 mitoses/10 HPF; 3-20% Ki-67 index |
| 3 (high) - NET | Well differentiated | > 20 mitoses/10 HPF; > 20% Ki-67 index |
| 3 (high) - Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) | Poorly differentiated | > 20 mitoses/10 HPF; > 20% Ki-67 index |

(Also Mixed Neuroendocrine Non-Neuroendocrine Neoplasm, MiNEN.)
- **Treatment principles**: complete surgical resection; even with liver spread, resect as much as possible to reduce systemic symptoms; the rest with chemoembolization, chemotherapy or targeted therapy (sunitinib). Several new drugs prolong progression and survival. Locoregional recurrence after surgery → re-resect if feasible.
- Worse prognosis with tumor > 5 cm or metastasis to node or liver — not whether it is functioning or non-functioning.

## Insulinoma
- 35-40%; most common sporadic PNET. Remember: in multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) the most common is gastrinoma.
- Rule of 10%: 10% malignancy (lowest), 10% metastasis to node or liver, 10% multiple lesions, 10% in MEN 1, 10% operated more than once. Best prognosis tumor.
- **Location**: 1/3 head, 1/3 body, 1/3 tail; most (> 80%) < 1.5 cm.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ)
  1. Neuroglycopenia — personality change, odd behaviour; severe cases confusion, seizure, coma
  2. Catecholamine effect — tremor, sweating, tachycardia
- Note (ข้อสังเกต): worse with exercise or fasting; insulinoma patients are often fat — frequent hypoglycemia makes them eat sweets constantly, and high insulin adds weight.
- **Whipple triad** — not very specific, but remember it:
  1. Hypoglycemia during fasting or exercise
  2. Serum glucose < 50 mg/dL during symptoms
  3. Symptoms relieved by glucose
- Before thinking of insulinoma, exclude the more common surreptitious or factitious insulin use, or taking diabetic pills.
- **Diagnostic tests** — to confirm the tumor really produces insulin:
  1. Insulin-glucose ratio > 0.3 — insulin very high, usually > 5 microgram/ml, with very low glucose (< 45 mg/dL) during fasting, inappropriate to the high insulin level
  2. Raised pro-insulin (from the patient's beta cells, split into insulin and C-peptide) → C-peptide > 0.7 ng/mL separates it from hypoglycemia due to injected therapeutic insulin
- **Preoperative localization**
  1. **Non invasive studies** — consider sensitivity:
     - 1.1 US 20-40% — low yield; contrast enhanced US shows hypervascular mass, but yield still low
     - 1.2 CT pancreas protocol with IV contrast — finds tumor > 82%; usually well-circumscribed (hypervascular) mass
     - 1.3 MRI — sensitivity similar to CT
     - 1.4 SRS — sensitivity only 30%, low because insulinoma has a low type of somatostatin receptor; a newer SRS, DOPA scan, is now preferred
  2. **Invasive studies** — when non-invasive studies cannot find the tumor:
     - 2.1 EUS — hypoechoic mass; now the diagnostic modality of choice; head sensitivity 90%; drawback: needs a skilled reader; low sensitivity in the tail
     - 2.2 Laparoscopy & LUS
     - 2.3 Transhepatic portal venous sampling of insulin — catheter into the PV, sampling each tributary; the vein with the highest insulin points to the hidden insulinoma; sensitivity 67-100%
     - 2.4 Selective arterial stimulation with hepatic venous sampling, or Imamura-Doppman test, or ASVS — intra-arterial stimulation: calcium into celiac, SMA, splenic artery for insulinoma (secretin for gastrinoma), then blood drawn from the hepatic vein; positive = insulin rises > 2 times baseline; sensitivity 91-100%
- **Notes** (หมายเหตุ)
  1. Tests 2.3 and 2.4 are very invasive and need experts — use for persistent or recurrent disease postop.
  2. Remember: pancreatic angiography is no longer used.
- **Steps for localization**
  1. Arterial phase CT first; +ve → explore; -ve → EUS.
  2. At surgery, palpation + IOUS in every case — best intraoperative localization (sensitivity 98%); if single best answer, choose IOUS.
- **Benefits of IOUS**
  1. Identifies almost all tumors at surgery
  2. Shows relation of tumor to main PD and blood vessels
  3. Makes surgery safer
- **Management of Insulinoma** — before surgery beware hypoglycemia; may need oral diazoxide 400-600 mg to inhibit insulin release.
  1. Midline or bilateral subcostal incision; usually benign single lesion, so MIS experts may use laparoscope with LUS. Appearance: small reddish-brown mass like a cherry.
  2. Tumor < 2 cm, not on the main PD, > 2 mm from the main PD → enucleation (จำ).
  3. Tumor on the main PD → resect regardless of size.
  4. > 2 cm in body & tail → distal pancreatectomy.
  5. Large, deep in head or uncinate process → PD or PPPD.
  6. Metastasis found at surgery → debulking, remove as much as possible.
  7. Tumor still not found despite everything:
     - 7.1 Blind subtotal pancreatectomy is no longer used for insulinoma; it is still used for nesidioblastosis (beta cell hyperplasia), seen in both children and adults
     - 7.2 Biopsy the tail to exclude adult nesidioblastosis, close, then PO. ASVS
  8. Unresectable insulinoma:
     - 8.1 Prevent hypoglycemia with frequent feeding, carbohydrate rich diet and carrying sweets at all times
     - 8.2 Drugs: diazoxide, propanolol, varapamil, chropomazine (as printed) and octreotide; octreotide cuts insulin secretion by 30%, use when somatostatin scan positive
     - 8.3 Chemotherapy: streptozotocin, dacarbazine, doxorubicin, 5FU
- **Drug roles worth remembering**
  1. **Diazoxide** — blocks beta cells, reducing insulin secretion; dose 150-800 microgram/day (as printed); effective 60-70%. Formerly only for unresectable cases; now also for preoperative preparation.
  2. **Steroid** — raises blood glucose, but reduces peripheral glucose uptake so cells cannot use glucose → symptoms worse. So no steroid in insulinoma (จำ).
- **Insulinoma in MEN 1** — usually multiple; local enucleation of every nodule is hard and nearly always recurs. After much debate, Orlo H. Clark recommends subtotal pancreatectomy + enucleation in the head, with IOUS.

## Gastrinoma
- **Sporadic** 75%, mean age about 44. Beware "most gastrinoma is sporadic" is false (จำ) — "most" means almost all (as printed).
- 25% of gastrinoma with MEN 1, mean age about 33.
- "50% disease" (roughly): 50% malignancy (some say up to 90%), 50% metastasis, 50% multiple.
- High chance of malignancy — think cancer first when a case is found.
- **Location**: 70-90% in Passaro or gastrinoma triangle; often in the duodenal wall > 60%; pancreatic head 50%.
- Figure: Gastrinoma triangle — line joining the confluence of the cystic and common bile ducts (superiorly), the junction of the second and third portion of the duodenum (inferiorly), and the junction of the neck and body of the pancreas (medially).
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): ulcerogenic syndrome — peptic ulcer 90%, diarrhea 50%, GERD 50%.
- Suspect gastrinoma when peptic ulcer is:
  1. Multiple
  2. In unusual locations, e.g. jejunum
  3. Recurrent after medical or surgical treatment
  4. With severe esophagitis
  5. Without H. pylori or NSAID history
- In patients without peptic ulcer symptoms, suspect gastrinoma with:
  1. Family history of MEN 1
  2. Family history of peptic ulcer
  3. Unexplained diarrhea — hyperacidity inflames the small bowel
  4. Unexplained steatorrhea
  5. Hypercalcemia
  6. Upper endoscopy for epigastric pain showing prominent gastric or duodenal rugal folds
- **Diagnostic tests**
  1. **Fasting serum gastrin** — stop PPI at least 72 hours before the blood test to reduce false positives; some institutions stop PPI 1 week, since PPI can raise serum gastrin up to 5 times. While off PPI, give high dose H2 blocker instead — short half life, so it can be stopped only 48 hours before the test.
     - 1.1 > 1000 pg/ml → likely gastrinoma, but seen in no more than 30% of patients.
     - 1.2 Equivocal (somewhat high) → secretin stimulation test: IV secretin 0.4 mg/kg (as printed), measure serum gastrin at 0, 2, 5, 10, 15 and 30 minutes. Formerly +ve = rise of 200 pg/mL above baseline; now a rise > 110 pg/mL is +ve; rise < 110 → not gastrinoma (see Shackelford 2019 guideline below).
  2. **Basal acid output**
     - 2.1 > 15 mEq/hr — no previous ulcer surgery
     - 2.2 > 5 mEq/hr — previous peptic ulcer surgery
  3. Do not forget other causes of hypergastrinemia, which usually have only modestly raised serum gastrin:
     - 3.1 Retained gastric antrum
     - 3.2 G-cell hyperplasia
     - 3.3 Gastric outlet obstruction
     - 3.4 Hypochlorhydria or achlorhydria
     - 3.5 Renal insufficiency, rheumatoid arthritis, vitilligo, pheochromocytoma, DM
- **Localization**
  1. **Preoperative localization**
     - 1.1 Initial: multiphasic CT
     - 1.2 Gastrinoma is somatostatin receptor +ve: radio-labelled octreotide shows a hot spot; detects tumor up to 90% → single best = SRS (SRS finds tumor in the Passaro triangle)
     - 1.3 SRS combined with EUS is the best localization, finding > 90%; but EUS is limited for tumors < 1 cm or in the duodenum, and its accuracy is now questioned — so US centers favour SRS + SPECT (printed "simple proton emission CT"), +ve showing a clear hot spot
     - 1.4 Upper endoscope in every case — tumors are often in the duodenum
  2. **Intraoperative localization**
     - 2.1 Palpation + IOUS
     - 2.2 Intraoperative upper endoscopy
     - 2.3 Duodenotomy
- **Management**
  1. Before treatment rule out MEN1 — serum calcium, phosphorus, PTH, prolactin; if MEN 1, treat HPT first.
  2. Rule of thumb: even if gastrinoma has spread to liver or lung, try to remove both the 1° and the metastases — so calling liver metastasis inoperable is wrong. Exception: massive metastasis, e.g. carcinomatosis filling the abdomen found before surgery — do not put the patient through surgery; give chemotherapy plus long term PPI until death.
  2. (numbered 2 again, as printed) Even when preoperative studies cannot localize the tumor, Sabiston 2017 p.954 advises exploring every case — palpation with IOUS and opening the duodenum usually finds it.
  3. Do not forget: initial treatment before surgery is PPI; a disease that does not respond to PPI is not this.
  4. At surgery: bimanual palpation with wide Kocherization of head and uncinate, mobilize body and tail for palpation + IOUS; if not found → intraoperative transillumination upper endoscopy; strongly recommended: routine duodenotomy at the 2nd part to inspect mucosa — tumors found here up to 60%; also sample lymph nodes in the gastrinoma triangle.
     - Figure caption: localization of duodenal gastrinomas by upper endoscopy, intraoperative endoscopic transillumination, or duodenotomy with direct palpation of the duodenal wall; duodenotomy should be opened longitudinally and closed transversely.
  5. Well capsulated, size < 2 cm, not on the main PD → enucleation acceptable.
  6. Well capsulated, size < 2 cm in the duodenal wall → full thickness resection/enucleation with periduodenal node dissection.
  7. Large > 2 cm, deep, on the main PD:
     - 7.1 Head or uncinate process → PD or PPPD
     - 7.2 Body & tail → distal pancreatectomy
  8. Liver lesions found → remove as much as possible; if not removable, or the tumor cannot be found → before closing do HSV to reduce gastric acid. Remember: gastric resection is a poor choice.
  9. Postoperative octreotide reduces symptoms but not tumor size (จำ).
  10. If everything fails to find the tumor → no blind pancreatectomy.
- **ZE syndrome** — leading experts, notably Jeffrey A. Norton (Cameron 12th ed) and Christopher E. Ellison (Shackelford 8th ed); summary:
  1. In skilled hands, surgical cure rate in sporadic cases up to 60%; 5 year survival 40%, 10 year survival 34%.
  2. Postoperative cure means:
     - 2.1 Normal fasting serum gastrin
     - 2.2 -ve SRS
     - 2.3 -ve CT
     - 2.4 -ve secretin stimulation test
  3. Surgery for sporadic cases: duodenotomy in every case — detects more tumors and improves cure rate.
  4. Liver metastasis is a key prognostic factor; pancreatic gastrinoma spreads to liver more often than duodenal gastrinoma, so overall prognosis of pancreatic is worse than duodenal gastrinoma.
  5. **Gastrinoma in MEN 1** — whether to operate is debated; the cautious say these are multiple and widespread, rarely fully removable, so most favour PPI plus chemotherapy. Leading experts recommend:
     - 5.1 Imaging -ve → do not operate; buy time with PPI and EGD yearly
     - 5.2 Imaging +ve, not yet spread to liver or bone → explore

## Summary guideline: Management of gastrinoma (Shackelford 2019)
Figure: algorithm, as steps —
1. High clinical suspicion (established low gastric pH) → screen with fasting serum gastrin (off PPIs for 72 h, ideally for 7 days).
   - Elevated → secretin stimulation test.
   - Equivocal → repeat serum gastrin a total of 3 times, calculate mean → not elevated → not gastrinoma; otherwise → secretin stimulation test.
2. Secretin stimulation test: gastrin 110 pg/mL (as printed) → gastrinoma. No response → consider calcium stimulation; otherwise not gastrinoma.
3. Gastrinoma → 1st priority: initiate antacid regimen (PPI, 40 mg bid); screen for MEN1 (serum calcium).
4. Localization studies: CT of abdomen and pelvis, triphasic, fine cuts through the pancreas; dual-phase MRI of abdomen (liver disease); octreotide scan (somatostatin receptor scintigraphy). Consider EUS; angiography with selective intraarterial secretin injection and gastrin measurement. 20%-30% will not localize — consider exploration.
5. Sporadic, confined disease: duodenal disease → enucleate; pancreatic head < 2 cm → enucleate, > 2 cm or duct involved → PD; body/tail → distal pancreatectomy & splenectomy.
6. MEN1 or metastatic disease: MEN1 → parathyroidectomy; negative localization → observe; positive localization → head < 2 cm enucleate, > 2 cm PD; body/tail → distal pancreatectomy and splenectomy. Metastatic disease to liver: unilobar → consider resection; bilobar → consider TACE vs RFA/MWA; streptozocin-based regimen/clinical trial.
- **Prognosis**
  1. Depends on liver metastasis, not lymph nodes
  2. With liver metastasis, 5 year survival only 20%
  3. Without liver metastasis, 5 year survival up to 80%

## Vipoma
- Also called Verner Morrison syndrome, WDHA (watery diarrhea hypokalemia achlorhydria), pancreatic cholera.
- Classic symptom: chronic watery diarrhea, tea-coloured, > 5L/min (as printed), not bloody, no mucus.
  - Diarrhea from gastrinoma improves with PPI; VIPoma diarrhea does not.
- **Location**: likes body & tail; often already metastatic at diagnosis.
- **Diagnostic tests**
  1. Electrolytes: hypo K+, achlorhydria, metabolic acidosis from fecal bicarbonate loss — caution (ข้อควรระวัง): acidosis, not alkalosis
  2. Serum VIP > 200 pg/mL
- **Differential diagnosis** of the diarrhea:
  1. Villous adenoma — mucus diarrhea & hypo K+
  2. ZE — diarrhea with peptic ulcer
  3. Carcinoid — diarrhea with facial flushing
  4. UC — bloody diarrhea
- **Treatment** (การรักษา)
  1. Hydration & correct fluid electrolyte
  2. Octreotide or lanreotide to reduce diarrhea — best palliation
  3. 5FU + interferon alpha — reported marked clinical improvement in inoperable cases
  4. Surgical resection, or debulking if possible — better than nothing

## Glucagonoma
- **Symptoms** — 5D: DM, Dermatitis, Dementia, Diarrhea, DVT.
  - Typical: skin rash called necrolytic migratory erythema, 70%; very itchy, commonly at the groin.
- Location: usually body & tail; at diagnosis usually > 5 cm and already metastatic.
- **Diagnostic tests**
  1. Hyperglycemia
  2. Hypoproteinemia
  3. Serum glucagon > 1,000 pg/ml
  4. Skin lesion biopsy: vacuolated keratinocytes in the upper epidermis
- **Treatment** (การรักษา)
  1. Control DM
  2. TPN — patients are usually very wasted
  3. Octreotide to reduce hormone output
  4. DVT prophylaxis
  5. Preoperative vaccination — splenectomy is almost always needed
  6. Surgery: distal pancreatectomy + splenectomy; if impossible because the tumor is very large, debulk as much as possible

## Somatostatinoma
- Very rare; hard to diagnose; found when large.
- **Symptoms** from high somatostatin:
  1. GS from GB stasis
  2. Steatorrhea from reduced exocrine function; wasting
  3. DM from glucose intolerance
- **Diagnostic test**: serum somatostatin > 50 times normal (> 14 pmol/L).
- **Treatment**: surgical resection or debulking; do not forget to add cholecystectomy.

## Summary of Functioning PNETs to remember (บทสรุปของ Functioning PNETs ที่ควรจำ)
1. **Insulinoma**
   - 1.1 10% disease
   - 1.2 1/3 each in head, body, tail
   - 1.3 Hypoglycemic symptoms
2. **Gastrinoma**
   - 2.1 50-90% malignant
   - 2.2 In gastrinoma triangle, especially duodenum
   - 2.3 Abdominal pain, peptic ulcer, diarrhea
3. **VIPoma**
   - 3.1 50% malignant
   - 3.2 Body & tail
   - 3.3 Tea coloured watery diarrhea + hypo K+
4. **Glucagonoma**
   - 4.1 70% malignant
   - 4.2 Body & tail
   - 4.3 Classic skin rash
5. **Somatostatinoma**
   - 5.1 50-90% malignant
   - 5.2 Head & neck
   - 5.3 GS
6. **Splenectomy** — for those with high malignancy chance, mostly in body & tail: VIPoma and glucagonoma.
7. **Cholecystectomy** — consider where recurrence is likely and lifelong postop octreotide is needed, e.g. VIPoma and glucagonoma (printed "Glucogonoma").

## Up to Date in PNETs
- Besides WHO classification 2017, newer points still awaiting answers:
  1. Small non-functioning PNETs ≤ 1 cm: observation with follow-up is gaining support
  2. Non-functioning PNETs 1-2 cm: undecided; if fit, probably operate
  3. Non-functioning PNETs > 2 cm: operate in every case
  4. Laparoscopic surgery: current short term outcome as good as open surgery
  5. Liver spread: still recommended to operate if > 90% of tumor can be removed

## Pancreatic Cystic Neoplasms (PCNs)
- PCNs are only 1% of pancreatic tumors, but a hot topic at HBP meetings — beware: rare diseases like this tend to come up in the Board exam, whether diagnosis, film interpretation, or especially the best treatment.
- With US and CT used for check-ups or abdominal pain, incidental PCNs are found more often — such cases are a headache to decide what to do.
- **Classifications of pancreatic cystic lesions** — 2 groups, non-neoplastic and neoplastic (printed "neoplastic cyst กับ neoplastic cyst"):

| Group | Lesions |
|---|---|
| Non-neoplastic cysts | Pseudocyst; simple or congenital cyst; retention cyst |
| Neoplastic cysts (PCNs) — mucinous cystic lesions | Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN); mucinous cystic neoplasm (MCN) |
| Neoplastic cysts — non-mucinous cystic neoplastic lesions | Serous cystic neoplasm (SCN); solid-pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN); cystic neuroendocrine neoplasm; acinar-cell cystic neoplasm |
| Other neoplastic lesions | Ductal adenocarcinoma with cystic degeneration |

- PCNs are more common in women than men — except IPMN, men > women. Always think of PCN with a pancreatic cyst and no previous pancreatitis. Most PCNs are asymptomatic (40-75%); if symptomatic, usually mild nagging pain.
- Before treating PCNs, ask 3 questions:
  1. Is the cyst on US or CT really pancreatic? Masses near the lesser sac can arise from other organs.
  2. If pancreatic, pseudocyst or neoplastic cyst? Pseudocysts are 80-90%; PCNs only 10-20%.
  3. If neoplastic, which type, how likely malignant, how to treat — observe, or must every case be operated?
- **Pseudocyst** — usually men, drinkers, or history of pancreatitis or trauma to the pancreas; high blood amylase; CT shows a single large cyst (unilocular) with features of pancreatitis; cyst fluid clear or brownish with high amylase but no raised tumor markers. Key: PCNs stay the same or grow, whereas pseudocysts can shrink.

## Serous Cystic Neoplasm (SCN)
- Benign lesion with true epithelium lined by glycogen-rich clear cuboidal cells; PAS +ve. Usually women aged 50-60, head lesion, asymptomatic.
- Often found incidentally on CT.
- SCN has 4 subtypes — the author omits details (not helpful).
- 17% of SCN occur with von Hippel Lindau syndrome (VHL), one subtype; differs from sporadic SCN only in being multifocal; behaviour like ordinary SCN.
- Serous cystadenocarcinoma, a very rare subtype of SCN.
- **Imaging study**: CT. Classic SCN — clusters of small microcysts, 2 mm - 2 cm, more than 6 cysts, like a honeycomb (**honeycomb appearance**), sponge or bunch of grapes; no PD communication.
  - Another classic CT feature: **central starburst calcification** in the middle of the mass, like a central scar or radial/stellate appearance.
  1. SCN fluid is clear, colourless, not viscous like MCN and IPMN — no mucin; amylase and all tumor markers normal.
  2. Cytology: cuboidal cells with glycogen rich cells (จำ).
- **Treatment approach** — SCN is 99.99% benign; malignancy is very bad luck; so 2 approaches:
  1. No surgery — observe and follow up periodically
  2. Surgery when indicated
- Before treating PCNs, keep in mind:
  1. Risk & benefit ratio — weigh well before operating
  2. Life expectancy — very old patients with little time left: leave them be
  3. Comorbidities — many diseases, poor outlook after surgery; find a way out, buy time, delay (as printed)
  4. Malignant potential — very low → follow up; high → operate
  5. Location — body & tail is easy; head needs Whipple, think hard (as printed, the author jokes about his age, and suggests telling patients equipment is not ready and referring)
  6. Hospital mortality & morbidity and the surgeon's skill — if 10 operated cases yield 11 problems, consider changing career (as printed, a joke)
- In tertiary centers with many experts and low mortality & morbidity, the trend is aggressive: even asymptomatic SCN in body & tail is increasingly operated, because left alone it may grow or become symptomatic. Data on natural history: some SCN grow 0.5 cm/yr, and tumors 4 cm may grow up to 2 cm/yr.
- The author, like most, holds the standard that SCN is not operated — but non-operation requires meeting the criteria.
- **Criteria for nonoperative approach** (SCN):
  1. No symptoms from the mass — abdominal pain, fullness
  2. Very old, many diseases, quota of chest pain — should not operate (as printed)
  3. Size < 4 cm when found
  4. Follow up: not growing, or growing very slowly
  5. CT, EUS+FNA (optional) show benign
- If not operated: FU with CT or MRI every 6 months for the first 2 years, then yearly; if symptoms appear or growth is rapid during FU → operate.
- **Criteria for operating on SCN**
  1. Symptomatic
  2. At diagnosis, mass ≥ 4 cm (จำ) — tends to grow quickly and become symptomatic
  3. Growth of 1 cm in 6 months, or 2 cm in 1 year
  4. Good risk patient, body & tail lesion, skilled surgeon and hospital
  5. Cannot exclude MCN or IPMN
- **Surgical treatment of SCN**
  1. Principle: complete anatomical resection
  2. Head lesion → PPPD
  3. Body & tail → distal pancreatectomy only; splenectomy not needed since mostly benign
  4. Neck lesion → PPPD would be too much and distal pancreatectomy loses too much pancreas; special option: segmental resection, closing the proximal side and inserting jejunum onto the distal pancreas = central or middle pancreatectomy
- **Things not to do** (as printed, will cause trouble):
  1. Enucleation — the mass is fairly large and often on the main pancreatic duct; 40% develop pancreatic fistula
  2. Cystogastrostomy or cystojejunostomy
  3. Percutaneous drainage
  4. Sclerosing agent injection
  5. Adding splenectomy

## Mucinous Cystic Neoplasm (MCN)
- In the USA thought to be the most common PCN; in Asia IPMN is more common.
- Younger than SCN: MCN usually in middle-aged women 45-50; mass in body & tail; mostly found incidentally on CT, like SCN.
- WHO divides MCN into 3 groups by degree of cellular atypia: low, intermediate, high grade dysplasia. About 20% of MCN are associated with invasive cancer → MCN is considered a precursor of pancreatic cancer; malignancy risk 5-15%.
- **CT**
  1. Cyst > 2 cm (macrocyst), thick wall, septa dividing the cyst into compartments; nodule or solid component may be seen.
  2. Classic: calcification around the rim = peripheral or rim calcification, like an eggshell (peripheral egg shell calcification), unlike SCN's central calcification.
  3. No PD communication — distinguishes from IPMN.
- **Cyst fluid** (from EUS+FNA)
  1. Clear but viscous from mucus; feels like thick oil, like a farang's sputum (as printed) — called string sign
  2. Mucin stain +ve
  3. Cytology: mucinous cells; histology: tall columnar cells with ovarian stroma
  4. Amylase low, but CEA, CA 15-3 high
- **Treatment** (การรักษา) — little debate: if fit, no hesitation — operate.
  1. Most MCN are in body & tail with high malignancy chance → distal pancreatectomy; trend now laparoscopic
  2. Head → PD or PPPD
  3. Neck → central pancreatectomy
- Current debates:
  1. MCN < 3 cm — resection or surveillance? Currently leaning toward resection.
  2. Splenectomy + LND — some do it only when intraop F/S shows invasive cancer; if benign, no splenectomy and no node clearance.

## Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm (IPMN)
- The most complicated papillary mucin-producing tumor — much debate and controversy, behaviour and aggressiveness vary so much that international IPMN meetings had to be held; take note.
- What follows is the author's own view, chosen from what he read that did not seem wrong.
- IPMN: proliferation of mucinous cells in the pancreatic duct forming papillary stroma, producing thick, sticky mucin that blocks the duct → recurrent pancreatitis, then chronic pancreatitis; patients often have pain, weight loss from exocrine insufficiency, and later diabetes.
- Unlike the others, IPMN is more common in men, age > 60, mostly in the head.
- Cancer risk like MCN; IPMN may also occur with cancers of other organs, e.g. CRC, stomach, lung, pancreas.
- **Classification** (HBP groups love it):
  1. By gross or anatomical classification of IPMN (used since 2006):
     - 1.1 Main duct (MD-IPMN)
     - 1.2 Branch duct (BD-IPMN)
     - 1.3 Combined or mixed type IPMN
     - Formerly MD-IPMN was thought more common than BD-IPMN, but the classic 2012 review (Tanaka 2012) found BD-IPMN more common.
  2. By histology, WHO 2010:
     - 2.1 Low or intermediate dysplasia
     - 2.2 High grade dysplasia
     - Revised 2012: high grade dysplasia = carcinoma in situ or intramucosal carcinoma; cancer = invasive carcinoma of IPMN.
  3. By histopathological subtype on immunohistochemistry (IHC), 4 subtypes:
     - 3.1 **Gastric type** — most common (49-63%); not very aggressive; progression to invasive cancer 10-30%; BD-IPMN > MD-IPMN; cells resemble gastric cells
     - 3.2 **Intestinal type** — 2nd (18-36%); aggressive; progression to invasive cancer 30-50%; MD-IPMN in the head, rarely involves the whole main duct; cells resemble intestinal cells
     - 3.3 **Pancreatobiliary** — 3rd (7-18%); most aggressive; progression to invasive cancer > 50%; MD-IPMN in the head, often involving the whole main duct; cells resemble pancreatic and bile duct cells
     - 3.4 **Oncocytic** — rare, 4th (1-8%); limited progression to invasive cancer, at most 30%; MD-IPMN in the head
- IPMN differs from MCN: IPMN has no ovarian-like stroma; MCN does.
- **Diagnosis**: history, symptoms, physical exam, non-invasive imaging and endoscopy.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ)
  1. Asymptomatic 60%
  2. Acute, chronic or relapsing pancreatitis (back pain) 40% — SCN and MCN rarely cause pancreatitis; jaundice fairly rare
  3. Weight loss 40%, anorexia
  4. DM 20-30%; steatorrhea uncommon
- **CT**: both micro- and macrocysts, but no thick capsule like MCN.
  - MD-IPMN: main pancreatic duct > 5 mm; sharp-eyed surgeons may see filling defect or mural nodule in the duct.
  - BD IPMN: cysts > 5 mm clustered like grapes, with duct communication; main pancreatic duct usually normal.
  - Combined or mixed type IPMN: both.
- **EUS + FNA** — tissue plus fluid:
  1. Mucin +ve and mucinous cells, like MCN fluid
  2. With pancreatitis, high fluid amylase
  3. Tumor markers high like MCN, except CA 15-3 lower than MCN
- Doppler EUS preferred — shows blood supply of a nodule.
- **ERCP** — MD-IPMN classic endoscopic signs (side-view):
  1. Bulging ampulla and fish mouth pancreatic duct opening
  2. Mucin extrusion from the ampulla
  3. Contrast shows dilated pancreatic duct (pancreatogram: dilated pancreatic duct and side branches with intraluminal filling defects)
- **MRI/MRCP** — MD-IPMN: main PD often > 1 cm with filling defects in the duct. BD IPMN: normal pancreatic duct, grape-like cysts at branch ducts with PD communication, main PD normal size.
- **PET scan** — selective cases.
- **Treatment** (การรักษา) — distinguish MD vs BD-IPMN. MD-IPMN should be resected (very high malignancy chance); BD has lower malignancy chance, so non-operative is an option. Current treatment follows the IAP consensus 2017 guideline by Tanaka and Fernandez-del Castillo (latest revision, 2017). A newer US group, the American GI association (AGA), issued a 2018 guideline but lacks the standing of Tanaka and Fernandez-del Castillo (as printed).
- Reports of BD-IPMN not operated, treated by endoscopic cyst ablation with alcohol plus paclitaxel — results poor, not recommended. Not for MD-IPMN — causes pancreatitis.

## International Consensus Guideline 2017 for BD-IPMN
- Revised at Fukuoka 2017 from the 2012 consensus, with small changes.
- Basics: MD-IPMN and mixed type IPMN have malignancy chance 67% and 45% respectively, so resect both types.
- BD-IPMN malignancy about 20% (less than main duct). Rushing every patient to surgery is unwise — patients are often old with many risks; if operating, first get imaging: pancreatic protocol CT or GD enhanced MRI/MRCP — size and lesion features, mural nodule or not — then judge whether CT shows high risk stigmata (likely malignant) or only worrisome features (Fukuoka guideline 2017).
- Figure: Fukuoka 2017 BD-IPMN algorithm, as steps —
  1. Any "high-risk stigmata"? (i) obstructive jaundice with cystic lesion of the pancreatic head, (ii) enhancing mural nodule ≥ 5 mm, (iii) main pancreatic duct ≥ 10 mm. Yes → consider surgery if clinically appropriate. No → step 2.
  2. Any "worrisome features"? Clinical: pancreatitis. Imaging: (i) cyst ≥ 3 cm, (ii) enhancing mural nodule < 5 mm, (iii) thickened/enhancing cyst walls, (iv) main duct 5-9 mm, (v) abrupt change in caliber of pancreatic duct with distal pancreatic atrophy, (vi) lymphadenopathy, (vii) increased serum CA19-9, (viii) cyst growth rate ≥ 5 mm / 2 years.
     - Yes → EUS: (i) definite mural nodule(s) ≥ 5 mm, (ii) main duct features suspicious for involvement, (iii) cytology suspicious or positive for malignancy? Yes → consider surgery. No or inconclusive → step 3.
     - No → step 3.
  3. Size of largest cyst:
     - < 1 cm → CT/MRI in 6 months, then every 2 years if no change
     - 1-2 cm → CT/MRI 6 months × 1 year, yearly × 2 years, then lengthen interval up to 2 years if no change
     - 2-3 cm → EUS in 3-6 months, then lengthen interval up to 1 year, alternating MRI with EUS as appropriate; consider surgery in young, fit patients needing prolonged surveillance
     - > 3 cm → close surveillance alternating MRI with EUS every 3-6 months; strongly consider surgery in young, fit patients
- Explained in text:
  1. **High risk stigmata** (HRS, Fukuoka 2017), likely malignant:
     - 1.1 Obstructive jaundice from a mass in the pancreatic head
     - 1.2 Enhancing mural nodule > 5 mm in the cyst (2012 did not state nodule size)
     - 1.3 Main PD size ≥ 1 cm
     - Treatment: surgical resection with free margin — head lesion PD, body & tail distal pancreatectomy.
  2. No HRS → look closer for **worrisome features** (WF):
     - 2.1 Cystic tumor size ≥ 3 cm
     - 2.2 Thickened enhancing cyst wall
     - 2.3 Main PD 5-9 mm
     - 2.4 Enhancing mural nodule < 5mm in the cyst (2012 gave no size)
     - 2.5 Abrupt change in caliber of PD with distal pancreatic atrophy
     - 2.6 Lymphadenopathy
     - 2.7 Clinical pancreatitis
     - 2.8 New: raised serum CEA (level not stated by the authors)
     - 2.9 New: growth rate > 5 mm per 2 years
     - With worrisome features → Doppler EUS to see nodule blood supply, with FNA; accuracy > 95%.
  3. On EUS, any one of these → operate:
     - 3.1 Definite mural nodule ≥ 5 mm
     - 3.2 Main PD involvement or suspicion, e.g. thick cyst wall, nodule or mucin in the duct
     - 3.3 FNA of cyst wall shows malignant or suspicious cells
     - Note — EUS features of a mural nodule: 1) does not move with position change; 2) Doppler flow in the nodule; 3) FNA yields tumor.
  4. If EUS of BD-IPMN lacks item 3 features → treat by size of the largest BD-IPMN cyst:
     - 4.1 < 1 cm → CT or MRI at 6 months; if unchanged, repeat CT/MRI every 2 years
     - 4.2 1-2 cm (favourite exam topic) → CT or MRI every 6 months for the first year; if unchanged, yearly for 2 years; if still unchanged, CT/MRI every 2 years
     - 4.3 2-3 cm → 2 options:
       - 4.3.1 Elderly (> 65) → EUS with MRI every 3-6 months for 1 year; if changed, consider surgery
       - 4.3.2 Young and fit, not wanting long follow-up → consider surgery
     - 4.4 > 3 cm → 2 options:
       - 4.4.1 Elderly → surveillance with MRI plus EUS every 3-6 months indefinitely; if changed, consider surgery
       - 4.4.2 Young → strongly consider surgery over long term follow up
  5. If EUS of BD-IPMN lacks item 3 features but is inconclusive → treat as 4 / 1.4 (as printed).
- **Surgical principle** for both BD and MD: R0 resection with lymphadenectomy — head lesion PD, body or tail distal pancreatectomy.
- The headache is MD IPMN surgery: the cancer tends to spread along the duct rather than radially; with diffuse type and dilated main duct along its length, too little resection risks recurrence, too much (overtreatment) causes exocrine and endocrine insufficiency.
- **Free margin problem** of MD-IPMN, always a headache:
  1. Where are the proximal and distal free margins — especially in MD type, which can also have skip lesions in the duct
  2. The duct is full of mucin hiding mural nodules, so nodules in the duct are hard to see, whether by thin-cut CT or ERCP
- **Surgical technique for MD-IPMN** — complex; decisions are often made in the OR: what to do and extent of resection:
  1. Review the preop EUS for anything in the duct. IOUS, which many like, has not been shown to add information beyond EUS — seems a waste of time.
  2. **Frozen section of transected margin** — shave pancreas about 3-4 cm at a time, keep cutting until margin -ve. On this, Blumgart seems more enthusiastic than others. Tanaka's approach after frozen section:
     - 2.1 Adenoma → Rx: enough, no further resection
     - 2.2 Low to moderate (intermediate) grade dysplasia → Rx: still debated whether to resect further
     - 2.3 High grade dysplasia or invasive cancer → Rx: resect until free margin
  3. **Intraoperative pancreatoscopy** — scope into the duct to see what remains in the main PD; tumor looks like fish eggs (fish egg like appearance). Pancreatoscopy views: normal dilated pancreatic duct; dilated duct with cobblestoning and nodularity; dilated duct with papillary projections and adherent mucin.
  4. **Intraductal US (IDUS)** — if available use it; not essential.
- Some surgeons do all of 1-4 and are still unsure of R0 — the decision depends on the surgeon's experience; debates in the OR whether to resect more. About 15% of MD IPMN are diffuse type needing total pancreatectomy for multiple skip lesions. Before total pancreatectomy assess the patient's condition — these are often frail elderly; can they tolerate brittle DM or exocrine insufficiency?
  - Experts advise informing the patient before OR that total pancreatectomy may be needed.
- **Postoperative surveillance**
  1. Low grade and moderate or intermediate grade dysplasia: check margin status; if negative, CT at 2 and 5 years; if margin +ve, MRCP every 6 months
  2. High grade dysplasia: MRCP or CT every 3-6 months
  3. Invasive cancer: CT every 3 months for the first 2 years, then every 6 months

## Solid Pseudopapillary Neoplasm (SPN)
- Also called solid cystic, Franz tumor, Hamoudi tumor; very rare, < 4% of PCNs, but may be examined if unlucky.
- **Key point**: women aged about 25-35; mass in head, body and tail about equally; mostly asymptomatic.
- **Gross & CT**: large multiloculated mass, heterogeneous peripheral enhancing lesion, cyst alternating with solid component, capsule, fairly well-defined margin; some have internal or central calcification. Many experts operate on sight.
- **EUS + FNA** — increasingly popular; aspirate is necrotic tissue, low CEA and amylase; histology shows papillary elements.
- Once SPN is diagnosed, operate: 85% benign, but 15% low grade malignant and 10-15% already metastatic.
- **Treatment**: complete resection like other PCNs; recurrence after surgery very rare; if spread, metastasectomy still gives good survival.

## Summary for PCNs (สรุปสำหรับ PCNs)
- Suspect PCN when the mass:
  1. Has no history of trauma or pancreatitis
  2. Normal serum amylase
  3. ERCP: no pancreatic duct communication as in pseudocyst
  4. CT: calcification in the cyst; calcification in the wall means high malignancy chance; internal septation or solid component; hypervascularity with IV contrast
  5. Recurs when treated as pseudocyst
  6. Intraoperative finding: PCNs have normal surrounding pancreas; pseudocyst has thick wall and heavy adhesion to nearby organs
- **SCN** — common in women, benign; CT honeycomb of small cysts < 2 cm; central stellate scar is diagnostic. < 4 cm and asymptomatic → observe.
- **MCN** — women, body & tail, cysts > 2 cm; high malignancy chance → operate.
- **IPMN** — elderly men, head mass, PCN arising from the duct; high malignancy chance especially main duct type; branch duct type → approach per guideline 2012 (as printed).
- **SPN** — young women; once diagnosed, operate every case.

| | Age - gender | Imaging |
|---|---|---|
| SCN — benign | 75% women, 60-70 y ("Grandma") | Lobulated microcystic; 18% central scar with Ca++ |
| MCN — malignant potential | 99% women, 40-50 y ("Mother") | Macrocystic; usually 1 cyst; 25% peripheral Ca++; 95% in tail and body |
| Main-duct IPMN — malignant potential | M = W, 60-80 y | Dilated pancreatic duct; protruding papilla of Vater |
| Side-branch IPMN — malignant potential | M = W, 60-80 y | Bunch of grapes; connection to PD |

## Metastatic Cancer
- < 1%; most from renal cell carcinoma, then melanoma, breast, lung, colon and gynecologic cancers.
- CT: hypervascular mass, unlike primary adenocarcinoma, which is hypovascular.
- Definitive diagnosis: EUS-guided FNA.
- Treatment: most patients have advanced primary cancer, so only chemo; exception — lucky cases with RCC spread only to the pancreas without systemic burden: pancreatic resection prolongs survival.

## Pancreatic Incidentaloma
- Pancreatic mass found unexpectedly on imaging, without symptoms. Problem: a solid incidental lesion has 30-50% malignancy chance, more than cystic lesions; if malignant, either metastasis or primary — mainly adenocarcinoma and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNET), less often solid pseudopapillary tumor.
- Our job: decide if it is malignant, how to investigate, whether to biopsy, how to treat. Quiet advice to newly graduated, inexperienced surgeons without expert radiologists and cytologists: refer such hard cases.
- Two types: solid and cystic pancreatic incidentaloma.

## Solid Pancreatic Incidentaloma
- Think of pancreatic endocrine tumor and adenocarcinoma; solid lesions are malignant in nearly 90%, cystic about 10% on average.
- First step: CT pancreas protocol, 3 phases:
  1. Arterial phase — about first 25 seconds
  2. Pancreatic phase — 25-60 seconds
  3. Portovenous phase — 60-90 seconds
- CT suggests cancer and patient good risk → treat as cancer: oncologic resection.
- Poor risk patient or likely metastasis to pancreas → EUS & FNA for tissue diagnosis to plan treatment:
  1. Pancreatic cancer or metastasis → palliative treatment only
  2. Benign → observe and follow up with CT every 3-6 months

## Cystic Pancreatic Incidentaloma
- First separate pseudocyst (about 90%) from cystic tumor (≤ 10%).
- Cystic tumors: serous, mucinous, IPMN or other rarer types.
- ACR guideline (J Am Coll Radiol. 2010), often cited by US pancreatic experts: cyst < 2 cm has low malignancy chance → follow-up imaging at 1 year; 2-3 cm asymptomatic → imaging every 6 months. For cyst imaging, MRI is preferred over CT — better for septa, nodules, duct communication.
- Figure: Incidental pancreatic cyst, as steps —
  1. < 2 cm → 1 yr follow up → stable → benign, no further FU; growth → manage as 2-3 cm.
  2. 2-3 cm → characterization, preferably MRI/MRCP → undetermined: yearly FU; BD-IPMT (as printed): follow up every 6 months for 2 years; serous cystadenoma: follow up every 2 years.
  3. > 3 cm → serous cystadenoma: consider resection when > 4 cm; uncharacterized mass or other cystic neoplasm → cyst aspiration → resect, depending on co-morbidity and risk.

## Complications of Pancreatectomy
- Mortality of pancreatic surgery has fallen greatly, but morbidity has hardly fallen; complications give surgeons headaches.
- Focus on common ones often questioned at conferences: postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF), postpancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH), delayed gastric emptying (DGE).
- **Anastomotic stricture** — less common, usually a late complication, months to years after surgery; at 5 years stricture ≤ 20%:
  1. **Biliary stricture** — jaundice, cholangitis, abnormal LFT; treatment: PTBD then dilate, success about 90%; if dilation fails, revise the anastomosis. Treatment: endoscopic dilation with stent placement, with good results.
  2. **Pancreatic stricture** — pancreatitis, diarrhea, steatorrhea.

## Postoperative Pancreatic Fistula (POPF)
- 10-33%; mostly PJ leak rather than other anastomoses. Many reports show leak risk rises with small duct size, soft parenchyma, poor nutritional status, malignancy. Fortunately most fistulas close and chronic pancreatic fistula is rare.
- Problems of leakage: infection, collection, bleeding, fistula, pancreatitis or necrosis.
- Pancreatic experts usually use Bassi's article from the international study group of pancreatic fistula (ISGPF), first in 2005, latest revision 2017.
- **Definition** (ISGPF): abnormal communication between the pancreatic ductal system and another epithelial surface, leaking pancreatic juice; drain fluid amylase > 3 times the upper limit of serum amylase.
- **Grading of POPF** (2005, Bassi, the group's leader) — by severity using clinical plus US/CT, grades A, B, C:

| Criteria | No fistula | Grade A | Grade B | Grade C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drain amylase | < 3 times normal serum amylase | > 3 times normal serum amylase | > 3 times normal serum amylase | > 3 times normal serum amylase |
| Clinical conditions | Well | Well | Often well | Ill appearing/bad |
| Specific treatment | No | No | Yes/no | Yes |
| US/CT (if obtained) | Negative | Negative | Negative/positive | Positive |
| Persistent drainage (> 3 wk) | No | No | Usually yes | Yes |
| Signs of infection | No | No | Yes | Yes |
| Readmission | No | No | Yes/no | Yes/no |
| Sepsis | No | No | No | Yes |
| Reoperation | No | No | No | Yes |
| Death related to fistula | No | No | No | Yes |

(Adapted from Bassi C et al, Surgery 138:8, 2005. Signs of infection: body temperature > 38°C, leukocytosis, localized erythema, induration or purulent drainage. Readmission: any admission within 30 days of discharge. Sepsis: localized infection with positive blood culture requiring IV antibiotics, or hemodynamic compromise within 24 hours of temperature > 38°C.)

- 10 years later, POPF grading revised in 2017 (as printed, very diligent and very tedious):
  1. Old grade A renamed biochemical leak (BL)
  2. Grades B and C combined as clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula (CR-POPF)

| Event | Biochemical leak | Grade B POPF | Grade C POPF |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drain amylase > 3× upper limit of normal serum value | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Persisting peripancreatic drainage > 3 weeks | No | Yes | Yes |
| Clinically relevant change in management of POPF | No | Yes | Yes |
| Percutaneous or endoscopic drainage of POPF-associated collections | No | Yes | Yes |
| Angiographic procedures for POPF-associated bleeding | No | Yes | Yes |

- **Biochemical leak (BL)** or 2005 grade A — mild; fistula usually closes within 3 weeks without intervention. By Callery's fistula risk score, low risk: firm gland, pancreatic duct > 5 mm, intraoperative blood loss ≤ 400 cc.
- **Grade B POPF** — fistula still open after 3 weeks (surgeon starts to worry); drains still leaking long-term, or patients without drains — grade B needs intervention, percutaneous or endoscopic drainage.
- **Grade C POPF** — grade B plus organ failure.
- Figure: ISGPS 2005 vs 2016 — grade A → biochemical leak (drain amylase > 3× upper limit, no clinical relevance); 2016 grade B = drain > 3 weeks, change in management, infection without organ failure, interventional drainage, angiographic intervention (old grade C interventional treatment moves to B); 2016 grade C = organ failure, reoperation, death.
- Grades B and C (CR-POPF): by Callery's fistula risk score, high risk — soft gland, pancreatic duct < 5 mm (smaller = higher risk), intraoperative blood loss > 400 cc, especially > 1,000 cc. Low risk score ≤ 2; high risk > 7.

- Leak risk depends mainly on pancreatic texture and duct size:
  1. Soft pancreas with small duct (as in cancer) — sutures cut through easily; PJ should be dunking.
  2. Firm pancreas as in chronic pancreatitis holds sutures better; with a large duct use duct to mucosa.
  - Summary (สรุป): firm gland, big duct — better; soft and small — worse (refers to the pancreas, not other organs — as printed).
  3. Surgical technique: calm surgeon, gentle hands, tension-free anastomosis, good blood supply to pancreas and jejunum, good flow of pancreatic enzyme into the jejunum, no blockage.
- **Methods to reduce leakage** — many, but most studies have not clearly shown benefit:
  1. PG instead of PJ
  2. Routine stent — if used, for those at high risk of POPF
  3. Fibrin glue coating, or omentum or falciform ligament wrapped around the PJ anastomosis
  4. Drain
  5. Octreotide — if given, for high POPF risk
  6. Total pancreatectomy — no pancreas, no leak (as printed)
  7. Occlude the pancreatic duct — tried; caused pancreatitis and still leaked
- **Prophylactic octreotide** — rationale: reduces pancreatic secretion, so should prevent leaks or make them low output and easy to close. In practice, a Cochrane review of 17 RCTs: octreotide to prevent POPF is not recommended as standard. Data show octreotide helps once a fistula exists, reducing fistula output, but does not reduce leakage incidence, postoperative pancreatitis, re-operation rate or ICU stay.
  - If used, recommended when:
    1. Soft pancreas with small duct
    2. The hospital has leak > 10% — the author thinks fixing the surgeon would be better
    3. Start before surgery, 100-150 microgram every 8 hr, continuing 5-7 days postop
- **Treatment** — fever, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, tachycardia, rising white count → suspect leak → US or CT for collection:
  1. Collection → drain to make a controlled fistula, control sepsis with antibiotics, adequate fluid, correct electrolyte imbalance, nutrition — especially enteral, which helps closure; compared with TPN, enteral has fewer complications and lower cost.
     - Conservative treatment: fistula closes in ≥ 75%. Octreotide reduces fistula output but does not speed closure.
  2. Sepsis or pancreatic necrosis — usually severe infection, a nightmare. If not manageable by debridement or drain intervention → must open: pancreas often mushy and jejunum swollen red; must take down the anastomosis, then 2 options (3 listed, as printed):
     - 2.1 Close the pancreatic end hoping the gland atrophies, and close the jejunal opening
     - 2.2 Completion pancreatectomy, then close the jejunal opening
     - 2.3 Bridge stent technique to divert juice temporarily; if the patient survives, deal with it later
     - Figure caption: dehiscence of the pancreaticojejunal anastomosis with a gap; bridge-stent technique with externalized stent plus external drain, or internal stent with external drain adjacent to the gap.

## Delayed Gastric Emptying (DGE)
- **Cause** (สาเหตุ): unclear; suspected reduced serum motilin, or operative trauma — bruised, inflamed tissue around the pancreas causes gastric and bowel atony.
- International study group of pancreatic surgery (ISGPS): DGE = inability to eat by mouth within 1 week; 3 grades:
  - **Grade 1** — still unable to eat by day 7 and NG tube reinserted at PO D 4-7; patients do not vomit much
  - **Grade 2** — still unable to eat by day 14 and NG tube reinserted at PO D 8-14; patients often vomit
  - **Grade 3** — most severe; still unable to eat by day 21, constant fullness, NG tube needed after PO D 14 or never removed since surgery
- Fortunately most have only bloating, nausea, vomiting; NG tube for 2-3 days and they can eat. If still not, start nutrition plus investigation, e.g. CT, EGD, gastrograffin swallow. Malleo 2016 recommends:
- Figure: Suspected DGE (Malleo, Surg Clin N Am 2016), as steps —
  1. CT → presence of concomitant complications (POPF, fluid collection)
  2. Gastrografin swallow → demonstration of DGE
  3. Upper endoscopy → in PD: intestinal anastomosis control (ulcers), placement of feeding tube
- **Drug of choice** for DGE: erythromycin — binds motilin receptor, causing motilin release that stimulates smooth muscle contraction.

## Postpancreatectomy Hemorrhage (PPH)
- ≤ 8%, but a nightmare; mortality 11-40%.
- **Location of bleeding**: extraluminal or intraluminal source; causes: unsecured vessels (usually GDA, splenic artery or SMA branches), bleeding pseudoaneurysm, anastomotic suture line bleeding, raw surface bleeding, etc.
- International study group of pancreatic surgery (ISGPH, as printed) classifies PPH into 2 groups (see table):
- **Early PPH** — within the first 24 hours; usually technical failure and coagulopathy. Mild bleeding (definition below) → usually conservative; severe bleeding → likely reoperation.
- **Late PPH** — after 24 hours; usually from pancreatic enzymes digesting vessel walls; pancreatic tissue is often mushy and friable, so stopping bleeding at reoperation is hard. Work up with contrast enhanced CT plus EGD to see whether bleeding is intraluminal or extraluminal.
- Late arterial PPH → try embolization first, stops bleeding in 50-80%; if unlucky, must open. (As printed, the author recounts one case where the pancreas was all mushy, only packing was possible, with nearly 10 returns to OR before the patient died.)
- For both early and late PPH, intraluminal or extraluminal, severity is graded mild vs severe:
- **Mild bleeding** — hemoglobin drop < 3 g/dL, few signs of blood loss, < 3 units transfused in the first 24 hours postop and ≤ 3 units thereafter; no invasive intervention needed to stop bleeding.
- **Severe bleeding** — hemoglobin drop > 3 g/dL, clear signs of blood loss (BP drop, tachycardia, oliguria), > 3 units in the first 24 hours postop; may need invasive intervention to stop bleeding.

## International Study Group for Pancreatic Surgery grading of post-pancreatectomy haemorrhage

| Grade | Time of onset, location, severity and clinical impact | Clinical condition | Diagnostic consequence | Therapeutic consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Early, intra- or extraluminal, mild | Well | Observation, blood count, US and, if necessary, CT | No |
| B | Early, intra- or extraluminal, severe; or late, intra- or extraluminal, mild (a) | Often well/intermediate, very rarely life-threatening | Observation, blood count, US, CT, angiography, endoscopy (b) | Transfusion of fluid-blood, intermediate care unit (or ICU), therapeutic endoscopy (b), embolization, relaparotomy for early PPH |
