# Liver 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · volume 2, printed pages 282-(end) (PDF pages 1379-1515; printed page n = PDF page 1097 + n)

- Intro: in 2017-2019 there was new liver news: management of echinococcal (hydatid) cyst; tumors, whether benign: molecular subclass classification of hepatic adenoma, the AASLD 2018 guideline; the role of imaging studies with LI-RADS categories (like BI-RADS for breast and TI-RADS for thyroid); Barcelona BCLC staging still has argument about approach to BCLC-B/C; and in portal hypertension, whether surgeons still need to do shunts: this is what this 2020 review updates.
- The liver is the largest solid organ in the abdomen, about 2% of body weight (the author's humorous aside on liver as food is non-clinical).

## Anatomy
- Most of the liver lies in the RUQ under cover of ribs 7-11, occupying the right hypochondrium and epigastrium. Figure: position of the liver (skip).
- Glisson's capsule: peritoneum covering the liver except posteriorly and superiorly, where there is no peritoneum: called the bare area.
- Portal pedicle: contains the hepatic duct (HD) and inflow vessels, hepatic artery (HA) and portal vein (PV), in the hepatoduodenal ligament; the portal vein lies most posterior (remember); the HA lies medial to the CBD.
- Blood supply: the liver is an organ with 2 blood supplies: HA and PV.
- Hepatic artery (HA) comes from the celiac trunk, giving branches: 1. gastroduodenal and right gastric artery going down; 2. proper hepatic artery going up to the liver, then dividing into right and left hepatic arteries.
- Normal anatomy of the HA is found in 60%; abnormal 40%; so the HA is the structure with the most variation in the portal pedicle; the common variation is a replaced or aberrant artery. Figure: normal, accessory right artery, accessory left artery (skip).
  1. Replaced right HA from the SMA: found 15-20%, running behind the pancreas, lying lateral to the CBD (remember).
  2. Replaced left HA from the left gastric artery: found 3-10% (remember).
  3. Both right and left HA replaced: 1-2%.
- Blood flow to the liver: HA supplies 25% of flow and 75% of the oxygen? As printed: the HA gives 25% of blood flow and provides 75% of oxygen to the liver; primary and secondary hepatic tumors are fed by the hepatic artery.
- Portal vein (PV): formed by the SMV joining the splenic vein; it has no valve (remember), so in portal hypertension multiple portosystemic collaterals form; normally the PV gives 75% of blood flow to the liver and 25% of the oxygen. Figure: anatomy of left portal vein (umbilical portion, transverse portion, segments) (skip).
- Outflow: venous blood leaves the liver to the systemic circulation through 3 hepatic veins (HV):
  1. Right HV drains the right lobe (segments 5, 6, 7, 8); more than 50% have no branch within 1 cm before entering the IVC.
  2. Left HV drains the left lobe (segments 2, 3); in 85% it joins the middle hepatic vein before entering the IVC.
  3. Middle HV drains the right anterior sector (5, 8) and left medial sector (4).
  - 80% of left HV and middle HV join together first, then enter the IVC. For the caudate lobe (segment 1) venous blood runs directly into the IVC through short hepatic veins (retrohepatic veins). Figure: confluence of 3 hepatic veins (skip).
- Hepatic ligaments: 1. right and left triangular with right and left coronary ligaments: fix the liver superiorly and posteriorly; if all of this group are cut, the liver floats out of the upper abdomen anteriorly: easier to feel and cut the liver; 2. falciform ligament: fixes the liver to the abdominal wall, a landmark separating the medial and lateral sections of the left lobe; 3. ligamentum teres: obliterated umbilical vein extending from the falciform ligament along the lower liver, helping separate the medial and lateral segments of the left lobe; when this ligament is lifted one finds the ligamentum venosum, the remnant of the ductus venosus, a landmark for the intrahepatic portion of the left PV. Figure: hepatic ligaments (skip).
- Hepatic division: by external landmark, or by segments (Couinaud):
  1. Topographic landmark: used in the past: 1.1 Cantlie's line divides the liver into right and left lobes, an imaginary line from the gallbladder fossa below to the right side of the IVC above; 1.2 the ligamentum teres divides the left lobe into medial and lateral sectors.
  2. Couinaud segmental anatomy: divides the liver into 8 segments by portal pedicle branches and hepatic venous drainage (remember): segments 2, 3, 4 on the left; segments 5, 6, 7, 8 on the right; each segment has its own artery, vein and bile duct, independent of other segments, so the liver can be cut into small segments; to divide by the Couinaud method one needs IOUS and injecting contrast into a subsegmental branch of the PV (staining technique). Figure: Couinaud's liver segments 1 to 8 (clockwise) (skip).
     - Caudate lobe (segment 1): inflow of artery, vein and bile duct comes from both right and left; outflow (venous drainage) enters the IVC directly without passing the HV; with HV thrombosis or Budd-Chiari syndrome, blood leaving the liver drains through the short hepatic veins of the caudate, so the caudate lobe hypertrophies (remember).
  3. Goldsmith and Woodburne: sectors: 3.1 right lobe: right anterior sector (segments 5, 8) and right posterior sector (segments 6, 7); 3.2 left lobe: left medial sector (segment 4) and left lateral sector (segments 2, 3). Figure: fissures (main portal, right portal, left portal, left suprahepatic) (skip).
- Neural innervation: parasympathetic from the vagus and sympathetic, whose function is unknown; in liver transplant patients the denervated liver still works as before.
- Lymphatic drainage: matters for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: drains into the perisinusoidal space then 2 ways: down to the hilar and cystic duct lymph nodes (Calot's triangle node), and up to the cardiophrenic lymph nodes.

## Function
- Just enough idea; too much gets dizzying:
  1. Involved in glucose and cholesterol metabolism in every phase.
  2. Makes vitamin K-dependent factors 2, 7, 9, 10; factor 7 has the shortest half-life; beware a trap: the liver does not make factor 8 or von Willebrand factor.
  3. Makes many proteins, especially albumin.
  4. Makes bile: bile from the liver helps absorb fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K.
  5. Reticuloendothelial (RE) system by Kupffer cells (liver macrophages).

## Basic Knowledge
- Glucose metabolism: after a meal or fasting 48 hours, the body gets glucose from lysis of hepatic glycogen; the liver makes glucose from albumin (protein) too.
- Regeneration: after liver resection the liver has a very strong hyperplastic response and the regenerated liver still has normal architecture.
- Cirrhosis: Ito cell (liver stellate cell) is believed to be the primary mediator of liver cell necrosis, releasing cytokines that stimulate fibrogenesis ending in liver fibrosis.

## Investigation
- Not written for added knowledge but to memorize for exams:
  1. Liver scan (not used much now): 1.1 octreotide scan for carcinoid metastasis to the liver; 1.2 Tc RBC scan for hemangioma in the past, now MRI works much better; 1.3 Tc albumin scan separates focal nodular hyperplasia (warm or hot nodule) from hepatic adenoma (cold nodule) (read under benign disease).
  2. CT scan: besides tumors, benign and malignant, residents should know the classic features of cirrhosis: caudate lobe hypertrophy, right lobe atrophy, ascites, recanalization of the umbilical vein, big spleen; beware that a portal vein with enlarged size is not "small" (remember).
  3. Angiogram: little role in investigation now; used for treatment, e.g. embolization.
  4. MRI: important like CT: the picture and interpretation of liver tumors are now much more detailed.

## Liver Abscess
- Residents should be able to speak about 3 conditions with eyes closed: pyogenic liver abscess (PLA), amebic liver abscess (ALA), and hydatid cyst, a liver infection from a parasite rare in Thai cities.

### Pyogenic Liver Abscess (PLA)
- Etiology: formerly ascending biliary infection from stones, tumor, bile duct stricture was believed the most common; now ascending biliary infection from biliary tract procedures, e.g. biliary stent placement or after investigations such as PTC and ERCP, is more common.
- Other causes: hematogenous spread: organisms run along the hepatic artery, e.g. bacterial endocarditis or from a dental abscess; in liver trauma; in treatment of liver disease, e.g. alcohol injection, RFA, chemoembolization; organisms via the portal vein, e.g. appendicitis, diverticulitis; direct extension, e.g. PU perforation.
- Cryptogenic PLA: PLA with no primary source found: about 25%; believed from: 1. large doses of antibiotic for the primary disease (e.g. diverticulitis that has resolved), leaving only the liver abscess; 2. PLA arising from nonoperative management in liver trauma patients.
- Table (most frequent causes of PLA): A. Hepatobiliary: benign: lithiasis, cholecystitis, biliary-enteric anastomosis, endoscopic biliary procedures, percutaneous biliary procedures; malignant. B. Portal: benign: diverticulitis, anorectal suppuration, pelvic suppuration, postoperative sepsis, intestinal perforation, pancreatic abscess, appendicitis; malignant: gastric, colonic Ca. C. Arterial: endocarditis, vascular sepsis, ENT infection, dental infection. D. Traumatic: benign: open or closed abdominal trauma; malignant: chemoembolization, percutaneous ethanol injection or radiofrequency. E. Cryptogenic. F. Direct extension from a nearby nidus of infection.
- Risk factors: 1. transplant patients, especially OLT; 2. immunocompromised: elderly, alcohol, chemo, cancer or diabetes; 3. underlying GI or biliary disease.
- Hospital mortality rises in: 1. cancer, diabetes, GI perforation; 2. WBC over 20,000; 3. high bilirubin, low albumin; 4. pleural effusion; 5. multiple small abscesses.
- Organism: gram positive, gram negative and anaerobes: 1. USA: E. coli and B. fragilis most often; 2. Asia: Klebsiella more often than E. coli; 3. if the cause is endocarditis: strep or staph; 4. 15-20% culture negative.
- Right lobe 60%; single abscess 60%, multiple 40%. Figure (skip).
- Symptoms: chills and fever, RUQ pain but not specific; malaise, anorexia, tender liver; about 1/3 have jaundice.
- CBC: WBC up. LFT: not specific, normal or only liver enzymes up; if PLA suspected use US as the initial screening study, sensitivity about 80-90%. Hemoculture positive about 50%.
- CT with IV contrast: sensitivity 95-100%, helps establish diagnosis, can see abscess under 2 cm (microabscess) and even as small as 0.5 cm; classic: low attenuation complex mass like a thick-walled cavity with small cavities coalescing into a larger cavity, with peripheral rim enhancement, maybe air-fluid level and internal septum; MRI is not better than CT.
- Before treatment, rule out amebic liver abscess first, because treatment differs: send hemoculture and aspirate pus for Gram stain and culture.
- Treatment:
  1. IV broad-spectrum antibiotic at once, e.g. pip-tazo, carbapenem, or ceftriaxone + metronidazole; no need to wait for culture, then adjust to culture; give 14-21 days then oral antibiotic another 4-6 weeks, but time matters less than response: afebrile, normal WBC, CT shows resolution of abscess.
  2. Find a way to drain: choose PAD (percutaneous abscess drainage) first; if PAD is not possible or not available, open drainage with IOUS guidance; only about 10% need laparotomy.
  - Figure: PLA right lobe, percutaneous catheter drainage (skip).
  - Absolute contraindication to PAD: 1. coagulopathy (remember); 2. next: ascites, or abscess near vital structures: should not puncture.
  3. Remember: PLA with a catheter left in: drain it if it can be drained; antibiotics alone suffice only for: 3.1 small single abscess under 1 cm; 3.2 multiple abscesses of biliary origin; 3.3 children; 3.4 CMV microabscesses; 3.5 rapid clinical response to antibiotic therapy.
  - Debate: intermittent percutaneous aspiration vs leaving a pigtail catheter in the abscess (percutaneous catheter drainage); both are used per the reports, but the tendency is to leave a catheter, except Schwartz 2019 whose author prefers aspiration without drain, believing the pus is thick; for one best answer: leave a catheter, for irrigation with NSS mixed with antibiotic into the cavity; not the accepted answer: whoever aspirates only gets hit.
  - After PAD, patients usually improve; if after 2-3 days symptoms are not improving or worse, repeat US or CT (abscess not collapsing); must consider operative drainage, open or laparoscopic as appropriate.
- Indication for open drainage: 1. failure of percutaneous drainage; 2. complications of percutaneous drainage; 3. multiloculation; 4. intraperitoneal rupture; 5. septic shock; 6. co-existing pathology requiring surgical treatment; 7. hepatolithiasis.
- Small technique for open drainage (know it, in case the hospital cannot do PAD): 1. incision: midline or right subcostal; 2. explore thoroughly for associated disease that might be the cause of PLA; 3. besides palpation use IOUS to locate the abscess; 4. pack around the abscess, open the cavity with an incision, take a biopsy of the abscess wall to rule out tumor; 5. place a large catheter in the cavity, bring the tube out the anterior abdominal wall through a separate incision, so one can irrigate or inject contrast to see whether the abscess is shrinking. Figure: open abscess cavity (skip).
- Factors associated with failure of percutaneous drainage: 1. multiple or multiloculated abscess; 2. size over 7 cm, or very small widespread abscess (too large or too small does not suit); 3. viscous fluid or substantial necrotic material; 4. intracystic bleeding; 5. abscess perforation.
- Liver resection: used in PLA caused by: 1. malignant tumor with central necrosis; 2. IHD stone with the affected liver already atrophic; 3. intrahepatic biliary stricture.
- Do not forget to treat the primary disease, e.g. if appendicitis, remove the appendix.
- New trends in drainage: 1. endoscopic transgastric or transduodenal drainage guided by EUS for abscess at the caudate and left lobe, placing a nasoabscess Teflon catheter for irrigation (figure: EUS guided PLA drainage, skip); 2. video-assisted abscess drainage: enter from the flank or back with a 10 mm scope into the abscess cavity, see the pus clearly and debride necrotic tissue, similar to the debridement method in infected pancreatic necrosis. Both have few patients and need a skilled operator, so not widespread.
- Notes: 1. today even multiple or multiloculated abscess can have PAD; 2. in a patient who worsens after drainage and the abscess will not collapse, the liver part must be cut: formal hepatectomy is used very rarely except PLA from cancer; 3. prognosis of single abscess is better than multiple; 4. principle of PLA treatment: "IV antibiotics + PAD + treat the primary cause".

### Amebic Liver Abscess (ALA)
- ALA is found in endemic areas like hydatid cyst.
- Infection: fecal-oral route: eating food or water contaminated with E. histolytica as mature cysts; after the cyst passes the stomach to the small bowel, it is digested by alkaline juice into trophozoites, which pass down to the colon, believed to invade the colonic mucosa, enter the portal vein and arrive at the liver; the trophozoites there are subjected to proteolytic enzyme produced by E. histolytica, causing hepatic cellular hydrolysis followed by liquefaction necrosis of liver tissue mixed with blood into pus looking like anchovy paste (anchovy pus). Figure: trophozoite at the abscess wall; life cycle (skip).
- Remember: the pus of ALA is sterile with no trophozoites: the trophozoites are at the cyst wall (remember), so aspirating pus for examination does not find ameba and bacterial culture is negative.
- If there is no secondary bacterial infection, the pus has no foul smell, unlike PLA pus which smells foul. Figure: necrotizing center of ALA (skip).
- Symptoms: differ from PLA: ALA is subacute: fever, anorexia, RLQ... RUQ pain for over 1 week up to 1 month; 10-20% of patients have diarrhea.
- Most common complication: secondary bacterial infection; next rupture.
- Chance of finding ameba in stool is not over 15%, so serology is used for diagnosis.
- Serology (for E. histolytica): 1. indirect hemagglutination test: result takes 1-2 days, not in time for the acute phase; 2. ELISA: a test taking not over 35 minutes, faster; the problem is that those who were previously infected with E. histolytica may have false positive; still ELISA is reliable over 90%; 3. PCR (genotyping): WHO recommends it for developing countries including Thailand: can test stool or pus or tissue, very high sensitivity and specificity, and can separate Entamoeba species.
- US: rounded hypoechoic lesion adjacent to the liver capsule with internal fluid; higher sensitivity than CT, so US is the imaging study of choice: peripheral rim edema without rim enhancement; the abscess has few septa; on CT the classic is low attenuation fluid inside surrounded by a high attenuation inner ring: double target appearance (double rim). Figure: double target sign (skip).
- Treatment: if symptoms fit (fever, RUQ pain), living in an endemic area, US or CT shows an abscess and serology is positive: start treatment at once, with eradication of ameba both in the GI tract and liver.
  1. Inconclusive (not sure whether ALA): a therapeutic trial with metronidazole orally or IV about 750 mg three times after meals for 10 days; metronidazole treats both intra- and extraintestinal amebiasis; usually ALA treated with Flagyl alone, in not over 3 days symptoms clearly improve, but the abscess resolution depends on the size of the abscess at the start, taking about 3-9 months; if after 3 days of metronidazole there is no improvement, suspect a complication or secondary bacterial infection, or not ALA.
  2. When Flagyl should not be used, e.g. pregnancy, 3 other drugs can substitute: 2.1 emetine, intramuscular: beware cardiotoxicity; 2.2 chloroquine phosphate; 2.3 now paromomycin and iodoquinol are advised; 2.4 diloxanide furoate suits carriers.
  3. Aspiration: normally not necessary, because it does not speed healing. Indications for aspiration: 3.1 uncertain diagnosis: serology unclear, cannot separate from PLA; 3.2 Flagyl given 3 days and clinical does not improve; 3.3 pregnancy: should not use Flagyl; 3.4 suspect secondary bacterial infection, found in 15% of ALA; 3.5 high rupture risk (though ALA has a capsule resistant to hydrolysis by trophozoites, in these cases aspirate because there is a right of rupture): 3.5.1 size over 5 cm or progressive; 3.5.2 left lobe; 3.5.3 subcapsular lesion; 3.6 evidence of pulmonary, peritoneal or pericardial extension; 3.7 involvement of both lobes. Send aspirated pus for bacteria and culture; if the first aspiration does not work, aspirate a second time, and at the second aspiration leave a drain in the abscess. Figure: aspiration (skip).
  4. Rupture: seen less because the ALA capsule is rather thick: incidence 2.7%; diagnosis and treatment depend on where it ruptures. Figure: rupture routes A-E (skip).
     - 4.1 Into the peritoneal cavity: found 18-70%: the patient looks toxic with signs of peritonitis; CT or US shows perihepatic collection; in the past was opened wholesale: opening the abdomen is difficult, but if opened there is not much to do; liver in such patients is usually bad with coagulopathy and bleeding hard to control, and after surgery bile leak problems follow. Indications for explore laparotomy: 1. doubtful diagnosis; 2. concomitant hollow viscus perforation or fistulization; 3. life-threatening hemorrhage or sepsis; 4. failure of conservative treatment. Currently there are reports of success with percutaneous drainage of both the abscess and the abdominal collection, so it is advised to try aspiration first and not drag the patient to open.
     - 4.2 Into the pleural cavity: the pus compresses the lung on that side rapidly until the lung collapses; patients have severe dyspnea, portable chest x-ray finds the lung opaque on that side. Figure: ruptured ALA to the right pleural space (skip). Symptoms are irritation of the diaphragm on that side, which is chronic, with dry cough, not much chest pain as in empyema; film shows blunt costophrenic angle; treatment: aspirate the chest as much as possible then ICD carefully because the diaphragm is often high (ICD may pass into the abdomen).
     - 4.3 Into a bronchus: the patient coughs a lot, a severe cough with cough fits until retching, coughing up chocolate-colored sputum; treatment: no surgery: postural drainage, percussion of the lung to let pus out through the mouth, with bronchodilator and antiamebic drug.
     - 4.4 Pericardial complication (remember, since it is asked almost every time): a serious complication, often ALA in the left lobe rupturing into the pericardium; the patient may have no symptoms, or some present with cardiac tamponade, with high chance of death. Figure (skip). CT or US finds left lobe ALA, pericardial thickening and effusion. Treatment (remember, asked repeatedly): 1. aspiration of the pericardium via the subxiphoid route; 2. PAD of the ALA; 3. antiamebic drug.
- Table (features of amebic vs pyogenic liver abscess): age: amebic 20-40, pyogenic over 50; M:F: amebic 10:1 or more, pyogenic 1.5:1; solitary vs multiple: amebic solitary 80% (in acute amebic abscess 50% solitary), pyogenic solitary 50%; location: usually right liver for both; travel in endemic area: amebic yes, pyogenic no; diabetes: amebic uncommon (about 2%), pyogenic more common (about 27%); alcohol use: common in both; jaundice: amebic uncommon, pyogenic common; elevated bilirubin: amebic uncommon, pyogenic common; elevated alkaline phosphatase: common in both; positive blood culture: amebic no, pyogenic common; positive amebic serology: amebic yes, pyogenic no.
- Figure (algorithm), as steps: liver abscess -> history of preceding travel to endemic area: yes: urgent amoebic serology: positive: oral metronidazole followed by eradication of bowel cysts with diloxanide furoate; if failure or threatened rupture: drainage; negative: empirical IV antibiotics for pyogenic abscess and drainage; no travel: empirical IV antibiotics for pyogenic abscess and drainage; older patient with preceding biliary/bowel disease: empirical IV antibiotics for pyogenic abscess and drainage.

### Parasitic or Hydatid Cysts
- Though uncommon in Thailand, residents should know a little, since Thais travel abroad often and foreigners from sheep-raising countries such as Australia or New Zealand, and the Middle East, visit Thailand, where the parasite is a cause of disease with infected animals.
- Cause: the tape worm Echinococcus granulosus, usually living in the intestine of dogs (definitive host), which defecate on roads, fields or meadows; sheep graze the grass containing eggs and become the intermediate host; the parasite egg then enters man's mouth: man is an accidental host (the author's remark); the egg goes to the small bowel, the parasite invades the mucosa of the duodenum, enters the blood and goes to settle at the liver in the form of a cyst: 2/3 are at the liver, the rest at the spleen or lung; the parasite goes from sheep to man, not human-to-human transmission. Figure: life cycle (dog, immature forms, adult worm, egg, sheep, human hydatidosis; cyst layers) (skip).
- Cyst wall of the parasite has 3 layers (layers 1 and 2 made by the parasite): 1. inner germinal layer (endocyst); 2. middle laminated membrane (ectocyst); 3. outer pericyst layer: made by the liver to wall off the hydatid cyst, thickness about 2-5 mm: the pericyst. Figure (skip).
- Inside the hydatid cyst are the larvae (scolex); 50% have calcification of the cyst wall.
- 1/3 of cysts enlarge; the other 2/3 mature with calcification, no parasite left, called inactive cyst.
- Symptoms: generally few until the cyst is large enough, then pressure symptoms, mostly in a large cyst about 10 cm: abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting; some present with a complication such as rupture then anaphylactic shock from intraperitoneal contamination: sudden abdominal pain, urticaria, bronchospasm, even death; also infection and jaundice.
- Investigation: 1. CBC: 25% have eosinophilia; 2. serology: 2.1 Casoni's skin test: injecting hydatid fluid into the skin like a drug test: positive 70-80%, but some have severe allergy so not used; 2.2 complement fixation test positive 65%; 2.3 indirect hemagglutination or ELISA: antibody response positive up to 85-90%, but beware false positive in people from endemic areas or previously infected; 2.4 hydatid antigen blotting: sensitivity 95%, specificity 100% (the best); 3. imaging: US is the initial imaging: shows a rather thick cyst wall, separating it from a simple cyst with thin septa; then CT or MRI may show the following features:
  - 3.1 complex cyst with thick wall; 3.2 wall calcification, typical of this disease (a liver abscess shows wall enhancement); 3.3 sometimes protoscolices of the parasite seen. Figure: CT with daughter cysts and peripheral calcification (skip).
  4. ERCP: about 25% show communication with the biliary system.
- Treatment:
  1. Even without symptoms, once diagnosed it must be treated; observation alone is used rarely; observation is acceptable only if the cyst is small, a deep calcified cyst, asymptomatic, and serology negative.
  2. Main methods: 2.1 surgery as the mainstay; 2.2 medical treatment; 2.3 percutaneous treatment (PAIR); 2.4 surgical treatment.
  3. Choice depends on: 3.1 patient fitness; 3.2 number and location of cysts; 3.3 complications.

### Medical Treatment
- Albendazole: the antiparasitic used as 1st line: 10-15 mg/kg/day morning and evening for 5-12 months; good absorption and high concentration in the cyst fluid; now preferred together with praziquantel, which gives better results than albendazole alone; drug alone cures only 30%.
- Albendazole is preferred together with surgery or PAIR; drug alone is used rarely but has a role in smaller uncomplicated cysts or deep lesions under 5 cm, or in patients at very high surgical risk: give it first to improve the patient then re-evaluate for surgery.

### Percutaneous Aspiration Instillation and Reaspiration (PAIR)
- Before PAIR give albendazole 1 week and continue after PAIR another 28 days.
- Technique: 1. percutaneous aspiration under US guidance; 2. inject a sclerosant to kill protoscolices in the cyst cavity; 3. finally reaspirate the cyst content. Figure: puncture, check for scolices, aspiration, injection, reaspiration (skip).
- Sclerosants: 0.9% NSS (the most used), 95% ethanol, 0.5% cetrimide, 0.05% chlorhexidine, 10% povidone-iodine.
- Indications for PAIR: 1. patient refuses surgery; 2. pregnancy; 3. relapse after surgery; 4. accessible location.
- Contraindications for PAIR: 1. age under 3 years; 2. uncooperative patient; 3. complicated cyst; 4. location not accessible, e.g. adjacent to major blood vessel or major bile duct.
- WHO Informal Working Group on Echinococcosis (WHO-IWGE) classifies by US criteria into CL (no cyst wall yet) and CE 1-5, as a guide to treatment.
- WHO recommends albendazole + PAIR for cysts not over 5 cm, unilocular CE 1-3, or recurrence after surgery; cases not fitting this, WHO recommends operating.
- Figure/table (WHO-IWGE, sonographic characteristics): CL: unilocular cyst, anechoic, no wall depicted; CE1: CL characteristics + wall + mobile internal echogenicities; CE2: multivesicular, multiseptated cyst, daughter cysts, honeycomb pattern; CE3: detached membrane (water-lily sign) (CE3a, CE3b); CE4: heterogeneous, hypo- or hyperechoic cyst, no daughter vesicles; CE5: cyst with partial or complete wall calcification. By viability: active: CE1, CE2 (unilocular simple cysts; multivesicular multiseptated cyst); transitional: CE3 (floating membrane, water lily sign); inactive: CE4 (heterogeneous degenerative contents), CE5 (thick calcified wall).

### Surgical Treatment
- In the past surgery was the treatment of choice for good candidates (one best); now WHO recommends surgery only for: very large cysts, cysts with infection, cysts with biliary communication, superficial cysts with high risk of rupture, cysts pressing on adjacent organs.
- Laparoscopy is possible, but open is preferred more now, since manipulation and control of spillage are easier.
- Techniques divide into 2 groups by extent: conservative vs radical surgery.
- Conservative surgery: open the cyst, evacuate, omentoplasty; suits superficial peripheral lesions. Tip and technique: 1. pack the operative field with scolicide-soaked gauzes; 2. aspirate the cyst as a closed system until empty (figure A); 3. after aspiration, if no bile comes out, inject sclerosant to fill the cavity (figure B); 4. evacuate the cyst content; 5. unroof, take part of the cyst wall out, then stuff omentum into the cavity (figure C). Figures (skip).
- Radical surgery: 3 methods: pericystectomy, liver resection and OLT; do not forget to pack the liver well.
  1. Pericystectomy: use a finger or dissector to shell it out. Figure (skip). 1.1 Open technique: suits a large cyst with a thin wall that looks likely to rupture when cut out: inject scolicidal into the cyst first to sterilize; 1.2 closed technique: suits a cyst in the middle with a thick wall, bordering the liver and not involving major vessels or bile duct; resect en loc, IOUS may help, no need to inject scolicidal into the cyst since the plane of cut is outside the pericyst.
  2. Liver resection: for a deep or central cyst: 2.1 non-anatomical resection; 2.2 anatomical resection: for: 2.2.1 large multiloculated cyst in one side of the liver; 2.2.2 cyst near a major blood vessel or bile duct; 2.2.3 complicated cyst with large biliary fistula.
  3. OLT: suits cases with: 3.1 so many cysts in both lobes that nothing can be done, leaving the liver to be bad; 3.2 sclerosing cholangitis; 3.3 Budd-Chiari syndrome.
- Remember: whoever drains or fenestrates this as a simple cyst gets hit, because anaphylaxis will occur.
- Intraoperative management of biliary-cyst communication: if known before surgery, e.g. patient with jaundice, do ERCP first; if found in the OR: suction bile, or after pericystectomy place a dry pad in the cavity and squeeze the gallbladder: the pad turns yellow-green where bile leaks: do IOC to find the communication:
  1. Communication size under 5 mm: inject methylene blue to see where it leaks, then suture there.
  2. Communication size over 5 mm: treat like a bile duct fistula: bile duct decompression with adequate drainage, e.g. T-tube into the CBD, or if the CBD is dilated, bypass; the last option is hepatic resection.
- Comparison of results: 1. PAIR morbidity 8-13%, mortality 0.1% (very low); surgery morbidity 25-33%, mortality 0.7-4%, much higher; 2. recurrence of disease: PAIR and conservative surgery not different, under 10%; radical surgery about 2%: not surprising: now surgery is kept for more advanced disease.
- WHO guideline for follow up: history, examination, CBC, LFT, US every 3-6 months.
- Cameron 2017 summary (based on WHO-IWGE): 1. unilocular lesion (size over 5 cm) suitable for puncture: albendazole + PAIR first; if failed, operate; 2. multilocular lesion with few cysts: may try albendazole + PAIR first; if many cysts or multiseptated: operate; 3. observation without drugs is used for completely calcified and inactive cysts (CE4, CE5).
- Figure (table, stage and treatment): CE1 simple unilocular cyst: under 5 cm albendazole 400 mg PO BID; over 5 cm albendazole + PAIR. CE3a cysts with liquid content and poorly defined septations: under 5 cm albendazole; over 5 cm albendazole + PAIR. CE2 complex cysts with multiple septations: PAIR contraindicated: large bore percutaneous treatment/surgery + albendazole 400 mg PO BID. CE3b defined daughter cysts within a mucinous or solid matrix: PAIR contraindicated: large bore percutaneous treatment/surgery + albendazole. CE4 and 5 solid cysts with degenerative changes, eventually calcification of the outer wall: PAIR contraindicated: observation (imaging recommended q6 months, ultrasound preferred).

## Liver Cysts
- 3 big groups: 1. congenital cysts: simple cyst and polycystic liver disease (PCLD); 2. acquired cysts: traumatic cyst and parasitic or hydatid cyst; 3. neoplastic cysts: cystadenoma (some call it mucinous cystic neoplasm (MCN) of the liver), carcinoma, IPMN of the bile duct.
- The diagnosis must be known before treating, because options range from observation to radical resection.
- Peliosis hepatis: a benign disorder with blood-filled cystic cavities; real cause unknown but related to anabolic steroid and oral contraceptives; no malignant risk; some may rupture or have liver failure; usually improves once the drug is stopped.

### Simple Cyst
- Most have no symptoms; F : M = 4 : 1; usually incidental findings; no malignant transformation.
- A minority have symptoms from cyst growth: pressure symptoms, usually in masses larger than 8 cm.
- Usually no communication with the bile duct.
- The most common benign lesion of the liver, counting benign tumors too; but if asked the most common benign tumor of the liver only, answer hemangioma.
- US or CT features separating from neoplastic cyst follow (figure of CT three phases: arterial, portal, delayed: no enhancement) (skip).
- Table (simple cyst vs neoplastic cyst): unilocular vs multilocular, septation; thin and sharp wall vs thick, irregular; no mural nodule or projections vs mural nodule, projection; thin, watery, straw-colored fluid (density 0-10 HU) vs thick, green-brown fluid; no mucin, usually serous vs mucinous fluid; normal CEA and CA 19-9 vs raised CEA and CA 19-9 in fluid; no malignant transformation vs possible; low density under 10 HU vs higher density over 10 HU.
- Treatment:
  1. Observe and follow up if no symptoms or vague symptoms unrelated to the cyst; remember cyst size is not an indication for treatment; we do not treat to prevent bleeding or rupture.
  2. Indications for treatment: 2.1 severe intractable symptoms from the simple cyst with no other cause; 2.2 intracystic hemorrhage; 2.3 diagnosis uncertain.
  3. Methods (per Shackelford 2019): 3.1 aspiration alone: recurrence 100%, tiring; 3.2 percutaneous aspiration, instillation of alcohol and reaspiration (PAIR): success 80%; before injecting alcohol aspirate fluid first: if the fluid is turbid and looks infected, place a drain (PAD) at once; if the aspirate is bile (green), showing bile duct communication, forbid injecting alcohol into the cyst absolutely; send the patient for ERCP to find the fistula instead.
  - Sclerosants other than alcohol (used most) can also be hypertonic saline or tetracycline; but new PAIR reports look poor: recurrence up to 80% in long follow-up.
  3.3 Formerly (before PAIR), laparoscopic unroofing or fenestration: opening widely, cutting the cyst wall down to the edge of normal liver; success 95%; now fenestration is the first-step treatment for a symptomatic simple cyst in a fit patient; open or laparoscopic as convenient, one best: laparoscopy; if failure after PAIR and the cyst lies anteriorly, use lap unroofing or fenestration. Figure: fenestration steps (unroof the superficial cyst, open the deep cyst, suction, bleeding coagulation, transection of septum) (skip). For a cyst at segments 7, 8, usually upper and posterior, lap unroofing is hard: open fenestration may be needed. Figure (skip).
  3.4 During surgery, aspirate fluid and send cytology, send the inner cyst wall and intracystic fluid with frozen section of the cyst wall to rule out neoplastic cyst.
- Remarks: 1. for a simple cyst, cystectomy or hepatectomy is excessive: whoever does it gets hit; 2. unroof means opening the roof and is used with a deep simple cyst; fenestration suits a cyst near the liver capsule, matching the term deroof (opening the back of the cyst); 3. Cameron seems not to like PAIR because after sclerosis, if it must be dissected later it is difficult; his view is that if there is an indication, treat a fit patient with lap fenestration as the first priority (the author says he would follow Schwartz for now); 4. before fenestration be sure it is not an echinococcal cyst or a neoplastic cyst.

### Polycystic Liver Disease (PCLD)
- More than 3 cysts. Figure: classic gross finding (skip).
- Usually found in women; autosomal dominant, except congenital PCLD which is autosomal recessive.
- 50% of PCLD have polycystic kidney disease (PCK); 15% of PCK have PCLD.
- The problem of PCK is renal failure, found quite often, while in PCLD liver failure is rare.
- 80% have no symptoms; in those with symptoms, related to age (older, more); also size (the larger, the more symptoms).
- No malignant transformation, so no symptoms means no treatment.
- CT looks like simple cyst but number over 3. Figure (skip).
- Treatment: no symptoms: no treatment. Symptoms needing treatment: 1.1 mass effect from enlarged liver; 1.2 complications such as infection, hemorrhage, rupture; portal hypertension is rare.
- Treatment depends on classification: formerly Morino (complicated, not working); now adult PCLD is divided into 3 types by CT: Gigot classification:
  - Type 1: large cysts up to 10 cm with few small cysts (2-3 cysts, not over 10), with most of the liver still normal. Treatment: if the cyst is deep and the patient not fit: PAIR with alcohol; if a superficial anterior cyst: lap fenestration.
  - Type 2: multiple medium-sized cysts 5-7 cm scattered through the liver, the rest still normal. Treatment: extensive open fenestration recommended; some need to resect the affected segment; OLT is not criticized.
  - Type 3: both small and medium cysts under 5 cm spread through the liver with very little normal parenchyma. Treatment: beware this type because 10% have cerebral aneurysm, which must be treated first, then deal with the liver by 2 ways: 1. partial hepatectomy with fenestration, used when after resection at least 2 liver segments remain; 2. OLT if after resection less than 2 segments would remain, ensuring liver failure: then do liver transplantation (in Cameron 2011 he recommends OLT for type 3; if choosing one best, choose OLT).
  - Table (Gigot): Type 1: number under 10; large (over 10 cm); limited involvement; normal parenchyma large. Type 2: number over 10; medium; diffuse involvement; normal parenchyma medium. Type 3: number over 10; small; massive and diffuse; normal parenchyma very little.
- Approach: choose lap before open.
- Treatment method: manage the dominant cyst and try to reduce the number of cysts to reduce liver size.
- Most common complication of surgery: ascites and bile leak.
- Some people gave patients oral contraceptive pill to try (as reported): besides not helping, it makes the cyst grow (report).

### Traumatic Cyst
- Current trend is nonoperative treatment of liver injury, especially subcapsular hematoma that after clot lysis often ends as a cyst; patients on anticoagulant who suffer minor blunt abdominal trauma can also get a traumatic cyst.
- Usually post-traumatic cysts have no symptoms; some have vague pain.
- Treatment: because traumatic cysts regress spontaneously, one should observe: do not rush to poke them; if pressure symptoms or rapid growth: treat like a simple cyst.

### Neoplastic Cysts
- 2 big groups: 1. primary: benign and malignant: cystadenoma, cystadenocarcinoma, IPMN-B; 2. secondary: usually malignancy from colon, pancreas, kidney, ovary, NETs.

### Cystadenoma and Cystadenocarcinoma
- A primary neoplastic cyst: found often in women over 40 years.
- Found in under 5% of intrahepatic cysts; many do not like the name hepatic cystadenoma since this cystic tumor probably arises from bile duct epithelium (ovarian stroma found up to 85%: wait for the pathology report to conclude).
- Early cases usually have no symptoms; but with time symptoms increase, especially pain from capsular stretch.
- Even benign cystadenoma has 25% chance of turning malignant, so once diagnosed, treat. Figure: neoplastic cyst with papillary projection from the cyst wall (skip).
- US: cyst with internal echo. CT: multiple loculations, internal septation with contrast enhancement, papillary projection, mural nodule; cyst content not clear because of mucin; do not aspirate fluid for examination, since tumor cells may spread.
- Outline irregular, enhanced vascularity, mural nodule at the wall, calcification, invasion of the liver or adjacent tissue: suggests cancer. Figure: biliary cystadenoma in 3 phases on CT (skip).
- Treatment: resect the whole cyst by enucleation or wedge resection with frozen section of the cyst wall (though frozen section accuracy for benign vs malignant is a problem, the author thinks one should still send); if pathology reads benign, enough; if malignant, formal hepatectomy.
- Indication for formal hepatectomy: 1. pathology reports cancer; 2. benign lesion very deep; 3. benign lesion communicating with the bile duct.

### Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm of the Bile Duct (IPMN-BD)
- Now classified in cystic neoplasms; IPMN of the bile duct has behavior and phenotype like IPMN of the pancreas.
- Differs from biliary cystadenoma: IPMN-BD is often found in men, no ovarian stroma, and has bile duct communication; biliary cystadenoma is often in women, has ovarian stroma, and no bile duct communication.
- Imaging: many papillary lesions in the bile duct lumen producing mucin making the duct dilated or forming a cystic mass with bile duct communication on IOC or ERCP. Figure: MRI/MRCP of IPMN-BD (skip).
- Cholangioscope: mucus in the bile duct and papillary projections at the duct wall. Figure (skip).
- Chance of invasive cancer 60%, higher than cystadenoma.
- Treatment: resection with IOC; if all removed, 5-year survival 60-80%, much better than cholangiocarcinoma.

## Approach to the Liver Mass
- A question often met in oral exams: managing a liver mass found incidentally, e.g. on CT or US work-up for suspected gallstone that incidentally finds a liver mass, or for abdominal pain.
- Before answering, take 2-3 deep breaths, control consciousness, do not tremble; order the answer in this system: 1. what is the mass: infection, cyst or solid tumor; 2. if a cyst: simple cyst or neoplastic cyst; 3. if a tumor, look at the face: is it likely benign or malignant; 4. if malignant: primary liver cancer or metastasis from elsewhere, and what is the extent of disease now; 5. plan treatment.
- Figure (liver mass classification): cystic: benign: simple cyst, PCLD, abscess, cystadenoma, traumatic; malignant: cystadenocarcinoma, metastasis. Solid: benign: hemangioma, FNH, hepatic adenoma; malignant: HCC, IHC, metastasis.
- History: 1. symptoms such as pain, fever with chills: suspect abscess; if weight loss and anorexia: points to cancer; 2. risk factors of cancer: alcohol, hepatitis virus, cirrhosis; 3. history of drugs: a woman on the pill suggests hepatic adenoma; addicts on IV drugs may be PLA; 4. living in a country with amebiasis: think ALA; those from the Middle East: beware hydatid; 5. previous malignancy may suggest liver metastasis; 6. do not ask irrelevant things in the exam, e.g. the teacher's lunch.
- Physical examination: signs of chronic liver disease and malignancy: hepatomegaly, big spleen, jaundice, ascites, enlarged lymph nodes.
- Lab: CBC, platelet count, LFT, PT, PTT, INR, hepatitis profile and tumor markers such as AFP, CEA, CA 19-9.
- Imaging: ask for dynamic post-contrast imaging, CT or MRI; if one can choose, ask for contrast-enhanced MRI; the resident must be able to read and describe the film correctly, state the phase, explain what is seen, and give a differential diagnosis.

## Benign Liver Tumors
- Pay attention, since asked for sure. Classified by tissue of origin: 1. hepatocyte: hepatic adenoma, focal nodular hyperplasia; 2. mesenchymal tissue: hemangioma, hamartoma; 3. heterotopic tissue: adrenal rest, pancreatic rest; 4. cholangiocyte: biliary cystadenoma, biliary adenoma. Small ones such as lipoma, angiomyolipoma: know or not know.

### Hemangioma
- The most common benign liver tumor (70%).
- F : M = 3 : 1; age 30-70; found in right lobe > left lobe.
- Capillary type is found more than cavernous type but capillary is usually under 2 cm with no clinical importance, so what we talk about and meet in real life is cavernous hemangioma.
- Cavernous hemangioma ranges from 1-30 cm but usually under 5 cm; "giant" means over 5 cm.
- Most are solitary; only 5-10% multiple; blood supply from the hepatic artery; the mass has no biliary or portal structures.
- No malignant transformation; fibrous tissue surrounds it; chance of rupture is very rare.
- Most have no symptoms; usually incidental findings on US or CT.
- Note: if size under 5 cm (some say 4-10 cm) usually no symptoms; symptoms in larger ones arise from pressure effect, hemorrhage, thrombosis or torsion.
- CBC, LFT not significant; patients with vague abdominal pain who get US may look like a gallstone but find a well circumscribed homogeneous density mass, needing CT or MRI.
- Gross (hemangioma): round, reddish-purple capsule with a thin capsule covering; feels soft and springy; if firm, suggests thrombosis, fibrosis or calcification. Figure (skip).
- Investigation:
  1. MRI: the single best imaging study: sensitivity 85-95%, specificity 99%. On T1 the mass is darker than surrounding liver (hypointense); on T2 look for blood-filled spaces: the mass is hyperintense or bright. Figure: hemangioma in arterial, portal and delayed phases: progressive centripetal enhancement on Gd-MRI (skip).
  2. CT: sensitivity 75-85%, specificity 96%: 2.1 early (arterial) phase: peripheral nodular enhancement starting at the edge, moving from outside inward to the center; 2.2 delayed phase: the dye spreads through the whole mass, with delayed central filling-in and delayed wash-out: contrast fills slowly from the periphery to the center (centripetal pattern, outside-in) and stays in the mass longer than normal.
  3. 99Tc-labeled RBC scan or hepatic angiography: classic cotton wool appearance: used little now because MRI is far better.
- Diagnosis depends on imaging alone; do not biopsy (bleeding, and whoever biopsies gets hit).
- Treatment:
  1. No symptoms, or few or vague symptoms: observe and follow up with US once a year (homogeneous hyperechoic well-circumscribed mass).
  2. Indications for surgery: 2.1 rapid enlargement or abnormal growth behavior during follow-up; 2.2 symptomatic case, after ruling out other causes such as gallstone and peptic ulcer; 2.3 inability to exclude malignancy; 2.4 Kasabach-Merritt syndrome: found in infants under 1 year or pregnant women: a large hemangioma traps platelets and platelets aggregate until thrombocytopenia, fibrinolysis and coagulopathy (DIC); after resecting the hemangioma the DIC is reversible; 2.5 spontaneous or traumatic rupture: seen very rarely.
  3. Operation: the most used is enucleation along the pseudocapsule (easy to shell out); some prefer resection: per preference; small superficial masses or at segments 2, 3 can be done laparoscopically; large or numerous masses: open enucleation, some need liver resection or OLT. Figures: intraoperative finding; resection (skip).
  4. Indications for embolization or RT: 4.1 high risk for anesthesia and surgery; 4.2 multiple extensive hilar involvement; 4.3 before surgery in a large mass, to shrink it and reduce intraoperative bleeding; 4.4 children; 4.5 spontaneous bleeding.
- Hemangioma in children: usually multifocal; small ones often resolve by themselves; if no symptoms observe; a large mass can have AV shunt problems with congestive heart failure: treat as suitable: steroid, or RT, or embolization.
- Cautions: 1. size of the hemangioma is not an indication for surgery; 2. even though the main blood supply is the hepatic artery, do not treat with hepatic artery ligation because embolization works and is non-invasive.

### Focal Nodular Hyperplasia (FNH)
- The second most common benign liver tumor.
- F : M = 9 : 1; relation with the pill is less than hepatic adenoma.
- Many believe the cause is embryonic malformation of arterial structure causing disturbed liver blood flow followed by hyperplasia and a regenerative response of liver tissue.
- Features resemble hemangioma: no symptoms, found incidentally, solitary mass, no complication such as rupture or hemorrhage.
- Symptoms usually from a large mass, or a pedunculated type with torsion.
- In the past FNH was believed associated with fibrolamellar carcinoma, but now it is concluded FNH has no malignant transformation.
- Usually in the right lobe, size under 5 cm, peripheral rather than central.
- Gross: subcapsular lesion reddish-brown, no capsule, round, merging with normal liver tissue; classic: the cut surface has a central scar, believed to result from vascular malformation and thrombosis. Figure (skip).
- Histology: normal hepatocytes in thickened plates, with Kupffer cells.
- Investigation:
  1. CT or MRI: 1.1 non-contrast: low density mass; 1.2 after IV contrast: rapid enhancement in the arterial phase and rapid wash-out in venous and delayed phases; the mass is homogeneous, lobulated, no capsule; 1.3 central scar found about 2/3: i.e. 1/3 without central scar may be hard to separate from hepatic adenoma. Figure: FNH in arterial, portal and delayed phases (skip).
  2. 99Tc labeled albumin scan: FNH has Kupffer cells that take up Tc: hot scan, while hepatic adenoma has no Kupffer cells: cold scan; but beware FNH may lack Kupffer cells in 20%, giving false negative (cold) though it is FNH.
  3. MRI: T2W bright signal central scar; with hepatocyte-specific contrast agent such as gadoxetic acid (Eovist) or gadoxetate (Primovist), normal liver absorbs it, seen at 20 minutes (hepatobiliary phase): FNH is very white, and the normal tissue then excretes the contrast; but in hepatic adenoma, an abnormal liver cell, Primovist is not absorbed, so it does not look white like FNH.
  4. Doppler US: branching of a small central artery like a car wheel (spoke-wheel pattern), specific.
  5. Angiogram: sunburst vascular or spoke-wheel pattern: no longer used.
- Clinical diagnosis is enough; with a clear central scar on CT no need for biopsy.
- Treatment: 1. no symptoms: observe and follow with CT once a year; if on the pill, stop it; 2. indications for surgery: 2.1 symptomatic case; 2.2 rapid growth in size; 2.3 uncertain diagnosis; 2.4 pedunculated FNH (fear of torsion).
- If surgery is needed, a margin-free resection (partial hepatectomy) is preferred over enucleation, since there are often thin veins around the mass; open or lap as convenient.

### Hepatic Adenoma (HA)
- The third most common. Figure: US and gross finding: yellow because of much fat (skip).
- Found mostly in premenopausal women aged 20-40, obese, associated with hyperestrogen state, especially those on the pill over 5 years; a chance of bleeding from CT about 50%, while in men it is associated with anabolic steroids to build muscle, especially athletes; about 78-88% are solitary masses, the rest several.
- Features differ from hemangioma and FNH: symptomatic up to 80%: about 50% have pain, another 30% symptoms from bleeding.
- Bleeding from ruptured tumor (15-30%): usually in masses over 5 cm, or during pregnancy or just after delivery: patient has abdominal pain, shock, low BP; mortality 8%.
- Hepatic vein compression is found rarely; portal vein compression very rare.
- Small foci of HCC are found in 5-10%, so it is believed that if HA is left for long, it may turn into HCC; studies find phenotype/genotype is an accurate predictor of malignancy, especially in cases with beta-catenin mutation.
- From the above, an expert meeting in France in 2017 divided hepatic adenoma into subclasses by genetic change into 4 types:
  1. HNF-1 alpha gene inactivation hepatic adenoma (HHA): found 30%, mostly in women, with steatosis, may be several, with low chance of cancer; follow up with MRI once a year for at least 5 years.
  2. Beta-catenin exon 3 gene mutation (10-19%): mostly in men; chance of cancer up to 45%.
  3. Inflammatory type (50%): mostly in obese women, with liver inflammation and much blood supply, so bleeding is frequent.
  4. Unclassified: group with no clear genetic relation: observation is not very safe: usually must be treated.
- Currently it is divided into 6 subclasses; the author thinks that is beyond what we need to know now.
- MRI findings of each subclass (table, adenoma vs HCC; T1WI, T2WI, chemical shift sequence, T1 C+ Gd): inflammatory subtype: T1 iso- or mildly hyperintense to liver; T2 "atoll" sign; chemical shift: minimal or no signal drop-off; Gd: strong arterial enhancement with persisting enhancement in portal venous and delayed phase. HNF-1 alpha-mutated subtype: T1 iso- or hyperintense to liver; T2 iso- or mildly hyperintense to liver; chemical shift: diffuse signal drop-off; Gd: moderate arterial enhancement with washout in portal venous and delayed phase. Beta-catenin-mutated subtype: T1/T2 homogeneous or heterogeneous hyperintense to liver depending on hemorrhage/necrosis; no signal drop-off; Gd: strong arterial enhancement that may or may not persist in portal venous and delayed phase. HCC: variable T1; usually hyperintense T2; signal drop-off when intracellular fat present; heterogeneous arterial enhancement with washout in portal venous and delayed phase. As the author says, MRI can tell only the subclass with rather specific features, i.e. HHA with much fat shows diffuse T1 signal drop-out; other subtypes have no specific MRI features. The trouble of molecular subclassification is that tissue must be obtained.
- Investigation:
  1. CT or MRI: like FNH but no central scar, because the HA is supplied by the hepatic artery, so contrast enters fast and exits fast; now MRI at 20 minutes (delayed hepatobiliary phase) is the best imaging study, using the fact that normal liver parenchyma takes about 20 minutes to take up Primovist (high signal intensity): 1.1 uptake: shows liver cells in the mass: found in FNH and dysplastic nodule; 1.2 no uptake: shows a tumor, e.g. hepatic adenoma, especially the inflammatory type, the most common; other types may lack typical findings. Figure: hepatic adenoma in 3 phases; MRI (skip).
  2. 99Tc labeled albumin scan: cold scan since HA has no Kupffer cells; formerly used to separate from FNH.
  3. Molecular biology and IHC to divide HA into subtypes as WHO 2013 recommends (the author says do not be distracted if not an HBP fellow).
- Treatment:
  1. Observation and follow up with CT or MRI with AFP every 6 months to 1 year for at least 5 years: used when: 1.1 female with mass under 5 cm (Schwartz cut value 4 cm) and asymptomatic hepatic adenoma; 1.2 AFP normal; 1.3 CT or MRI features without suspicion of HCC; 1.4 associated with the pill; 1.5 MRI fits HHA subclass. In observed cases stop the pill (not forbidding sex, but forbidding pregnancy, since the mass may rupture).
  2. Indications for surgery: 2.1 asymptomatic HA over 5 cm (Schwartz cut value 4 cm), but if symptomatic, do not care about size, operate; 2.2 stopped the pill and the mass still does not shrink or grows over 20%; 2.3 cannot stop the pill; 2.4 planning pregnancy; 2.5 complications such as rupture, bleeding; 2.6 exophytic mass from the capsule (fear of torsion); 2.7 male sex (cancer risk higher than female); 2.8 imaging cannot separate adenoma from liver cancer.
  3. Procedure of choice: elective segmental resection, open or lap by size and location, aiming for a free margin about 1 cm; urgent resection only with complications such as bleeding; formal resection like HCC seems excessive, except foci of HCC; enucleation alone seems too little, not suitable. Figures (MRI arterial phase hyperintense lesion; hepatobiliary phase no uptake; resection with free margin; MRI 3 months after resection) (skip).
  4. High-risk patients: send for RFA, or if RFA cannot be done, embolization.
  5. Adenomatosis: more than 4 masses (Cameron: over 10 masses): 5.1 resection of the largest mass with RFA of the rest; 5.2 staged resection; 5.3 OLT if many masses and nothing else works (keep for special cases).
  6. Rupture or active bleeding: 6.1 after ABC resuscitation, treatment should start with angiographic embolization to stop bleeding first, followed by elective resection later; 6.2 if embolization is not available (hospital without it, no one able, or the mass ruptures outside office hours when the interventionist has gone home): the resident faces the situation of having to open the abdomen, suck blood, scoop clots, pack with big swabs, then perhaps ligate the hepatic artery, then call a senior or refer; if the team is ready and the patient stable enough, may continue to resection. In the author's opinion, for residents the answer should be 2-stage more (pack and refer), not too radical.
  - If unsure how to handle: do not be tempted to open; after active bleeding, 2-stage is safer: embolization or packing first, resection later.
- Guideline for hepatic adenoma by molecular subclass using liver biopsy (still debated) (figure), as steps: hepatocellular adenoma: stop OC and androgen, weight loss. Male: high rate of complications: surgery. Female: HCA under 5 cm: HNF1A HCA at MRI: follow-up; other HCA: tumor biopsy: B-cat exon 3 and shHCA (sonic hedgehog): high rate of complications: surgery; other HCA: follow-up (if increase in size: surgery). HCA over 5 cm: surgery (if no regression after OC stop); other option? (dashed).
- Table (HA vs FNH): sex: HA F >>> M; FNH F > M. Pill: HA 3+; FNH 1+. Malignancy: HA positive; FNH none. Symptom: HA positive 80%; FNH rare. Rupture: HA positive; FNH none. Gross: HA intrahepatic separation; FNH peripheral blending. Histology: HA hepatocyte without bile duct; FNH hepatocyte with bile duct. CT or MRI: HA without central scar; FNH with central scar. 99Tc scan: HA cold; FNH hot. Surgery: HA usually required; FNH symptomatic case.

## General Rules for Benign Liver Diseases
1. Usually female, incidental finding, solitary, asymptomatic, required observation and follow-up.
2. Symptomatic and with malignant transformation: HA and neoplastic cyst.
3. Screening investigation: US.
4. One best investigation: MRI.
5. Biopsy is not necessary.
6. Usually requiring surgery: HA, neoplastic cyst, hydatid cyst; and an interesting one: bile duct hamartoma: from abnormal development of small intrahepatic bile ducts (bile ductules) with fibrous stroma, which may be found at any exploration of the abdomen for any reason: grossly it is whitish, hard, with an irregular surface, looking very much like metastatic colorectal cancer, so wedge it out for examination; if possible send frozen section in the OR.
- Table (differential diagnosis of benign hepatic neoplasms; cavernous hemangioma / FNH / hepatocellular adenoma): incidence 0.4-20% / 3% / 1%; solitary 80-90% for all; pathogenesis: unknown (vascular malformations, congenital) / unknown (vascular malformations, congenital) / steroid hormones (estrogen, progesterone, androgen); imaging: US, ce-CT, MRI, 99mTc, RBC-SPECT / US, ce-CT, MRI, 99mTc / US, ce-CT, MRI, 99mTc; AFP normal for all; macroscopic: dark purple, soft, compressible lesions well demarcated and frequently surrounded by a thin capsule (blood-filled "cyst") / light brown, well-circumscribed lobulated tumor without capsule (central scar) / well-circumscribed masses with a pseudocapsule of compressed hepatic parenchyma, areas of inhomogeneous yellow-brown lipid-rich tissue, hemorrhage, necrosis, calcifications; microscopic: cavernous vascular spaces lined by endothelium separated by connective tissue, totally lacking biliary or portal structures / normal hepatocytes arranged in thickened plates, with biliary ductules derived from bile ducts at the interface between fibrous bands and nodules, totally lacking actual bile ducts / large lipid- and glycogen-containing hepatocytes arranged in plates separated by dilated sinusoids fed by arterial perfusion; treatment: surgical resection only if symptomatic or over 10 cm / discontinue estrogens, periodic imaging / discontinue estrogens, resect if possible (malignant potential).

## Malignant Tumors of the Liver
- Secondary (metastatic) tumor : primary tumor = 20 : 1.
- Both primary and secondary tumors are fed by the hepatic artery, but on contrast CT they look different: primary tumors are mostly hypervascular, while secondary tumors are mostly hypovascular.

### Primary Malignant Tumors
- Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is found most often; the rest are found less, e.g. hepatoblastoma, angiosarcoma; read later; residents should know a little bit.

## Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)
- M : F = 4 : 1.
- Risk factors:
  1. Chronic liver disease: 1.1 HCC is found with cirrhosis 84%: alcohol, hepatitis B and C; 1.2 chronic hemochromatosis (iron load in the liver); 1.3 non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD): now very strong; some reports find NAFLD a greater risk factor for HCC than HCV.
  2. Hepatotoxins: aflatoxin, nitrosamine, arsenic, vinyl chloride, anabolic steroids, etc.
  3. Alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency.
  4. Malnutrition, low protein diet, or kwashiorkor.
  5. Obesity (thin or fat both a risk, as printed).
  6. Primary biliary cirrhosis. 7. Glycogen storage disease. 8. Hepatitis B carrier without cirrhosis.
- Hepatitis B carrier: the chance of dying of cirrhosis and HCC is up to 50 times; besides, about 70-80% of chronic HBV patients with HCC have cirrhosis; hepatitis A is not a risk factor (remember).
- Half-believed risk factors: Wilson's disease, male smokers, alcohol.
- Spread: HCC spreads in every direction: direct extension, vascular (especially PV and HV). Note: HCC goes via lymphatics less than via vascular; if a liver mass with big lymph nodes is found, beware intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.
- Clinical manifestation: early cases are hard to diagnose; 25% are diagnosed at the late stage; 75% have no symptoms, or non-specific ones such as abdominal pain, weight loss, anorexia, or fever from necrotic tumor; some have paraneoplastic syndrome such as polycythemia, hypoglycemia and hypercalcemia.
- Diagnostic studies (choose only 2 to know):
  1. Tumor marker: alpha fetoprotein (AFP).
  2. Imaging: contrast-enhanced CT and MRI; percutaneous biopsy and angiography are not popular any more.
- AFP: an oncofetal antigen normally high in the fetus in the mother's womb; after birth AFP declines rapidly, and may rise again during pregnancy: in HCC with cirrhosis about 75% have high AFP; in HCC without cirrhosis only 1/3 have high AFP. If AFP over 20 microgram/L is used in cancer diagnosis, sensitivity is only 60%; higher values do not relate to tumor size, staging and prognosis. AFP is also high in teratocarcinoma, yolk sac tumor, chronic liver disease, active viral hepatitis, IBD; for metastatic carcinoma from pancreas and stomach AFP may be higher than normal; but metastasis from the colon or cholangiocarcinoma has normal AFP, with colon usually high CEA, while cholangiocarcinoma has high CA 19-9. Attempts to find more specific tumor markers, e.g. AFP-L3, exist but sensitivity is not good and detection rate low, so not suited to screening.
- Contrast-enhanced CT (ceCT): should be dynamic contrast-enhanced CT, viewing all 3 phases with IV iodinated contrast: 1. arterial phase: image at 5-15 seconds; 2. portovenous phase: image at 60-90 seconds; 3. delayed phase: image at 5-15 minutes. Figure: HCC CT phases and gross specimen (skip). The CT machine should be a high-quality model; when reading CT look for cancer features, tumor extension, whether the liver parenchyma is hard or empty, nodes, and atrophy and hypertrophy of the liver; CT sensitivity about 83%, specificity 91%.
- Features suspicious of HCC: 1. vascular enhancement in the arterial phase: non-contrast phase hypodense; in the arterial phase rapid vascular enhancement because the main blood supply of HCC is the hepatic artery; in the portovenous phase hypodense again (washout); 2. cirrhosis, ascites, big spleen; 3. a mass pushing the liver capsule out (intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: capsule retracted, dimpled); if the capsule is not intact and the patient has abdominal pain, shock: a ruptured HCC; 4. no enlarged lymph node; 5. mosaic pattern from necrosis with fatty change; 6. vascular or bile duct invasion.
- MRI: very useful in diagnosing liver mass, and suits cases unclear on CT: sensitivity and specificity close to CT; the author prefers MRI, viewing sequences like CT plus the hepatobiliary phase at 20 minutes; contrast: a hepatocyte-specific agent such as Primovist, taken up by normal liver and excreted via the bile duct.
- PET scan: should be done for secondary tumors from the colon or sarcoma group.

### Screening and Surveillance
- In cirrhotic patients, regenerative nodules turn to cancer by low-grade dysplasia then high-grade dysplastic nodule and finally HCC; screening high-risk patients reduces HCC mortality by 37%.
- Criteria for selecting patients to screen (APASL 2017): 1. patients with cirrhosis from any cause, men and women; 2. chronic hepatitis B carriers: 2.1 African over 20 years; 2.2 Asian men over 40 years (like AASLD); 2.3 Asian women over 50 years; 2.4 family history of HCC (a first-degree relative with liver cancer); 3. chronic hepatitis C with pathology showing fibrosis stage 3 or 4.
- Method: most guidelines (NCCN, EASL, APASL) agree surveillance is AFP + US liver every 6 months as best screening, except AASLD who advise US alone. Figure: surveillance guidelines: NCCN: AFP/US every 6-12 months; AASLD: US every 6 months; EASL: AFP + US every 6 months; APASL: AFP + US every 6 months.
- APASL 2017 uses US as screening test, while contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is used as a diagnostic test like CT or MRI.
- Interpretation: if AFP over 200 ng/mL, or US abnormal with a mass larger than 1 cm, indistinct border, marginal hypoechoic and mosaic pattern (irregular internal tumor echoes) with poorly defined margin: send CT or MRI. Figure (skip).
- Comparing CT with MRI: MRI has slightly higher sensitivity than CT, especially in cirrhosis; studies on contrast to enhance liver nodule for early HCC diagnosis are ongoing.
- Classic imaging: rapid arterial hypervascularization with portovenous or delayed phase washout. Figure: arterial and portal venous phase (skip).
- CT recommended now: 4-phase MDCT: 1. non-contrast phase; 2. arterial phase; 3. portovenous phase; 4. delayed phase.
- MRI (works very well; residents should view): 1. phase T1 (in and out of phase): where signal loss occurs it is fat; 2. T2 heavy fat saturation: whether the lesion is true or a pseudolesion; 3. T1 dynamic enhancement with gadolinium: look at the vascular enhancement pattern, both fill-in and washout; 4. then 20 minutes later a delayed phase with Primovist or gadoxetic acid, a hepatic specific contrast taken up by normal liver parenchyma: 4.1 normal liver and FNH take up Primovist; 4.2 HCC and hepatic adenoma do not take it up.

### Screening and Surveillance of AASLD Update 2018
- Accepted that early HCC resected has 5-year survival 75-80%.
- Screening and surveillance programs in USA and Europe have criteria for selecting high-risk patients, slightly different from China, especially cirrhosis with HCC incidence over 0.2% per year; surveillance reduces HCC mortality by 37%.
- AASLD 2018 criteria: adults with cirrhosis having the highest risk of HCC should enter screening and surveillance to be cost-effective:
  1. Hepatitis B carrier: 1.1 with cirrhosis; 1.2 Asian male aged 40 or more, Asian female 50 or more; 1.3 African over 20 years; 1.4 family history of HCC.
  2. Hepatitis C with cirrhosis or bridging fibrosis.
  3. Cirrhosis from: 3.1 alcoholic cirrhosis; 3.2 hemochromatosis.
  4. Surveillance benefit uncertain: 4.1 hepatitis carrier under 40 in men or 50 in women; 4.2 hepatitis C and stage 3 fibrosis; NAFLD without cirrhosis.
  5. No surveillance in Child C cirrhosis not on the transplant waiting list.
- AASLD 2018 recommends screening and surveillance with US alone every 6-12 months; AFP is not mandatory: because AFP in cirrhosis has low sensitivity and specificity; if sending AFP, the cut-off value is 20 ng/mL.
- AASLD is researching a phase 3, 4 biomarker to replace AFP; screening with CT and MRI is too costly and not worthwhile except when US is unsure. AASLD concludes: whenever US finds a mass larger than 1 cm, proceed to a diagnostic study.

### Diagnosis
- AASLD 2018: use multiphase CT or multiphase MRI, either one; routine biopsy is unnecessary.
- Interpretation of diagnostic imaging with multiphase CT or MRI: use LI-RADS 2018.

### LI-RADS 2018
- Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System: radiologists categorize images in high-risk HCC patients so reports are standard; guidelines consist of: 1. US surveillance; 2. diagnosis with CT, MRI or contrast-enhanced US; 3. assessment of treatment response with CT or MRI.
- LI-RADS diagnostic categories: LR-1 definitely benign; LR-2 probably benign; LR-3 intermediate probability for HCC; LR-4 probably HCC; LR-5 definitely HCC; LR-5V definitely HCC with tumor in vein; LR-M probably malignant, not specific for HCC. Note: LR-NC means not categorizable (like BI-RADS 0 for breast).
- To decide whether a nodule is LR-3, LR-4 or LR-5 with HCC risk, use arterial phase, washout, capsule and threshold growth: Arterial phase hyperenhancement: non-rim-like enhancement in the arterial phase unequivocally greater than the liver. "Washout": non-peripheral reduction in enhancement in the whole or part of the lesion (hypointensity/hypoattenuation compared with parenchyma). Capsule: smooth, uniform border around most or the entire lesion, visible as an enhancing rim in portal venous, delayed and/or transitional phase. Size: largest outer-to-outer dimension, including capsule if present. Threshold growth: size increase by at least 5 mm AND: 50% or more in 6 months or less, OR 100% or more in more than 6 months, OR previously (within 24 months) not seen on MDCT/MRI, now 10 mm or more.
- Table (LI-RADS category by size and features): arterial phase hypo- or iso-enhancement: diameter under 20 mm: no major feature LR-3; one feature LR-3; two or more LR-4; diameter 20 mm or more: none LR-3; one LR-4; two or more LR-4. Arterial phase hyperenhancement: under 10 mm: none LR-3, one LR-4, two or more LR-4; 10-19 mm: none LR-3; one LR-4 or LR-5 (as printed); two or more LR-5; 20 mm or more: none LR-4; one LR-5; two or more LR-5. (Features counted: washout, capsule, threshold growth.)
- Management by category (multiphase CT or MRI): negative: return to surveillance in 6 months. LR-NC: repeat or alternative diagnostic imaging in 3 months or less. LR-1: return to surveillance in 6 months. LR-2: return to surveillance in 6 months; consider repeat diagnostic imaging in 6 months or less. LR-3: repeat or alternative diagnostic imaging in 3-6 months. LR-4: multidisciplinary discussion for tailored workup; may include biopsy. LR-5: HCC confirmed; multidisciplinary discussion for consensus management. LR-M: multidisciplinary discussion; often biopsy. LR-TIV: multidisciplinary discussion; may include biopsy. If biopsy: pathology diagnosis.

### AASLD Approach (2018, same as before)
1. If screening US in a patient with cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B finds a mass under 1 cm: this small nodule more likely a cirrhotic nodule than HCC, and even if HCC the blood supply is not enough to say it is cancer. AASLD advises follow-up with repeat US every 3 months for 1.5-2 years; if unchanged, return to standard surveillance; if enlarging, proceed as for a nodule over 1 cm. NCCN advises follow-up with CT or MRI with IV contrast or contrast-enhanced US (CEUS) every 3 months for 1.5-2 years; if unchanged standard surveillance; if enlarging treat like over 1 cm.
2. Mass over 1 cm in a cirrhotic patient: 4-phase MDCT or dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI: if arterial hypervascularity or arterial enhancement brighter than the adjacent liver (not always homogeneous, may be heterogeneous) with washout in venous or delayed phase (darker than adjacent liver), plus AASLD adds threshold growth and capsule appearance: fits HCC, treat as HCC.
   - 2.1 If the result is inconclusive, do a 2nd study: if the first was CT then MRI; if MRI first then CT.
   - 2.2 If the 2nd study is still inconclusive, biopsy (biopsy has less role than imaging).
- Figure (algorithm): mass on surveillance ultrasound in a cirrhotic liver or with chronic HBV: under 1 cm: repeat US at 3-month intervals: stable over 18-24 months: return to standard surveillance; enlarging: proceed according to lesion size. Over 1 cm: 4-phase MDCT/dynamic MR: arterial hypervascularization and venous or delayed-phase washout: positive: treat as HCC; negative: other imaging modality (CT or MRI) or biopsy: if positive treat as HCC; if negative repeat as above.
- NCCN says deciding HCC needs both CT and MRI, advising both; if classic enhancement on either, core biopsy. The author's view: the AASLD approach has more acceptance (but in Thailand AFP can also be used: AFP over 400 means chance of HCC over 90%).
- Another group, Bruix and Sherman (Hepatology 2011), uses 2 ways: 1. cytological or histological diagnosis: needle biopsy, done less now except in patients planned for OLT; 2. imaging diagnosis: dynamic CT or MRI without biopsy: HCC diagnosis relies on non-invasive criteria: focal lesion larger than 2 cm (AASLD uses 1 cm) with one imaging technique showing arterial hypervascularization with rapid venous washout, on CT or MRI either one; in non-cirrhotic patients diagnosis relies on CT or MRI features together with AFP over 200 ng/mL.

### Staging
- Many systems exist but none is the best; AASLD 2018 recommends Barcelona (BCLC staging).
- Okuda staging: used in the past (1984) when HCC was found as big masses; now imaging finds small masses, so Okuda looks too simple. Method: the more positive factors, the worse. Table: tumor size: negative under 50% of liver volume; positive over 50%. Ascites: absent / present. Serum albumin: over 3 / 3 or less. Serum bilirubin: under 3 / 3 or more. Okuda 1: no positive factor; Okuda 2: 1-2 positive factors; Okuda 3: 3-4 positive factors.
- BCLC staging (Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer): staging with great support in both America and Europe; besides the liver, BCLC considers other factors: performance scale, ECOG, liver condition, Child-Pugh classification; other staging (Chinese, French, Italian, TNM) are not worth remembering: they do not work because sometimes a small HCC without N and M has a patient in bad condition, bedridden, or a hard liver at end-stage liver disease, and formal resection could kill them.
- However, the choice of treatment should not look at BCLC alone: patients in stage B, per BCLC, are not operated: but in real life, even a fairly large liver mass of 8-10 cm in a well-compensated cirrhosis (bilirubin under 1.1 mg/dL, no evidence of portal hypertension such as esophageal varices, ascites, thrombocytopenia, portal vein gradient not over 10 mmHg) can have liver resection and live longer; the textbook's later parts summarize criteria for resection decisions.
- Because of this stage B problem, a Hong Kong group subclassified stage B into B1-B4 (the author thinks wait for BCLC to be updated better).

### The BCLC 2018
- AASLD 2018 recommends this; not much different from 2011; HCC is still divided into 3 big groups as before: stage 0, A-C, D; but for explanation they divide into 5 groups:
  1. BCLC-0 or very early stage: truly early (some call it CA in situ): 1.1 single mass size 2 cm or less; 1.2 preserved liver function: well-compensated cirrhosis without portal HT, no invasion of major vein, no extrahepatic metastasis; 1.3 ECOG PS 0, Child-Pugh A. Treatment: if Child-Pugh A, PS 0: resection is the treatment of choice, 5-year survival 90%; if the condition is not suitable for resection: ablation with RFA or PEI as alternative, or if meeting transplant criteria send for liver transplant.
  2. BCLC-A or early stage: 2.1 single HCC under 5 cm; 2.2 if multiple, not over 3 nodules, the largest not over 3 cm; 2.3 preserved liver function, no invasion of major vein such as IVC, SMV, main portal vein, no extrahepatic metastasis; ECOG PS 0, Child-Pugh A or B. Treatment: 1. Child-Pugh A, PS 0 with a single mass: should do resection; 2. multiple masses, Child-Pugh B or C: fits Milan transplantation criteria: send for liver transplant; 3. those who refuse: RFA or PEI.
  3. BCLC-B or intermediate stage: 3.1 single large HCC but size not specified, or multifocal disease with more than 3 nodules, the largest over 3 cm, without vascular invasion or extrahepatic spread; 3.2 ECOG PS 0, still preserved liver function, Child-Pugh A or B. Treatment: BCLC does not recommend resection in this stage: treatment is TACE (chemoembolization); in our country after TACE, evaluate the patient's condition against liver status: some cases consider liver resection if Child-Pugh score not over 7, bilirubin under 2, no signs of portal hypertension and the remaining liver is enough; so the textbook answer is TACE, but in practice treat case by case; reports from Asia, especially our resection in the intermediate stage, show survival better than TACE.
  4. BCLC-C or advanced stage: 4.1 regardless of size or number, with portal vein invasion or extrahepatic spread but preserved liver function; 4.2 ECOG PS 1-2; 4.3 Child-Pugh B. Treatment: sorafenib as the main drug; another approach is palliative combined treatment, e.g. resection of the cancer mass with RFA, TACE, chemo (FOLFOX4), RT; reports, especially the Hong Kong group, say patients treated this way live longer, up to 2 years; they say better than doing nothing, where the patient dies in 4 months.
  5. BCLC-D or end stage: 5.1 severe impairment of liver function, major vein invasion or Child-Pugh C; 5.2 ECOG PS 3-4: cannot help themselves, in bed over 50% of the day. Treatment: symptomatic treatment and best supportive care.
- Debate topics in BCLC 2018:
  - Intermediate stage: the stage with the most problem as people try to subclassify: the most famous is the Bolondi classification, dividing BCLC-B into B1 to B4 with criteria to select some cases for liver transplant; the disadvantage is that Bolondi did not set criteria for which cases can have liver resection, so Kudo modified Bolondi into the Kinki BCLC-B subclass: the interesting is the B1 subclass: those with Child-Pugh score 5-7 who can be resected: after resection they live longer than 4 years, better than TACE; so in the future a new guideline is likely to come out emphasizing BCLC-B.
  - Everyone accepts the group treated for cure is BCLC stage 0 and 1; but in Thailand we meet only advanced cancer, rapidly growing masses, or spread into major veins or already to the lungs; the textbook answer is not to operate, but the Thai practice (members of the HBP club) follows BCLC but also operates; the author notes there will certainly be cases referred in, with the mass nearly 10 cm, some with many masses. If asked in an oral, think about whether the patient can be operated: whether intermediate or advanced stage, if operable, operate: survival is better than TACE.
  - Practice now, by trend: 1. BCLC-C meeting Hong Kong criteria that can be resected together with adjunctive treatment: may operate; if not operable, give sorafenib; 2. spread to one other organ, e.g. lung, and the liver mass is resectable: try resecting both (the author does not agree much); 3. Major vein invasion: 3.1 invasion into the portal vein (a CT in the example showing right portal vein invasion): by BCLC guideline exactly, no operation; 3.2 in our country practice resembles the Japanese: if invasion enters PV or HV but lies next to the cancer mass to be cut out and looks resectable, resect; 3.3 if invasion goes from the cancer mass into main PV, IVC or atrium: not worth operating; everyone agrees: treat symptomatically. Figure: tumor thrombus in the portal vein (skip). 4. HCC with biliary tumor thrombus: if obstructive jaundice, do PTBD on the opposite liver side first; then evaluate the patient and liver: if the patient can be operated and the remaining liver is enough, take them to operate: they live longer than without surgery.

### Management of HCC in NCCN
- Looks operable if: 1. Child-Pugh A, B, no portal hypertension, no major vascular invasion, the future liver remnant still enough for resection (major vascular invasion is controversial in NCCN); 2. Child-Pugh C fitting Milan criteria: recommended liver transplant.

### Management of HCC in Schwartz
- Since editions 9-11 no change; the guideline resembles NCCN. Figure (algorithm), as steps: HCC identified: resection candidate? yes: resection for non-cirrhotic/Child's A, single lesion, no metastasis. Transplant candidate? yes: OLTx evaluation (1 lesion 5 cm or less, or 3 lesions 3 cm or less, Child's A/B/C, no gross vascular invasion, no metastasis): LDLT if suitable donor; UNOS list (cadaver) with MELD score: wait over 3 months: neoadjuvant therapy RFA/TACE/90Yttrium then OLTx. ### Treatment Options for HCC
1. Curative treatment: 1.1 surgical resection; 1.2 liver transplantation (OLT); 1.3 ablative treatment (also has a role in palliative intent).
2. Palliative treatment: 2.1 transarterial chemoembolization (TACE); 2.2 RT and stereotactic body RT; 2.3 laser, microwave ablation and cryoablation; 2.4 chemotherapy; 2.5 targeted therapy; 2.6 immunotherapy.
- Figure (management options and therapies for primary hepatic neoplasms): Surgical resection: major hepatectomy, minor hepatectomy (anatomic or wedge), laparoscopic liver resection, total hepatectomy with orthotopic liver transplantation. Ablation (operative, laparoscopic or percutaneous): radiofrequency ablation, microwave ablation, laser hyperthermia, cryotherapy, irreversible electroporation. Nonoperative: chemotherapy (systemic therapy, TACE, hepatic artery infusion (HAI)); radiotherapy (yttrium-90 intraarterial radioembolization, stereotactic external-beam radiotherapy); chemical therapy (percutaneous ethanol injection).

### Surgical Resection
- Only 20% of HCC are suitable for curative resection: it should be Child A with well-preserved liver function.
- If the mass is very large, TACE first may be needed; when the mass shrinks enough to operate, return to cut it later.
- Curative resection of HCC should be an anatomical resection, since HCC likes to spread along veins; the margin should be free, at least 1 cm; now an RCT comparing margin 1 cm vs 2 cm found 2 cm free margin has better survival than 1 cm (Cameron 2017 p.361); and while operating, blood loss must be kept to the minimum.
- Technique is written later.

### Standardized Terminology of Hepatic Resections
- 3 main systems: 1. Couinaud 1957: most like it since easy to understand, but now not much used; 2. Goldsmith and Woodburne 1957: similar to Couinaud, losing popularity; 3. the international group (liver, bile duct, pancreas) met in Brisbane in 2000 and defined new terms for liver resection, so use these:
  3.1 Extended right hepatectomy or right trisectionectomy: resect segments 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 (about 80% of liver).
  3.2 Right hepatectomy or right hemihepatectomy: segments 5, 6, 7, 8 (65%).
  3.3 Right anterior sectionectomy: segments 5, 8. 3.4 Right posterior sectionectomy: segments 6, 7.
  3.5 Extended left hepatectomy or left trisectionectomy: segments 2, 3, 4, 5, 8.
  3.6 Left hepatectomy or left hemihepatectomy: segments 2, 3, 4 (35%).
  3.7 Left lateral sectionectomy: segments 2, 3. 3.8 Left medial sectionectomy: segment 4.
- Others: caudate lobe resection = removing segment 1; central hepatectomy = removing segments 4, 5, 8.
- Table (nomenclature of the most common major anatomic hepatic resections): segments V-VIII: Couinaud right hepatectomy; Goldsmith and Woodburne right hepatic lobectomy; Brisbane right hemihepatectomy. IV-VIII: right lobectomy; extended right hepatic lobectomy; right trisectionectomy. II-IV: left hepatectomy; left hepatic lobectomy; left hemihepatectomy. II, III: left lobectomy; left lateral segmentectomy; left lateral sectionectomy. II, III, IV, V, VIII: extended left hepatectomy; extended left lobectomy; left trisectionectomy.
- Second-order division (based on bile ducts and hepatic artery): right anterior section = segments 5, 8: right anterior sectionectomy; right posterior section = segments 6, 7: right posterior sectionectomy; left medial section = segment 4: left medial sectionectomy or resection of segment 4 or segmentectomy 4; left lateral section = segments 2, 3: left lateral sectionectomy or bisegmentectomy 2, 3.

### Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG)
- A patient performance status grading used to help choose treatment, especially for major surgery: is the patient suitable for the operation, can they tolerate it.
- Popularly used in esophageal cancer and HCC patients. 4 grades:
  - Grade 0: very good condition, active, can run 5 comfortably.
  - Grade 1: can do normal work, walk around, restricted in strenuous physical activity.
  - Grade 2: can work normally 50% or more (alive in bed less than 50% of the day); can walk around, light exercise; heart problem but body can still use life well [as printed].
  - Grade 3: limited self-care; in bed or chair over 50% of the day.
  - Grade 4: completely disabled; cannot care for themselves at all; bed or wheelchair 100%, needs help.
  - Some add grade 5: dead.
- Grade 0-1: usually tolerate major surgery; grade 2 can still tolerate it but is more tired afterward; grade 3-4: not suitable for major surgery: operating may kill them; for HCC choose non-surgery.

### Liver Transplantation (OLT)
- Normally HCC should choose resection as first choice, but in Child B or C, resecting the liver usually causes liver decompensation: this group should have transplant, since it treats HCC and the underlying cirrhosis too.
- Remember: do not take a resectable Child A case for transplant instead: liver donors are scarce and must be used worthwhile.
- Milan criteria for selecting patients for OLT (still believed in 2017): 1. patient tolerates surgery; 2. Child B or C cirrhosis; 3. no vascular invasion; 4. no extrahepatic metastasis; 5. single mass under 5 cm; 6. if multiple, not more than 3 masses, each under 3 cm; 7. bonus: not a ruptured HCC. Figure: Milan criteria: 1 lesion 5 cm or less; 2-3 lesions none over 3 cm; absence of macroscopic vascular invasion and extrahepatic spread (skip).
- Those waiting for transplant may use TACE or local ablation to buy time, if they do not die first; with good selection, OLT 5-year survival is 75%.
- In 2009 the Mazzaferro group issued "up to seven" criteria, an expanded Milan: 1. the size of the largest mass + the number of masses must total not over 7 (e.g. largest 6 cm, exceeds old Milan, but with one more nodule of 1 cm the sum is not over 7: acceptable); 2. no microvascular invasion. With up to seven criteria, OLT 5-year survival 71.2%.
- Yao (Editor-in-chief of Shackelford, UCSF) came out with expanded selective criteria (UCSF) for OLT, long-term data 5-year survival similar to Milan (75%): 1. single lesion 6.5 cm or less; 2. up to 3 lesions, the largest 4.5 cm or less and total sum of all 3 not over 8 cm.
- Table (liver transplantation criteria): Milan's: single tumor 5 cm or less; or up to 3 tumors 3 cm or less. UCSF: single tumor 6.5 cm or less; or up to 3 tumors 4.5 cm or less and total sum 8 cm or less. Silva et al: up to 3 tumors 5 cm or less and total sum 10 cm or less. All 3 criteria lists: no vascular invasion; no regional nodal/distant metastasis.
- Interesting: Shanghai criteria: not over 3 masses, the largest not over 5 cm and total of the 3 not over 9 cm (if a single mass, up to 9 cm), 5-year survival up to 76%. There are also Kyoto criteria of Ito, the 5-5 rule of Sugawara, Hangzhou criteria of Zheng, Toronto criteria of DuBay: not memorizing.
- 2 types of transplant: 1. primary liver transplantation: OLT from the start; 2. salvage liver transplantation: treatment starting with liver resection; if tumor recurs and the patient cannot have liver resection again (e.g. liver decompensation), the 2nd treatment is liver transplant.
- After transplant recurrence of tumor in the graft about 10-20%, mostly within 2 years; if the mass is resectable with enough future liver remnant: resect; if not: RFA or TACE; if nothing works: try sorafenib.

### Ablative Treatments
- Another line of treatment: besides HCC, metastatic tumor in the liver also benefits; many methods:
1. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA): the most used ablative treatment: a needle with an electrode at the tip is inserted into the tumor; radio waves cause alternating current in the cells giving heat energy 60-100 C for about 5 minutes: coagulation necrosis of cancer cells; CT shows a black necrosis zone. Figure: RFA probe and zone (skip). The heat circle is about 2-5 cm, average 3 cm, so the suitable mass size is about 3 cm. Newer probes may burn a mass up to 7 cm but wait for data; answering in 2019: 3 cm best; maximum not over 5 cm; larger than 5 cm use chemoembolization rather than RFA. RFA may be used with resection, or instead of resection in extensive disease or limited hepatic reserve; before, during, or recurrence after resection; or in patients waiting for OLT.
   - Choose RFA for patients with: 1. mass size not over 3 cm; 2. surrounded by liver tissue and lying deeper than about 1 cm from the liver surface; 3. avoid RFA near the hilar plate (bile ducts tolerate heat poorly; burning causes bile duct stricture: do not); 4. avoid RFA near major blood vessels, portal vein, hepatic vein, hepatic artery or vessels over 3 mm in diameter, since heat is lost to blood flow (heat sink effect): use RFA for masses more than 2 cm from major vessels; 5. avoid the position near the diaphragm (diaphragm injury); 6. avoid the inferior edge of the liver (bowel injury).
   - Do not use RFA in: 1. uncontrolled sepsis; 2. coagulopathy; 3. severe debilitation expected to die within 6 months; 4. liver failure.
   - RFA gives complete ablation 50% if the mass is under 2.5 cm; sometimes one burn makes the mass disappear; reports 1, 3, 5 and 10 year survival 90%, 60%, 38% and 23% respectively. Figure: RFA steps (skip). Compared with cryotherapy: RFA takes less time and costs less.
2. Cryoablation: percutaneous or open abdomen, using a cryoprobe with liquid nitrogen in contact with the tumor, using cold to freeze until cell protein denatures. Suits peripheral superficial lesions under 3 cm; each freeze takes 30-40 minutes per mass; with many masses it is long, hard for the young. Disadvantages: many complications: bleeding, bile duct injury, myoglobinuria, renal failure and cryoshock (release of many cytokines causing multiorgan failure ending with DIC): so not popular for HCC.
3. Interstitial laser hyperthermic ablation and microwave coagulation: use heat from laser or microwave to burn the tumor: results not better than RFA.
4. Percutaneous 95% ethanol injection (PEI): causes coagulative necrosis of the tumor: suits masses under 2 cm, the smaller the better, maximum not over 5 cm, and peripheral lesions: the problem is pain, needs several injections since efficacy is lower than RFA; still used for masses not suitable for RFA, e.g. near major vessel, bile duct and gallbladder.

### Transarterial Chemoembolization (TACE)
- Suits large HCC 5-7 cm, or multiple masses that cannot be resected or ablated by RFA, or those awaiting a transplant; based on the thinking that the liver tumor is fed by the hepatic artery: place a catheter in the hepatic artery at the take-off of the gastroduodenal artery, then bury an implantable continuous infusion pump in the subcutaneous pocket of the abdominal wall. Then inject chemo (often doxorubicin) mixed with iodized oil (lipiodol) through the catheter to run directly into the tumor, followed by gelatin sponge or polyvinyl alcohol to block the vessel temporarily, so chemo stays in the tumor longer and embolization makes the tumor shrink, so some become operable; TACE reduces systemic complications of chemotherapy.
- Indications for TACE: 1. unresectable HCC; 2. insufficient FLV (future liver volume); 3. ruptured HCC; 4. patient refusing surgery or in poor condition: if operated, will die.
- Contraindications for TACE: 1. complete main portal vein thrombosis (remember); 2. IVC thrombosis, atypical hepatic artery anatomy; 3. Child-Pugh class C or severely compromised LFT (hepatic encephalopathy) or significant ascites; 4. bilirubin over 3 mg/dL (remember); 5. severe cardiac or renal insufficiency (creatinine over 2 mg/dL); 6. biliary obstruction; 7. diffuse or massive HCC: tumor volume over 50% of the liver; 8. extrahepatic metastasis; 9. anaphylactic reaction to the contrast media or chemotherapeutic agent; 10. severe leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy that cannot be corrected; 11. acute upper GI bleeding (recent variceal bleeding).
12. Ongoing systemic infection. 13. ECOG performance status over 2.
- Before TACE look at the CTA in portovenous phase for an arterioportovenous shunt: if a shunt exists, embolize to occlude the shunt before TACE.
- Response rate of TACE up to 80%, but repeat treatment every 3-6 months; TACE failure means after TACE twice, each time not over 8-10 weeks apart, the mass still enlarges, or major vascular invasion or spread outside the liver appears: give sorafenib in addition.
- Radioembolization with intraarterial yttrium-90 injected into the tumor is an option for unresectable cases.

### Radiotherapy and Chemotherapy
- Results are not yet very good, since the tumor is resistant; looks a bit better than oral antineoplastic drug; RT helps reduce pain from HCC bone metastasis.

### Targeted Therapy
- Drugs that block signaling pathways that cause HCC and vascular endothelial growth factor pathways, so blood does not feed the tumor.
- The drug used: sorafenib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that blocks tyrosine kinase receptors, inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and tumor angiogenesis.
- Give 400 mg bid continuously until the patient cannot tolerate it, or the disease progresses (stop). Side effects that may be fatal: toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) with sloughing of skin like a burn; but the classic rash of sorafenib is a red rash at the palms and soles (hand-foot skin reaction) that becomes blistered and peels; sorafenib also causes diarrhea.
- Result: those on sorafenib live a mean 6.5 months; those not on it 4.2 months; the author says he is not on anyone's side: taking sorafenib is only a little better than not taking it, but compared with chemo they say to take it: better than lying around waiting, though it also costs money so one should consider carefully: consider complications, and the price, whether suitable for Thailand.

### Hepatic Resection
- Major hepatic resection means resection of 3 segments or more.
- About 200 years ago, in the reign of Rama III, the ancient teacher said the liver is a "don't touch" organ: whoever operates on the liver should prepare to be a ghost.
- Nowadays the liver can be touched freely: surgeons cut it easily; operative mortality should be under 5% now; Fan's group reports their own major hepatic resection mortality 0%.
- Aim of hepatic resection: R0 with free margin over 1 cm; best if the portal territory of the segment or lobe can be removed entirely while preserving maximal functioning liver parenchyma; for HCC margin over 1 cm since tumor cells spread along veins often.
- Preoperative assessment: major hepatic resection is a heavy procedure: assess it well; a death would be bad; before surgery assess 3 big issues:
  1. Patient assessment: fitness of heart, kidney, lung; if one cannot even tolerate a skin lesion removal, and ECOG 3, 4: residents do not meddle.
  2. Tumor assessment: whether the tumor is resectable: by CT, ideally spiral; look at: 2.1 size; 2.2 number; 2.3 segment location; 2.4 invasion of PV, HV, IVC, hepatic artery and bile duct (Doppler US helps with suspected venous involvement); 2.5 rupture; 2.6 cirrhosis, atrophy or hypertrophy; 2.7 extrahepatic disease.
  3. Liver assessment: hard liver, fatty liver, especially liver function: assess whether the remaining liver after resection will be enough to work.
- Criteria for resectability (by CT): 1. no distant or extrahepatic metastasis; 2. anatomically resectable disease, not bilobe involvement; 3. no tumor thrombus in main PV, IVC or right atrium (except tumor thrombus in the bile duct, which is not a contraindication); 4. after resection, adequate liver functional reserve.
- Mass size under 5 cm with very few daughter nodules: good chance of R0.
- PV involvement: the Japanese do not agree much: they believe a small PV invasion, if the cut looks fine, go ahead and cut.
- CT cannot say for sure that HCC is resectable; the decision of unresectable disease should be made in the OR after careful dissection and looking at everything.
- Preoperative diagnosis: tissue diagnosis is not necessary first, since biopsy risks hemorrhage and tumor dissemination; if resectable, resect; use AFP and MRI or good quality CT.
- Liver assessment: normally the liver regenerates in number and size of hepatocytes from the first postoperative day; what to beware is whether the remnant liver is usable; the surgeon must assess well:
  1. Cirrhosis or hepatitis? fatty liver? A patient who had chemo before: be careful with liver resection in colon cancer with metastasis who got 5-FU, irinotecan, oxaliplatin: liver function worsens: play safe: stop the drug at least 4-6 weeks before resection.
  2. Function: very important in assessing hepatic functional reserve:
     - 2.1 Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) classification: still popular and in BCLC; the author likes it since it is easy and works; but accuracy has some problems.
     - Table (CTP): bilirubin (mg/dL): under 2 = 1 point; 2-3 = 2; over 3 = 3. Albumin (g/dL): over 3.5 = 1; 2.8-3.5 = 2; under 2.8 = 3. Ascites: absent 1; moderate 2; severe 3. Encephalopathy: absent 1; moderate (grade 1-2) 2; severe (grade 3-4) 3. Prothrombin time: seconds prolonged: under 4 = 1; 4-6 = 2; over 6 = 3; INR: under 1.7 = 1; 1.7-2.3 = 2; over 2.3 = 3. Child A = 5-6 (good risk); Child B = 7-9 (moderate risk); Child C = 10-15 (poor operative risk).
     - Reported: even Child A who can have more than 3 segments resected but with macronodular cirrhosis, after major hepatic resection: the remaining liver is the part with poor cirrhosis so hepatic encephalopathy can occur; Child B if it must be cut should not exceed 2 segments; if Child C: do not meddle, except if doing liver transplantation.
     - Residents should remember the CPS parameters; beware parameters not involved that may trick you, such as SGOT, SGPT and nutritional status.
     - 2.2 MELD score (model of end-stage liver disease): popular to indicate prognosis of liver transplant, can be used for hepatic resection too but must be calculated carefully with many parameters: score under 10 OK; 10-15 liver condition fair; MELD over 15 no different from CPS-C; the problem that MELD is so complicated makes it less popular than CPS.
     - 2.3 Indocyanine green (ICG) clearance test: a quantitative dynamic test used much by the Japanese: assess liver function by clearing indocyanine: inject IV; ICG then goes to liver cells, which take it up, metabolize it and excrete it in bile 100%; we look at the 15-minute value of ICG: in normal people with normal liver function the liver clears ICG until under 10% remains in blood; if ICG remains, the more, the worse the liver function.
     - 2.4 CT/MRI volumetry: calculates the volume of liver removed per segment and the liver remaining after resection: the calculation is time-consuming and error-prone: if the mass is small, multiple, or bile ducts in the liver are large, the calculation is wrong; CT volumetry cannot say whether the remaining liver, which should have enough volume, works well: measures only anatomy, not function (the author's opinion: do not memorize; in practice not working well).
     - 2.5 Hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG): used by the Europeans: if HVPG over 10 mmHg shows portal hypertension: should not resect the liver; this is too invasive and does not work well either.
- ICG clearance test: not popular in the USA; but since our friends in Japan, e.g. Machiuchi, use it a lot and bring recommendations, residents should know the rules:
  1. Do not do major hepatic resection if: 1.1 patient cannot control ascites; 1.2 serum total bilirubin 2 mg/dL or more.
  2. Enucleation only if: 2.1 serum total bilirubin 1.6-1.9 mg/dL; 2.2 ICG 15 of 40% or more.
  3. If no ascites, or ascites controllable, and normal total bilirubin: how much liver can be removed: look at ICG 15: 3.1 under 10%: can cut 3 segments comfortably, or extended right hepatectomy is still tolerated; 3.2 10-19%: can cut 2 segments, e.g. left lateral sectionectomy (2, 3); 3.3 20-29%: can cut only 1 segment; 3.4 30-39%: limited resection, free margin only; 3.5 40% or more: enucleation only.
- CT volumetry: measuring volume of the liver from 3D images then calculating by formula the resected tissue and future liver remnant (FLR); the problem is FLR gives only volume, not quality: if the remaining FLR is a poorly functioning part, after resection the liver can still fail. In 5-star centers (the author's phrase), they assess everything, especially cases with poor liver, spending much money.
- Practical approach for Thailand (what the author uses every day): 1. clinically no signs of portal hypertension (big spleen or EGD varices) or chronic liver disease, e.g. no jaundice, spleen not big, no ascites; 2. CBC: platelet over 100,000; 3. LFT: bilirubin under 2, albumin over 3, AST under 2 times; 4. coagulogram PT, PTT, INR normal; 5. overall Child-Pugh class A; 6. CT shows complete resection or R0; 7. patient condition able to tolerate the OR: not very ill, not bedridden.

### Portal Vein Embolization (PVE) or Ligation
- Before major hepatic resection the surgeon must be sure that the remnant liver after cutting will regenerate enough to work: in academic terms, there must be an adequate future liver remnant (FLR). The rules:
  1. If the liver is normal, smooth surface, not nodular: FLR 25-30% is enough.
  2. If over 30%, most people live well after surgery; in about 2-3 weeks the remaining liver regenerates volume enough to work.
  3. But if cirrhotic liver, FLR over 35-40% is needed.
- In cases where a patient before surgery looks like the case would be too risky: if the remnant liver cannot cope, e.g. by CT the future remnant is small: if opened and found R0 resection possible but expected to die postoperatively: send the patient for PVE on the side of cancer first, to cause hypertrophy of the lobe without tumor.
- PVE can increase liver volume 10-45%, and about 85% of patients who had PVE can have major hepatic resection.
- Before PVE, Makuuchi advises TACE first: to control tumor growth by reducing arterial supply and occlude arterioportovenous shunt; he says if not done, when PVE blocks the PV, more blood runs to that lobe via the hepatic artery, making the tumor grow fast and damaging.
- Approach of PVE: 1. percutaneous transhepatic route: the most popular, with reports of increasing FLR more than approach 2; 2. transileocolic PVE: needs general anesthesia, laparotomy, to cannulate a vein.
- After PVE, when can resection be done: 1. normal liver: wait about 2 weeks; 2. cirrhotic liver: wait 4-6 weeks: Makuuchi: 3-6 weeks after PVE; Schwartz says 4 weeks.
- Contraindications for PVE: 1. unresectability: tumor spread everywhere in and outside the liver; 2. coagulopathy that cannot be corrected; 3. portal vein invasion already; 4. bile duct obstruction, which must be treated first; 5. portal hypertension; 6. renal failure needing hemodialysis.
- Causes of PVE failure: 1. concurrent liver cirrhosis; 2. unresolved jaundice; 3. incomplete occlusion; 4. arterio-portal shunt.
- Caution about FLR: even if after resection the remaining liver is more than enough, liver failure can still happen, because FLR tells only volume, not the function of the remaining liver.

### ALPPS
- Classic Associating Liver Partition and Portal Vein Ligation for Staged Hepatectomy: another way to increase FLR, increasing faster than PVE, published 2012; the purpose is to resect a liver mass without leaving the remnant too small (Schwartz 2019 p.1386 writes about it).
- Method takes only 7-10 days then the liver can be cut; but ALPPS is a 2-staged liver resection: the patient must be cut twice. Figure: ALPPS steps (skip).
  - First stage: open, ligate the PV on the side with tumor to send blood to the remaining liver part, together with splitting the liver along the umbilical fissure; wait about 10 days (1-2 weeks) to re-assess the volume of the remaining liver by CT; if enough, resect the cancer side.
  - Second stage: resect the diseased lobe.
- Reports show many patients have both morbidity and mortality: not very impressive.

### Surgical Techniques for Hepatic Resection
- Preparation before surgery: 1. improve nutrition, deep breathing exercise, chest PT; 2. talk with patient and relatives much: forbid promising that the liver can be cut for certain or "zero mortality"; teach relatives that birth, aging, sickness and death are normal: complaints taken to court happen while the doctor took full care with good intention: sin.
- Preparation 1 day before: 1. soft diet; 2. SSE or Fleet enema in the evening; 3. blood and FFP ready, warm before use each time.
- Prophylactic antibiotic: broad spectrum cephalosporin IV at induction of anesthesia.
- VTE prophylaxis: consider LMWH about 12 hours before surgery, continue until the patient ambulates.
- Intraoperative monitoring: 1. pulmonary artery catheter if resources (rich hospital) or at least an arterial line; 2. for the rest of us: a CVP line alone: cut down or C-line puncture; if lucky with a good anesthesiologist measuring CVP at the internal jugular vein; so that the operation is comfortable, maintain CVP under 5 cmH2O: bleeding is less (low CVP anesthesia).
- Position and draping: 1. supine, arms tucked; 2. prep and drape from lower chest to lower abdomen.
- Incisions: 1. start with right subcostal incision like open cholecystectomy; then assess the abdomen: look at the liver: if the liver is not hard, no ascites, no extrahepatic metastasis: extend the incision to the left as bilateral subcostal incision. Figure (skip). 2. The author prefers adding a vertical incision (Mercedes Benz). 3. Thoracoabdominal incision suits a large right lobe tumor, but by experience the author never needs it. 4. Retractor: if rich, use a Thompson, otherwise a Bookwalter; the author modifies by tying the lower blade with roll gauze to the bed head.
- Intraoperative assessment: besides looking and palpating, use IOUS if possible; the operator must be skilled. Advantages of IOUS: 1. detects tumors as small as 0.5 cm not seen on CT (reports say adding detection of small tumors 5-50%); 2. shows major vascular involvement and intravascular thrombus; 3. confirms the line of resection; 4. with experience, benign and malignant can be separated; 5. helps in dye staining technique by injecting dye into a portal vein branch.
- Liver mobilization: cut the hepatic ligaments enough to lift the liver and bring it up over the abdominal wall; when manipulating the liver tissue it must be gentle to reduce tumor cell dissemination via PV and HV; often the mass sticks to the diaphragm, chest wall; mostly stuck tight: do not peel apart; tumors rupture this way: resect en bloc the mass with diaphragm, cutting 2 cm away from the edge.
- Minimize blood loss techniques:
  1. Inflow control:
     - 1.1 Pringle maneuver: clamp 10-15 minutes, release 5-10 minutes (to limit ischemia-reperfusion injury of the liver); Lau 2010 advises clamp 10 min, release 10 min, once only: IR injury is less (the author's aside: the teacher, a fast liver surgeon, needs only a few clamps).
     - 1.2 Selective intermittent clamping: dissect the portal pedicle, separate the branches of PV and hepatic artery and clamp only the side to be cut: hemihepatic inflow control; some even control inflow only to the segment to be cut.
     - 1.3 Total hepatic vascular occlusion (Heaney technique): control both inflow and outflow at once: clamp suprahepatic IVC and infrahepatic IVC above the adrenal vein, together with Pringle; blood loss small, but the problem is reduced venous return to the heart and cardiac output down about 50%; anesthesiologist must be good; use only for a tumor stuck to the IVC.
     - Note: choose by preference; reported complication rates do not differ.
  2. Outflow control: ligating the HV inside or outside the liver. Suggestions for ligating the right hepatic vein outside the liver: 2.1 do not hurry, since the right HV is short; if torn, or the ligature slips, bleeding is severe; the author uses double ligation; 2.2 ligating the HV before Pringle clamping beware congestion and more bleeding from the raw surface.
  3. Low CVP: bleeding during resection is usually venous: ask the anesthetist to keep CVP about 5 cmH2O; BP stable and urine about 25 cc/hr is OK. Anti-fibrinolytic drug tranexamic acid or procoagulant such as recombinant factor VIIa may reduce blood loss (data not better than none).
  4. Liver hanging maneuver: dissect between the right HV and middle HV in front of the IVC about 11 cm, then pass a tape to lift the liver up; suits the anterior approach; beware when dissecting in front of the IVC: if the end is clamped or tears the IVC, serious.
  5. Venovenous bypass: divert blood not through the liver like the OLT technique.
  6. IOUS: to avoid dissecting major blood vessels and to find tumors not seen on CT: saves blood.
- Remark: if bleeding is much during surgery and emotion is high, the resident should take a deep breath, pack 2-3 swabs or press by hand, then slowly continue.
- Parenchymal transection: the most bleeding step:
  1. Low tech: finger fracture, knife handle, or suction tip: finger fracture breaks the liver tissue (the author does not like it since a tumor may rupture or the finger may enter the tumor, causing tumor contamination); the author likes suction tip with a small clamp (clamp crush technique): slowly ligate and cut vein, artery and bile duct in 15-30 minutes; reviews (2009) say low tech results do not differ from high tech; the difference lies in bleeding and surgical complications; the author uses the clamp.
  2. High tech: CUSA, harmonic scalpel, ligature, water jet, or RFA and microwave: depends on hospital affordability.
- Transection approach: 4 patterns:
  1. Blumgart, Starzl, Fan and the Hong Kong group's method: start by dissecting the hilum, ligate and cut the portal vein, hepatic artery and bile duct of the side to be removed: clear demarcation line appears; when ligating, ligate near the liver to avoid including anomalies; 1.2 then ligate the right HV outside the liver; then transect the liver; this technique is beautiful, suits teaching residents, but wastes time on hilum dissection. Figure: Blumgart technique (steps 1-4) (skip).
  2. Modified Blumgart: dissect like Blumgart (PV, HA, bile duct first), transect the liver, then ligate the HV inside the liver: advantage: easy HV control; disadvantage: back bleeding from the HV.
  3. Anterior approach: technique of Foster and Ton That Tung: split the liver anteriorly down to the IVC: suits a large mass or one that cannot be mobilized or is stuck to the diaphragm: disadvantage: bleeding much, surgeon operating in the dark, tiring.
  4. Lanois technique: 4.1 start with cholecystectomy, then dissect right along the GB bed to the PV, hepatic artery and bile duct of the side to remove, in the liver; 4.2 then transect the liver, then enter and ligate the HV in the liver; the author assesses the length of the right HV first: if it can be ligated and cut, do it before splitting the liver.
- Raw surface management: principle: complete hemostasis and biliostasis; many approaches: 1. Starzl: open and leave, do nothing, only a good suction drain; 2. some use argon beam coagulator on the raw surface then leave it; 3. some use gel foam, surgicel or fibrin glue; 4. some suture, ligate, cauterize, clip, or staple; 5. the author's way: use a round needle of 0 chromic suture, interlocking, before dividing, then divide; at the raw surface cauterize, ligate or suture until dry; then cover with omentum (omentoplasty) as in liver trauma.
- Bile leak testing: some insert a small feeding tube in the cystic duct during cholecystectomy; after liver resection inject methylene blue to see whether bile leaks; the author uses a swab pressed on the raw surface: wherever bile stains green, suture or ligate; various topical agents do not reduce bile leak; after surgery if the drain shows green fluid, send drain fluid bilirubin: if over 3 times serum bilirubin, suspect bile leak: treatment depends on whether major or minor leakage (read in biliary surgery).
- Drainage: place closed suction drains 2: right subphrenic and under the remaining liver.
- Others: some do falciform ligament repair, especially the Hong Kong group, because they fear torsion of the portal pedicle causing liver necrosis (the author thinks it too much).
- How the author does it: Kocher, then assess, Mercedes incision, mobilize, cholecystectomy, Pringle, Lanois-type split, cover with omentum, drain, close.
- Postoperative care: 1. after major hepatic resection hypoglycemia, hypoalbuminemia and transient jaundice occur; 2. bilirubin and jaundice slowly fall and return to normal after about 3 weeks; 3. give glucose as 10% dextrose until oral intake; 4. give albumin 25-50 gm/day for 7-10 days until serum albumin over 3; 5. broad-spectrum antibiotic; 6. avoid morphine; 7. beware complications: bleeding, bile leak, infection or abscess; 8. correct coagulopathy with FFP, platelets, RBC: the cause of coagulopathy after major hepatic resection is often massive transfusion more than vitamin K deficiency. Remember: ammonia level does not change after major hepatic resection.
- Factors determining survival after HCC resection: the problem is recurrence after surgery, usually in the liver; 78-95% of remaining recurrence within 5 years, from intrahepatic metastasis since the start, only undetectable. Factors for recurrence: 1. vascular invasion: the single most important; 2. tumor size over 5 cm; 3. multiple masses, satellite nodules; 4. presence of symptoms and absence of tumor capsule; 5. poorly differentiated histology; 6. positive resection margin; 7. male patient (as printed: ผู้ป่วยโทรม, i.e. debilitated patient); 8. much blood transfusion (believed to suppress host antitumor immune mechanism); 9. surgeon manipulation until the tumor ruptures.
- Follow up: if the patient is not yet dead from surgery: appoint to follow up: 1. history, examination and AFP every 3 months for 2 years: if the HCC had high AFP before surgery; but if HCC with normal AFP, follow up with CT or MRI abdomen every 3-6 months for 2 years instead; 2. if 2 years and still alive with no recurrence: follow up once a year until death from something else or until the doctor tires.

### Laparoscopic Liver Resection
- Now used for benign and malignant liver tumors: reports of success in left lateral segmentectomy and partial resection; now more and more, even major resection.
- Techniques: pure laparoscopy (75%) and hand-assisted liver resection (16%).
- Those who do it report tumor-free margin, oncologic outcome and overall survival no different from open; pain less, blood loss less, return home faster; the problem is higher cost of equipment than open, but total cost, because of fast discharge, falls about 50%.
- Those who do lap must be good: those who do few and rely on a camera only: the author advises choosing open.
- Those who do lap hepatectomy: Schwartz says should have done at least 60 cases.
- Selective criteria (may change in the future as more experts appear): 1. Child A, B; 2. tumor: 2.1 size under 5 cm (pedunculated under 6 cm); 2.2 solid or nodular type; 2.3 location in anterior segment 4b, 5, 6 or lateral segment 2, 3; 2.4 mass not abutting hilar structure, major hepatic vein, IVC.
- Contraindications: 1. cannot tolerate pneumoperitoneum; 2. dense adhesions that cannot be lysed; 3. lesion too close to vasculature; 4. lesion too large to be safely manipulated laparoscopically.
- Robotic liver resection: reports in stages, and a little, certain to see more.

## Ruptured HCC
- Found 2-3% of liver cancer: suspect in a patient with a history of hepatitis or liver inflammation: sudden onset epigastric and RUQ pain, distended abdomen, maybe peritonitis with hypovolemic shock; why a liver cancer ruptures: believed from the cancer growing and necrosis, or invasion of hepatic vein so that the efferent blood is blocked and liver pressure rises until rupture.
- Cases with no symptoms or signs of acute blood loss because it is a subcapsular or intraparenchymal hematoma, or rupture sealed by clot tamponade.
- Work up: easy: start with US (a simple test) with paracentesis if free fluid, with abdominal tap yielding blood in a patient with no trauma: think of rupture of a tumor such as HCC: if the patient can tolerate, send CT.
- CT signs suggesting ruptured HCC: 1. hypervascular mass or mass close to the liver capsule or protruding with hemoperitoneum and surrounding hematoma; 2. subtle rim enhancement or enucleation sign; 3. densely enhancing peripheral rim with marginal break; 4. extravasation of contrast. CT helps give details of the liver, rupture site and the blood in the abdomen. Figure: CT of ruptured HCC in segment IV with active contrast leakage and adjacent blood clot (skip).
- Do not send MRI because it takes too long and is unsuitable in an emergency.
- Management of ruptured HCC: immediate treatment follows ATLS resuscitation: ABC like trauma, correct coagulopathy; then divide patients into 2 groups:
  1. Unstable (falling BP, bleeding on and off): follow:
     - 1.1 Should be conservative, letting go in peace if: 1.1.1 moribund patient or in a terminal event, e.g. already brain-dead; 1.1.2 malignant cachexia: advanced cancer, only skin over bones; 1.1.3 terminal liver failure CTP class C with hepatic encephalopathy; 1.1.4 extrahepatic metastasis, e.g. ascites full of carcinomatosis.
     - 1.2 If not fitting those criteria (not letting go): if no signs of ongoing bleeding, observe; if still bleeding and looks like they can fight, choose a way to stop it:
       - 1.2.1 Transarterial embolization (TAE): the treatment of choice first: if lucky bleeding stops, giving time to recover, then resect the next days; before doing check that there is no complete main PV occlusion.
       - 1.2.2 If not stable enough for embolization, or nobody can do it (out of hours interventions often have problems; ruptured cancer likes to burst out of hours): we must open before the operation; talk with relatives to understand that the patient has a high chance of death; then open and rush to scoop out blood, using swab packing even if it does not work much; at least it slows the bleeding, since the ruptured tumor tissue is friable like soft bean curd; but pack a lot; better than doing nothing; stay calm, control emotion, use calmness to settle the commotion; then find a way to stop bleeding: suture (often cuts through), alcohol injection, burning with RFA may help; or the commonly used: resectional debridement: cut out only the ruptured nodule without a standard segmentectomy; some can be packed only and left to survive; if they survive it is merit.
       - Hepatic artery ligation: the liver often necroses; or attempting to suture-ligate the ruptured mass: poor choice.
       - If the patient survives: evaluate tumor staging whether resectable: assess the liver, if resectable, with a ready patient and doctor, that is a suitable case for delayed hepatectomy.
  2. Stable: liver rupture but stable (tamponade effect): rush a work-up: look at the condition of the patient and liver, whether spread or not; if not, consider elective surgery later.

## Other Primary Malignant Tumors of the Liver
- Residents should know for exams; the teacher dares to ask 3: 1. fibrolamellar variant of HCC (FLHCC); 2. angiosarcoma; 3. hepatoblastoma.

### Fibrolamellar Variant of HCC (FLHCC)
- Found in women as often as men, younger than HCC: FLHCC at age 20-40; not associated with viral hepatitis; the liver looks normal, no cirrhosis.
- A solitary fairly large mass with clear border; cut section shows central scar (40%) like FNH. Figure: gross specimen with central scar and necrosis (skip).
- CT/MRI of FLHCC: heterogeneous mass with central scar and central calcification, but the central scar does not enhance; unlike FNH, where there is no calcification but the central scar enhances. Figure: CT (skip).
- Management: 1. surgical resection like HCC, but FLHCC resectability rate up to 95% since the remaining liver is normal, so survival is better: mean 5-year survival about 60%; some advise lymph node dissection together since FLHCC spreads to nodes more than classic HCC (awaiting data); OLT is not very suitable since there is no cirrhosis, so resection should be done. 2. Chemotherapy: FLHCC responds to chemo fairly: 5-FU with interferon alpha 2b, response rate up to 62%.
- Table (classic HCC vs FLHCC): character: mass with cirrhosis vs mass with normal liver; M : F 2-8 : 1 vs 1 : 1; median age 55 vs 25; tumor invasive vs well-circumscribed; resectability under 25% vs 50-75%; cirrhosis 90% vs 5%; AFP positive 80% vs 5%; hepatitis B positive 65% vs 5%.

### Angiosarcoma
- A cancer of the blood vessels of the liver; found 1-2%; M : F = 3 : 1; mean age 60-70 years; associated with chemical carcinogens, especially vinyl chloride and arsenic; when it involves the liver: usually several masses, in both lobes. Figure (skip).
- Very aggressive, rarely resectable; RT and chemo are not useful: prognosis very poor, about 6 months to death.

### Hepatoblastoma
- Found in boys about 2 years old: the most common primary liver tumor in children; no association with cirrhosis; a large solitary mass with well-defined border; AFP high 90% but LFT usually normal. Figure (skip).
- Diagnosis by CT and MRI.
- Because the mass is large: neoadjuvant chemotherapy to increase resectability; if resected: 5-year survival 60-90%.

## Surgical Treatment of Hepatic Metastases
- By TNM, cancer that has spread to the liver is stage 4, with a chance of seeing Phra Indra (dying) within 1 year.
- Now surgeons are more experienced, imaging studies are better and diagnose quickly, anesthesiologists use low CVP technique; results are good when patients suited for R0 resection are selected, especially CRC with liver metastasis, with long-term survival not only 5 years but 15 years.
- Hepatic resection is now used more widely, not only CRC: also neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and non-colorectal non-neuroendocrine liver metastases, e.g. breast, stomach cancers: for which cancer benefits from liver resection, do know with confidence.
- Purpose of hepatic metastasectomy: 1. curative intent: especially CRC and NETs give good results; 2. palliative intent: 2.1 pressure symptoms: pain, gut obstruction from the mass; 2.2 complications such as rupture or bleeding; 2.3 reduce hormone effect in NET patients.
- Hepatic metastases are classified in 3 groups: 1. colorectal liver metastases (CRLM); 2. non-colorectal neuroendocrine liver metastases (NCNE); 3. non-colorectal non-neuroendocrine liver metastases (NCNN): 3.1 GI, e.g. stomach, pancreas; 3.2 non-GI, e.g. breast, melanoma.

### Colorectal Liver Metastases (CRLM)
- The liver is the primary organ receiving portal vein blood, so colon and rectal cancers spread to the liver often; 70-80% of CRC metastasis occurs in the liver; and about 30% of CRC hematogenous spread goes to the liver alone.
- About 25% are synchronous lesions: liver metastasis already found when CRC is diagnosed; 25% are metachronous lesions: liver metastasis found later: if the liver mass appears within 1 year it is early metachronous; after 1 year, late metachronous.
- If not treated, mean survival 6-12 months; with chemo about 11-18 months; at most not over 3 years; RFA 3-year survival 40%.
- 25% of CRLM can have R0 resection; if cases are well selected, operative mortality under 2%, 5-year survival 45-60%, 10-year survival 20-28% (not bad: surgeons may relapse elsewhere before).
- Evaluation: 1. colonoscopy to screen for a 2nd primary CRC and rule out anastomotic recurrence: if done within the last 6 months, no need to redo; 2. survey for extrahepatic disease: 2.1 contrast-enhanced CT of abdomen and pelvis: with spiral CT features: a low-attenuation mass with irregular border in portal phase compared with surrounding liver, with slow washout; 2.2 chest X-ray or CT chest: choose one: NCCN guideline cheers CT; 2.3 PET scan: still debated whether needed: those who cheer say PET reduces unnecessary operations by 29% (so do PET in every case); those who say unnecessary note PET specificity is not even 90%, so do not send.
- Sensitivity of imaging in detecting liver metastasis: transabdominal US 40%; non-contrast CT 50%; ceCT 60-75%; spiral ceCT 80-85%; MRI 80-85%; IOUS 90-96% (the best: remember).
- Laparoscopy and laparoscopic US (LUS): like PET, still debated whether to put a scope in: those who cheer say it reduces unnecessary operations by 31%, especially finding tiny peritoneal seeding so the case can be dropped, saving time. Against: 1. non-invasive imaging is much clearer now; 2. criteria for resecting CRLM have widened even with extrahepatic disease: if en bloc removal is possible, go on; 3. previous abdominal surgery: adhesions make it hard to see all; 4. after laparoscopy there are reports that the disadvantages besides pain and anesthetic risk include rapid tumor growth: port site recurrence, peritoneal seeding and rapid tumor recurrence after surgery: believed because cancer likes the CO2 environment and the insufflation increases tumor cell contamination: so use it only in selected cases where imaging is doubtful or only tissue diagnosis is needed.

### Criteria of Unresectable CRLM
- Before 1994, if the 4 following were present, hepatic resection was not done: 1. more than 4 masses in the liver; 2. masses in both lobes; 3. sure free margin less than 1 cm; 4. presence of extrahepatic disease.
- After 1994, criteria reduced: aim to resect all intra- and extrahepatic disease leaving a future liver remnant of at least 20% (or two contiguous segments) while preserving adequate vascular inflow, outflow and biliary drainage: for normal liver; not less than 30% if previously on chemo, or with fatty liver or viral hepatitis without cirrhosis; if cirrhosis, the remnant must be at least 40%.
- Newer criteria: 1. even with more than 4 masses or in both lobes, as long as R0 can be done and enough liver remains, if able, let it be done; if the first operation cannot clear all, a second resection later (two-staged hepatectomy) can be considered; if on assessment the liver mass cannot be fully removed, do not operate; 2. resection with negative margin of 1 cm or more; but if the margin is under 1 cm there is no problem since RFA or cryotherapy can be used at the raw surface; 3. even with extrahepatic disease, if all can be removed, survival is prolonged, e.g. 3.1 liver plus lung: resect both; 3.2 liver plus isolated portal vein lymph node metastasis: resect both; 3.3 liver and a recurrence at the anastomosis of the previously resected colon: resect both, and continue the colon if possible; 3.4 locally invasive to diaphragm, bowel, kidney, adrenal: resect en bloc with the liver mass; 3.5 peritoneal metastasis if only partial, not diffuse carcinomatosis: some centers still do cytoreductive surgery + HIPEC.
- Current contraindications to resection of hepatic metastases: 1. diffuse peritoneal carcinomatosis; 2. bone or brain metastasis; 3. extraabdominal lymph node metastasis; 4. multiple unresectable pulmonary metastasis.
- Ways to increase resectability: the aim is to increase FLV so more patients can be operated by 15-30%:
  1. PVE can increase liver volume 26-37% in 4-6 weeks.
  2. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy first line: usually FOLFOX (folinic acid + 5-FU + oxaliplatin) with response rate 70%, especially newer regimens, often with biologic agents such as bevacizumab (Avastin) or gefitinib (Iressa): can down-size and down-stage the mass: reports of liver masses shrinking up to 50%; when neoadjuvant chemo is used with surgery, 5-year survival 37-58%, 10-year survival 16-30%. Figure: downstaging chemotherapy (skip).

### Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy
1. About 15% reach complete pathological response: if the CT finds no liver mass or "disappearing liver metastasis" (DLM), though some advise observation with close follow-up with CEA, MRI or CT every 3 months, most still insist on resecting the liver at the original tumor position before chemo, since patients who respond to chemo have better prognosis than non-responders.
2. Complete response on PET scan does not mean complete pathological response.

### Operative Considerations
1. If anatomical resection is possible, oncologic outcome is better than non-anatomical resection.
2. For non-anatomical resection, get at least a 1 cm free margin.
3. If after resection the free margin is under 1 cm (mostly from surgical technique reasons): use RFA to burn the raw surface of the liver.
4. A normal liver can be resected comfortably up to 70%; Cameron says it can be 85%, leaving 15% (risky somewhat); in general (including the author) take 20% remaining: the remnant must be at least 2 contiguous segments with adequate vascular inflow, outflow and biliary drainage.
5. Wedge resection suits tumors at the liver surface of size not over 2 cm: when wedging, cut a trough-shaped (boat) cut, not V-shaped.
6. When compared, resection gives better results than RFA.

### Surgical Approach
1. Principle: if planning anatomical resection, especially right hepatectomy, make a wide incision, mobilize the liver as much as possible so the liver comes out to the front of the abdomen; look with eyes, palpate with hands, review CT again, and if IOUS is available it is great, but if not, it is not a problem: surgeons in the old days used no IOUS.
2. IOUS is regarded as the gold standard; liver surgeons advise it: advantages (cheerers say): 2.1 detects small deep liver masses; 2.2 can change the surgical plan in 25%; 2.3 guides safe resection, especially near the hilum and hepatic vein; 2.4 finds portal vein branches for dye injection (staining technique). IOUS costs about 20 minutes more; the author uses it sometimes, not always, since he uses fine MRI and anatomical resection; for young, new hands IOUS should be used.
3. Parenchymal R0 resection: remember do not let the tumor rupture, especially those who like finger fracture (cancer ruptures easily): the author likes suction tip with a small-mouthed Kelly clamp as dissector, slightly bleeding liver tissue ligated with silk or clips; surgeons of high-tech preference may use ultrasonic or water jet dissection: no matter.
4. Hemorrhage control: resect with least blood loss by good inflow and outflow control; anesthesiologist helps with low CVP technique: CVP under 5 mmHg and urine output about 25 cc/hr is OK.
5. Two-staged hepatectomy: suits patients with multiple masses in both lobes, called oligometastases, since a one-stage resection might leave too little liver; so before surgery give chemo 2-3 courses: practical approach: 2 ways, starting with preoperative chemo 4-6 times: 5.1 first operation: remove as many masses as possible, then postoperative chemo to control the remaining masses; with regeneration about 3-4 weeks, then do the second complete resection; 5.2 resect the left lobe masses by non-anatomical (multiple wedge) resection, together with right PV ligation, using the same principle as PVE (or postoperative PVE); when the left side hypertrophies, return to cut the right lobe. Figure: two-stage hepatectomy (skip).

### Predictors of Adverse Outcome
- Fong et al. (J Surg Oncol 2010) collected 1,001 patients and gave points (clinical risk score): 1. positive margin 1.7; 2. extrahepatic disease 1.7; 3. number of tumors over 1: 1.5; 4. CEA over 200: 1.5; 5. size over 5 cm: 1.4; 6. node positive at primary: 1.3; 7. disease-free interval under 12 months: 1.3.
- Positive margin and extrahepatic disease give the worst outcome; if neither factor 1 nor 2, look at 3-7 giving 1 point each: score 0-2: survival clearly better than score 3-5; but these factors should not be used to exclude patients from surgery since Fong reported that even patients with 5 factors still had 5-year survival 14% after hepatic resection; to prevent unnecessary operations Fong advises helpers such as PET/CT or diagnostic laparoscopy.

### Repeated Hepatectomy
- CRLM, even after liver resection, still has a 50-60% chance of liver recurrence, so if nothing more is done, all die within 5 years; but re-operating gives 5-year survival up to 16-41%: so if the liver recurs again with no contraindication, one should resect a second time, since it is the only way to long-term survival; choose patients for re-resection with the same criteria as the first resection.

### Synchronous Primary CRC and CRLM
- Means liver metastasis found at the same time as CRC diagnosis or within 6 months after.
- If the colon causes symptoms that make the surgeon anxious, e.g. obstruction, bleeding: treat the colon first.
- If the colon is not very troublesome and can be worked up, consider where the colon mass is, or whether it is rectal cancer, look at the liver for how extensive, and the patient's condition.
- 3 possible options for management: 1. colon first or classical staged approach; 2. resect colon and liver simultaneously; 3. reverse strategy: resect the liver first (liver first), then deal with the colon later.
- If a good patient, an experienced surgeon, and a good team, it can be done safely, with one hospitalization, without raising mortality, especially liver resection not over 3 segments (minor hepatectomy) found together with colon cancer: do both together; but if both colon and liver look extensive, the patient likely cannot tolerate; do in 2 stages: whichever first.
- Colon first: the most used (classical approach): in general start with neoadjuvant chemotherapy 2-3 months then resect the colon; some resect the colon at once and follow with adjuvant chemo another 2-3 months; then, if the patient is still fit, readmit for liver resection (staged approach); used when: 1. patient not fit enough to cut the liver at once; 2. colon with perforation problem; 3. liver problem such that after resection the remaining liver is insufficient; 4. liver is cirrhotic; 5. extensive colon and liver operation together may kill.
- Simultaneous approach: resect colon and liver together: suits patients whose liver resection is a minor hepatectomy, i.e. not over 3 segments, usually choosing to cut the liver first then the colon.
- In 2010 criteria were set for which cases can be done simultaneously: consider: 1. patient factor: age not over 70, good physical fitness, ready to go home; 2. colon factor: R0 resection possible, no obstruction or bleeding problems, lesion not over T3 (not T4), histology not poor or undifferentiated, not mucinous adenocarcinoma, and not a rectal cancer where surgery is more difficult; 3. liver factor: liver resection not more than 3 segments, adequate future liver remnant.
- Liver first (reverse strategy): Brouquet (M.D. Anderson, reported 2010) advises using it when the liver disease is advanced or with several masses while the colon has no symptoms yet: chemo first about 3-6 cycles, then resect the liver, finishing with colon resection; later reports from Mentha, de Jong, Rosa: summary: indication for liver-first: should be locally advanced rectal cancer, where resection is rather complex because chemoradiation neoadjuvant was given, the mass at the colon may shrink, and surgery is hard; Mentha gives chemo first, followed by liver resection, followed by chemoradiation at the rectum, and finally the rectal cancer resection last.
- Practical approach (Cameron JL 12th ed p.362): 1. if colon cancer has obstruction or bleeding: do a staged procedure: 1.1 start with resecting only the colon, then give chemo; 1.2 wait 3-6 months before dealing with the liver. 2. Colon without obstruction or bleeding: start with chemo for 2-3 months to see tumor response; then do one stage or two stage depending on how extensive the colon or liver resection will be: 2.1 should do staged if the colon needs very extensive resection, such as total colectomy, or lower sigmoid with extensive pelvic dissection such as LAR, or rectal cancer needing low LAR or APR, while the liver needs right hepatectomy or extended right hepatectomy: doing it simultaneously will certainly kill; 2.2 the following can be done simultaneously (assess your own ability first): 2.2.1 liver needs right hepatectomy or extended right hepatectomy but colon only right half or extended right half colectomy; 2.2.2 colon and rectum need LAR or APR but the liver only wedge resection or left lateral sectionectomy: can be done together; the surgeon must always assess their own ability; Greenfield 2011 advises if resecting 3 or more segments of liver, staged is better; the author goes with Cameron.

### Metachronous Liver Metastases
- Means liver mass found after diagnosis of CRC: early metachronous: within 1 year after diagnosis or resection of primary CRC; late metachronous: after 1 year.
- Prognosis of metachronous is better than synchronous.
- Investigate beforehand whether the mass to be resected is only in the liver, or if elsewhere there is only 1 more site: oligometastasis, often liver and lung.
- If no contraindication to resection, these cases should be dragged to resection because it leads to long-term survival.
- Now the trend is to give chemo first, then consider resection later.
- For chemotherapy: regimens without bevacizumab can be operated 2 weeks after stopping; but with bevacizumab-containing regimens there is a bleeding disorder problem: wait about 6 weeks.

### CRLM with Pulmonary Metastases
- If possible, resect liver and lung: Miller C., J Am Coll Surg 2007: resect since it prolongs survival; lobectomy has better survival than wedge resection.

### CRLM with Hilar Lymph Node Metastases
- Found 14%; hilar lymphadenectomy still has poor outcome.
- Old belief taught to juniors: if extrahepatic lymph node positive, do not operate: this belief now has more opposition.
- It is still debated; the trend is to operate more, especially positive portal lymph node only (N1 group), giving chemo first; those responding well: operate; but if N2 group positive (celiac node, paraaortic or retroperitoneal node positive): do not resect the liver.

### CRLM with Extrahepatic Metastases
- Elias D. reported in Ann Surg Oncol 2004 on patients with liver mass and extrahepatic spread: results of Elias (a very surgical surgeon who did not care for others' opinions):
  1. Even with hilar lymph node positive, still resecting the liver in 10 cases: 5-year survival 33%.
  2. Even with anastomotic recurrence at the colon and retroperitoneal lymph node positive with the liver mass: resecting liver, colon and nodes entirely in 21 cases: 5-year 37%.
  3. Liver and lung: resecting both liver and lung in 10 cases: 5-year 29%.
  4. Even with peritoneal carcinomatosis: resecting liver in 22 cases: 5-year 16%.
  - Personal opinion of the author: like most, accept items 1, 2, 3; for carcinomatosis still does the liver resection... not much.
- Additional lymph node issue, especially hepatic hilum (hepatoduodenal nodes): in the past positive nodes of this group were a contraindication; collected reports conclude it can still be done; do not allow positive common hepatic, celiac, periaortic nodes only; hepatic resection with lymph node dissection gave 5-year survival 38%.

### Summary of Treatment of Isolated CRLM
- Hepatic resection is first-line treatment with the best results; RFA is used for masses smaller than 3 cm in patients unsuitable for surgery, e.g. cannot tolerate anesthesia: median survival of RFA about 33 months; patients unable to be operated on with masses over 5 cm: chemoembolization as 2nd-line treatment, survival about 20 months.
- Figure (algorithm), as steps: diagnosis of CRC with synchronous liver metastases -> referral to HPB cancer multidisciplinary team -> clearly R0 resectable metastases: single lesion under 2 cm, favourably located, and an easily resectable tumour of the colon: synchronous resection of primary tumour and metastases: 6 months of adjuvant FOLFOX therapy; single lesion over 2 cm unfavourably located, or multiple metastases: 3 months preoperative chemotherapy: synchronous or staged resection of primary tumour and metastases: 3 months postoperative FOLFOX. Unresectable or borderline resectable liver metastases: intensive chemotherapy: resectability achieved? yes: synchronous or staged resection as above; no: continue chemotherapy or palliative care.

### Non-colorectal Neuroendocrine (NCNE) Liver Metastases
- Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are slow-growing tumors that like to spread to the liver, the group of gastroenteropancreatic NETs (GEP-NET): carcinoid and pancreatic endocrine tumors (PETs); whether functioning or nonfunctioning NETs, since the disease course is slow, even with liver metastasis, resecting both primary and metastatic lesions gives significant long-term survival.
- In carcinoid, if symptoms are present they say 85% already have liver metastasis.
- Liver metastases from PETs are often multifocal or miliary pattern, and may not produce hormone like the primary; overall only 10-20% of NCNE are suitable for liver resection.
- Hepatic resection in NCNE has 2 benefits: 1. relieving mass symptoms such as pain from liver stretch or pressure symptoms; 2. reducing hormonal problems, helping quality of life in the final period.
- CT: hypervascular enhancement in the arterial phase and hypovascular in the venous phase; besides CT also do somatostatin receptor scintigraphy (SRS) to rule out metastasis elsewhere; then look at whether the primary NET site is resectable.
- If assessed that the primary can be resected, resect the primary entirely, even PETs at the pancreatic head: if a surgical candidate and Whipple can be done, do Whipple.
- For liver metastases: patient condition expected to tolerate the operation, G1, G2 tumor with spread to the liver only and no other unresectable extrahepatic disease: consider treating liver mass by these rules (Blumgart's guideline): 1. if hepatic resection can remove over 90% of the liver mass, should resect; the remaining mass handled by RFA; 2. if resection removes less than 90%, consider liver transplantation, especially carcinoid: reported to clearly increase survival (remember); 3. if resection with RFA and recurrence occurs again: 3.1 limited recurrence: choose between re-resection and RFA or use both; 3.2 diffuse recurrence: embolization, somatostatin analog therapy, systemic chemotherapy; 4. if the primary cannot be resected or only palliative resection: for liver metastases: 4.1 limited: ablative treatment such as RFA or alcohol injection; 4.2 diffuse: as in 3.2; 5. OLT: besides HCC, NCNE liver metastases also benefit, especially carcinoid tumor (remember).
- Criteria for selecting NCNE patients for OLT: 1. age under 50 and fit (Milan cut-off age 55 or less), the primary tumor should be well differentiated or G1 G2 (low grade NET); 2. primary tumor site must have had complete resection for at least 1 year and have low Ki-67 (under 5%), normal E-Cadherin; 3. no extrahepatic disease, or disease stable for more than 6 months; 4. excessive hormone symptoms not responding to medical treatment; 5. liver involvement not over 50% and resection of the hepatic mass can remove at least 90%; 6. metastatic diffusion at the liver not over 50% of liver volume (Milan criteria).
- Figure (algorithm, hepatic neuroendocrine metastases): staging by CT-MRI-somatostatin scintigraphy: resected/resectable primary NET: over 90% hepatic metastases resectable: resection +/- ablation: recurrence: limited: ablation vs resection; diffuse: embolization or chemoembolization, somatostatin analogue therapy, systemic chemotherapy; under 90% resectable: liver transplantation. Unresectable primary NET: palliative operation as indicated: hepatic metastases: limited: percutaneous vs laparoscopic ablation (RFA, PEI, cryoablation); diffuse: embolization or chemoembolization, somatostatin analogue therapy, systemic chemotherapy.

### Non-colorectal Non-neuroendocrine (NCNN) Liver Metastases
- Groups other than CRC and NETs spreading to the liver, e.g. breast, melanoma, stomach: some try resecting the liver hoping for longer survival, but since few patients, the advice is to follow results for 10-20 years; select patients very highly.
- Pancreas (exocrine cancer): do not meddle: with liver mass survival is not over 7 months: send for a stent. Exception: malignant solid pseudopapillary tumor of the pancreas: a low-grade cancer found in young girls, not very aggressive; even with liver spread, R0 can be done with very good results.
- Kidney, Wilms tumor and adrenal: normally cancers of this group with liver spread mostly die within 1 year, especially primary from the kidney; but if patients are selected well, especially those responding to chemo, resection reported 5-year survival up to 13%. Summary: this group: select patients well, then do it.
- Reproductive tract tumors: in men: germ cell tumor of the testis responding well to chemotherapy benefits from liver resection, except if the primary is pure embryonal cancer (exocrine pancreatic cancer type: do not meddle); in women: ovarian or fallopian tube cancer: cytoreductive surgery to reduce volume as much as possible, including liver resection, is believed to prolong survival.
- Melanoma: if patients are selected as advised, survival is longer, except uveal melanoma: not useful.
- Breast cancer: no clear criteria yet; some resect these patients with R0; those who may benefit (living longer) have these 4 features: 1. isolated liver metastasis only, no other extrahepatic metastasis, except bone that is easily controlled by RT; 2. the breast disease must be controlled: axillary lymph nodes not over N1A; do not meddle with N1B or N2; 3. disease-free interval over 1 year before liver mass is found; 4. before resecting the liver, try a taxane regimen for a few courses, then repeat CT: if the mass does not grow, hepatectomy. If all 4 are met, fewer than 5% of patients would qualify for R0, but if done, 5-year survival up to 24%: interesting; chemo continues after surgery.
- Soft tissue sarcoma: if the primary is GIST, do not resect: give imatinib or chemoembolization; other sarcomas than GIST that go to the liver normally die within 1 year, unless well-selected patients undergo R0: 5-year survival up to 30%.

## Surgical Management of Portal Hypertension
- Portal hypertension: the role of surgery has decreased to only drugs and endoscopist helping stop bleeding: surgeons are happier.
- Normal portal pressure = 5-8 mmHg or not over 25 cmH2O; when portal pressure rises above 8-10 mmHg, portosystemic collateralization begins.
- Portal hypertension means pressure in the portal system over 8 mmHg; when portal pressure exceeds 12 mmHg, like a time bomb, there is a chance of variceal bleeding; to summarize: as long as portal pressure is under 12 mmHg, chance of bleeding from varices is very low.

### Normal Flow of Portal System
- Blood passes through the portal vein (PV) running to the liver to clear waste absorbed from the GI tract; then leaves the liver through the hepatic vein (HV), runs into the IVC, which is the systemic system, then to the right heart. Figure: normal portal circulation (left gastric (coronary) vein, portal vein, pyloric vein, superior pancreaticoduodenal vein, splenic vein, inferior mesenteric vein, superior mesenteric vein, spleen, pancreas) (skip).
- PV and HV have no valve: when cirrhosis occurs the liver becomes hard and shrunken, causing resistance to PV flow, so portal blood has difficulty entering the liver, so it must find a way into the systemic system, i.e. the IVC, by opening shunts or new channels, in 2 flow patterns:
  1. Hepatopetal flow: the portal vein opens small branches called veins of Sappey, running into the liver more, then finding a way to connect to the IVC; this route has no major effect since the vessel size is small.
  2. Hepatofugal flow: when blood cannot run into the liver, the PV blood flows in reverse (reverse flow), opening new portosystemic collaterals in 4 directions (remember) to find its way into the IVC.
- Figure: new collateral channels: azygos vein; esophageal and gastric varices; coronary vein; short gastric veins; gastroepiploic vein; splenic vein; IMV; SMV; superior hemorrhoidal vein; umbilical vein with caput medusae; retroperitoneal collaterals; hemorrhoidal veins (skip).
  2.1 Via the coronary vein or left gastric vein: blood from the PV runs up to the EGJ; when much blood goes here the veins bulge and twist, which we call esophagogastric varices; from the EGJ the blood continues via the azygos vein to the SVC. This portal-systemic collateral is the most problematic, especially bleeding from esophageal or gastric varices.
  2.2 Via the IMV, a portal system vein: runs into the superior rectal vein (also called superior hemorrhoidal vein), then the blood runs down to the lower rectum to the middle and inferior rectal veins, then into the internal iliac vein, then into the IVC.
  2.3 From the PV opening a new path into the umbilical vein: at the umbilicus we see tortuous bulging veins called caput medusae; then flow through superficial veins of the abdominal wall, which is the systemic system, so the anterior abdominal wall is full of superficial veins.
  2.4 Via SMV branches of the portal system opening retroperitoneal collaterals or perilumbar veins of Retzius, then running into the IVC.

### Causes of Portal Hypertension
- By mechanism:
  1. Increased resistance in the portal system:
     - 1.1 Presinusoidal: 1.1.1 extrahepatic: the most common cause in children: portal vein thrombosis; in adults think of thrombophilia such as protein C, protein S and antithrombin 3 deficiency; 1.1.2 intrahepatic: most common is the parasite Schistosomiasis, next congenital hepatic fibrosis.
     - 1.2 Sinusoidal: most common is cirrhosis (from the Greek Kirrhos, meaning dark yellow).
     - 1.3 Postsinusoidal: cause from vascular etiology such as BCS (Budd-Chiari syndrome) and the veno-occlusive disease group.
  2. Increased portal blood flow, e.g. AV fistula.
- Notes: 1. in a child aged 8-9 months with massive UGIB, esophageal varices (EV) is the most common cause, from extrahepatic PV thrombosis, especially in infants who had umbilical catheterization then infection; luckily the child's liver is still alive, so bleeding usually stops with conservative treatment. 2. But massive LGIB in a child of 1-9 months: think Meckel diverticulum bleeding and investigate with a Meckel scan.

### Assessment of Portal Hypertension
- Principles: 1. in a patient diagnosed with cirrhosis, besides HCC surveillance with US and AFP every 6 months, every cirrhotic patient should be sent for upper endoscopy to assess varices; 2. if varices are found, portal hypertension is likely, so evaluate the patient further.
- Evaluation with these tools:
  1. Upper endoscopy: the most important: 1.1 see size and extent of varices and assess bleeding risk; 1.2 look at the gastric mucosa for portal hypertensive gastropathy.
  2. Imaging studies: Doppler US, CTA or MRA to see the portal venous system: its shape and where it goes; if needing TIPS or a shunt operation, this helps choose correctly.
  3. LFT: too basic, no explanation needed.
  4. Assess the Child-Turcotte-Pugh score, which residents should remember since it tells hepatic function reserve: Figure (table): encephalopathy: none 1; grade 1-2 (or precipitant induced) 2; grade 3-4 (or chronic) 3. Ascites: none 1; mild to moderate (diuretic responsive) 2; severe (diuretic refractory) 3. Bilirubin: under 2 = 1; 2-3 = 2; over 3 = 3. Albumin: over 3.5 = 1; 2.8-3.5 = 2; under 2.8 = 3. INR: under 1.7 = 1; 1.7-2.3 = 2; over 2.3 = 3. Class A 5-6 (least severe), B 7-9 (moderately severe), C 10-15 (most severe).
     - 4.1 Child A 5-6 points: if operated, mortality 5%. 4.2 Child B 7-9: mortality 10%. 4.3 Child C over 10: mortality over 40%.
  5. MELD score (Model for End-stage Liver Disease): the most accurate in assessing the liver, using serum bilirubin, creatinine and INR; but must be calculated a lot, fairly troublesome; good to know.

### Cirrhosis with Esophagogastric Varices
- These numbers, besides being kept as trivia, help explain the concept of varices management: 30% of cirrhotic patients develop varices and 33% die of bleeding; if varices exist, over a lifetime there is a 26% chance of bleeding; if bleeding from varices: the first bleed has 30% mortality; a second bleed usually occurs within 5-7 days, and 40% bleed within 6 weeks; a second bleed has 50% mortality; if followed, 70% will certainly bleed again within 1 year; if death, usually after bleeding about 48-72 hours, so there is still time to plan.
- In a cirrhotic patient with varices who bleeds, about 50% bleed from varices; the other 50% bleed from other causes, so scope every case if possible.

### Management
- 3 patterns depending on the situation:
  1. Primary prophylaxis: preventive treatment so varices in a cirrhotic patient who has never bled do not bleed: the main treatment is a beta blocker.
  2. Management of acute bleeding.
  3. Secondary prophylaxis: treatment after bleeding has stopped, to prevent rebleeding: the main treatment is a beta blocker with endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL).
- Now the role of surgeons in treating portal hypertension has decreased: OLT is left, and shunt operations are also less since TIPS arrived strongly.
- Even Prof. Dean Warren, a known name in standard textbooks, and after Warren's retirement J. Michael Henderson, accept that surgical shunt is now used only in highly selected patients.
- So in the past 20 years the concept of treating portal HT leans toward: 1. pharmacological treatment; 2. endoscopic treatment; 3. radiologic decompression; 4. OLT.
- J. Michael Henderson summarized the roles of treatment in Cameron 2011 p.307: Table (role of treatment options in managing portal HT): Prophylaxis: pharmacotherapy 3+; endoscopic treatment 1+; TIPS -; surgical shunt -; OLT 1+. Acute variceal bleeding: pharmacotherapy 3+; endoscopic 3+; TIPS 2+; surgical shunt -; OLT -. Prevention of recurrent bleeding: pharmacotherapy 3+; endoscopic 3+; TIPS 2+; surgical shunt 1+; OLT 2+. Note: "-" means no indication; 1+ means little chance of use; 2+ secondary treatment; 3+ primary treatment.
- Observations: 1. for primary prophylaxis, a beta-blocker is the most used, next endoscopic treatment (EVL); TIPS and shunt have no role; 2. while bleeding, do not do a shunt or OLT: use somatostatin with endoscopic treatment (EVL); if not controlled, a Sengstaken-Blakemore tube; if that fails, finish with TIPS; 3. now surgical shunt is only a secondary prophylaxis, the last path to choose: in the USA OLT is advised over shunt.

### Primary Prophylaxis of Variceal Bleeding
- When a cirrhotic patient is scoped and varices that have never bled, or not yet exposed to bleeding, are found: be careful so they do not bleed.
- Features of varices with high bleeding risk: 1. size over 5 mm: the most important risk factor; 2. wall appearance showing thin walls: cherry red spot, red wale marking, diffuse redness, or tortuous varices on varices; 3. found in Child C patients; 4. found in patients with hepatic vein pressure gradient (HVPG) over 12 mmHg.
- HVPG: calculates the pressure difference between PV and IVC using a balloon catheter occluding the hepatic vein, similar to wedge pressure of the pulmonary artery; the HVPG value tells portal vein pressure; the cause of portal hypertension is not predictive of whether the varices will bleed.
- Guideline for varices in cirrhosis: 1. scope finds no varices: re-scope in 2 years (figure says 2-3 years); 2. varices under 5 mm: re-scope in 1 year; 3. varices over 5 mm, or under 5 mm but with high-risk features such as red wale marking or CTP class C: first-line therapy.
- First-line therapy: a beta blocker such as propranolol or nadolol to reduce cardiac output and cause splanchnic vasoconstriction: reduces portal blood flow, ending with reduced portal pressure.
- Dose propranolol so HR falls 25% but not under 55 beats per minute; take it for life; reduces bleeding risk from 30% to 18%.
- Giving propranolol with EVL seems excessive: at this step choose one drug first; propranolol together with other drugs, e.g. nitrite, not much benefit.
- If propranolol cannot be taken, e.g. COPD, asthma, CHF, bradycardia, insulin-dependent DM: do EVL instead, which reduces risk of bleeding about as well as propranolol.
- In primary prophylaxis: no EVS, TIPS or shunts: absolutely none; whoever answers otherwise gets hit.
- Figure (management of patients with cirrhosis following EGD screening): no varices: repeat EGD in 2-3 years; small varices (5 mm or less): high risk of bleeding (red wale marks and/or CTP class C): yes: nonselective beta-blocker (nadolol, propranolol, carvedilol); no: repeat EGD in 1-2 years; medium/large varices (over 5 mm): nonselective beta-blocker (nadolol, propranolol, carvedilol) OR endoscopic variceal ligation.

Nadolol 20 to 40 mg once a day (adjust for renal insufficiency); goal maximal tolerance or HR 55; assess HR every visit. Propranolol 20 mg twice a day; goal maximal tolerance or HR 55; assess HR every visit. Endoscopic variceal ligation every 2 to 4 weeks; goal variceal obliteration; surveillance EGD 1 to 3 months after initial obliteration, then once every 6 to 12 months.

### Management of Acute Bleeding
- Whoever meets UGIB from ruptured varices: an acceptable consult is in the cool of the evening: ask the on-call group to sleep and play with the family to get an expensive consult (the author's humorous aside, non-clinical).
- Treatment: start with ATLS principles, ABC, most reaching C at once, initial resuscitation: the current concept is "under-resuscitation" like trauma or ruptured AAA: keep SBP about 80 mmHg only, since if BP is too high it bleeds more; give blood to keep Hb about 7-8 g/dL.
- Monitor in the ICU: line for CVP, Foley for urine output.
- Hurry to correct coagulopathy; give prophylactic antibiotics (quinolone or ceftriaxone) to kill gut flora for about 5-7 days, since the risk of sepsis in bleeding varices is up to 33%.
- Then hurry to give somatostatin: IV bolus 250 microgram followed by IV drip 250 microgram per hour for 5 days; with somatostatin alone the bleeding usually stops gradually.
- While bleeding, avoid potent splanchnic vasoconstrictors such as vasopressin or terlipressin: though they lower portal pressure about 23%, they also raise BP, cause coronary vasoconstriction and decrease cardiac output, so are dangerous in patients with MI or mesenteric ischemia, even if we add NTG.
- If the patient has abrupt hepatic encephalopathy (HE): treat with lactulose and correct fluid and electrolytes, being careful of hypokalemia.
- Once the situation is controlled and the patient stable enough, send for upper endoscopy, which is both diagnosis of the cause of UGIB and treatment through the scope, e.g. endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL) or endoscopic variceal sclerotherapy (EVS); generally advised within 12 hours.
- Before the scope, the author advises an ET tube, to prevent aspirated blood going into the airway and shock mid-scope: safer.
- Scope features indicating bleeding from varices: 1. ruptured varices seen; 2. clot sticking on varices; 3. varices seen alone with no other cause.
- When esophageal varices are bleeding do EVL; but a bleeding gastric varix: inject glue.
- To control bleeding gastric varices: reflex the scope back, inject fibrin glue 1-2 cc into the varix then flush with 1 cc water: if a skilled person does it, bleeding stops about 93%; the problem is that gastric varices are in the submucosa: injection may cause ulceration and rebleeding, and these are hard to treat with EVL; after glue injection the vessel is hard, and when scoping, rubber-banding is hard, and in some cases gastric perforation follows. TIPS in gastric varices has a rebleeding rate of 30%: TIPS results are worse than in esophageal varices.
- Sclerotherapy (EVS) stops bleeding almost as well as EVL, but EVS has more problems such as perforation or stricture, so choose EVL first. Figure: EVL band (skip).
- Limitation of EVL: small varices are hard to band, or varices previously injected with sclerosant become hard and hard to band: in such cases EVS may be used, injected often.
- Endoscopic treatment stops bleeding 90-95%, then go on to secondary prophylaxis.
- If the 1st EVL fails, try a 2nd time; if the 2nd stops bleeding, proceed to secondary prophylaxis.
- If the 2nd EVL still does not stop the bleeding, place an SB tube as a bridge (some places do not use an SB tube but take the patient to scope and place a removable covered self-expanding metal stent 25 mm wide, 13.5 cm long to press on the varices); the situation is like a soccer team in the relegation zone: if the first ball goes in the team loses its lines.
- Technique of placing an SB tube (Sengstaken-Blakemore) after securing the airway (endotracheal tube): Figure: esophageal balloon port, gastric balloon port, gastric aspiration port, tension with 250-500 g weight (skip).
  1. Insert the tube via nose or mouth, swallow it until the tip is in the stomach; then inject NSS 150-200 cc mixed with contrast 25-50 cc into the gastric balloon (check position by x-ray/fluoroscopy); some advise inflating with 250 cc air.
  2. Traction the gastric balloon, using a 500 cc IV bottle hung so the balloon sits against the EGJ; see whether bleeding stops.
  3. If still bleeding, inflate the esophageal balloon with pressure not over 30 mmHg.
  4. Insert an NG tube with the tip in the esophagus for suction of saliva.
  5. Traction with a 500 cc NSS bag generally to the SB tube; stops bleeding 85-90%; 24 hours later try deflating the esophageal balloon first, leaving the gastric balloon; 50% rebleed, then re-inflate a new esophageal balloon; rebleeders: consider adjunct therapies such as TIPS, BRTO/BATO or surgical shunt.
- With a Minnesota tube there are 4 lumens, so no NG tube is needed for saliva suction. Figure: Minnesota tube (gastric aspirate, esophageal aspirate, esophageal balloon, gastric balloon; 24-45 mmHg; 300 cc air) (skip).
- Balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO): a new intervention to control bleeding from gastric varices: studies found that in gastric varices there is a gastrorenal shunt between gastric varices and the left renal vein. A balloon is placed to occlude the gastrorenal shunt as the first step, then sclerosant (ethanolamine oleate) is injected into the shunt where the blood is stagnant; waiting for clot formation, the clot blocks the shunt until it is obliterated; finally blood from the left renal vein no longer fills the gastric varices; bleeding stops. Reported 100% stopping bleeding from gastric varices, rebleeding only 0-5%; BRTO is now entering management guidelines. Disadvantage of BRTO: the occluded gastrorenal shunt raises portal pressure, until bleeding from esophageal varices occurs; the sclerosant used may cause hemolysis followed by hemoglobinuria and renal damage. Figure: BRTO diagrams (skip).
- Figure (algorithm, suspected variceal bleeding): airway protection, PRBC transfusion, vasoactive agents, antibiotics, medical ICU admission -> EGD within 12 h -> esophageal variceal bleeding: endoscopic variceal ligation: no further bleeding: secondary prophylaxis NSBBs or EVL: EVL failure or NSBBs not tolerated: TIPSS; rebleeding: repeat EVL: further bleeding: TIPSS; uncontrolled: balloon tamponade or metal stent: TIPSS. Gastric variceal bleeding: endoscopic or EUS glue injection: eradication by glue injection: failure or for secondary prophylaxis: TIPSS; glue injection unavailable: TIPSS. TIPSS uncontrolled bleeding or contraindicated: porto-caval shunt surgery.

Vasoconstrictor: IV octreotide (50 microgram bolus then 50 microgram/hour infusion x 2 to 5 days); IV terlipressin (2 mg every 4 hours for first 48 hours, followed by 1 mg every 4 hours x 2 to 5 days). Antibiotic prophylaxis: IV ceftriaxone 1 gm daily x 7 days (preferred in Child class B and C); oral norfloxacin 400 mg twice a day x 7 days. Endoscopic therapy: endoscopic variceal ligation (preferred); endoscopic variceal sclerotherapy. Salvage therapy: balloon tamponade (only temporary, maximum 24 hours); transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt.

### Secondary Prophylaxis of Variceal Bleeding
- After bleeding is treated and has stopped: if nothing more is done, rebleeding is very high: followed for 1 year, about 70% bleed.
- To prevent rebleeding, know the following data: 1. beta blocker alone: rebleeding 40%; 2. beta blocker with EVS: rebleeding 35%; 3. beta blocker with EVL: rebleeding 25% (the best).
- Choose either EVL or EVS, not both (do not catch fish with 2 hands), except in cases with more than 2 (rebleeds).
- EVL: band 6-8 rings each time, every 2-4 weeks; generally 2-4 sessions finish banding all. Figure: EGD post-EVL: varix almost gone (skip).
- After banding varices, scope at 1 month shows the varices have disappeared; then continue lifelong secondary prophylaxis: beta blocker with endoscopic surveillance; if varices reappear, do EVL again.
- Those bleeding from alcoholic cirrhosis are tougher than average; when bleeding stops they tend to drink again, then bleed again.
- If after secondary prophylaxis there is still rebleeding, or at follow-up scope there are high-risk varices: should put TIPS, but look at the patient's condition: if good, Child A or Child B: a selective shunt; but if poor condition or end-stage liver disease, then OLT only.
- Figure (algorithm for prevention of recurrent variceal bleeding): acute bleed controlled with banding -> evaluation -> beta-blocker and course of banding -> varices obliterated: beta-blocker; persistent high-risk varices or rebleeding: repeat banding, or decompress: TIPS, DSRS; end-stage liver disease: transplant.

### Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt (TIPS)
- A way to reduce portal pressure: via a guide wire through the right internal jugular vein down into the right hepatic vein, then puncture across until the tip goes into the PV (do not go down as far as the SMV); then place a self-expanding metal stent 10-12 mm. Figure: TIPS (hepatic vein, portal vein) (skip).
- TIPS is a side-to-side portocaval shunt inside the liver tissue: it reduces portal pressure.
- Indications: 1. first-line therapy for refractory bleeding or recurrent bleeding; 2. refractory ascites; 3. Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS); 4. hepatorenal syndrome type 2; 5. hepatopulmonary syndrome; 6. portal hypertensive gastropathy refractory to beta blocker.
- Absolute contraindications: 1. uncorrectable coagulopathy; 2. unrelieved biliary obstruction; 3. multiple hepatic cysts; 4. right-side CHF, severe pulmonary hypertension; 5. severe active infection or sepsis.
- Relative contraindications: 1. portal vein thrombosis; 2. hepatic vein thrombosis; 3. poor liver function reserve (INR over 5 or platelets under 20,000); 4. polycystic liver disease or central liver tumor; 5. uncorrectable bleeding diathesis.
- Problems of TIPS:
  1. TIPS stenosis: in the early era stents were not good: followed 1 year, about 50% of stents stenosed; now newer stents are much better: stent stenosis only 15% (remember). Suspect a stent blockage when a patient with TIPS rebleeds: do duplex US: if narrowed or blocked, consider catheter thrombolysis or replace with a new stent.
  2. Encephalopathy: from shunting portal blood through the liver into the systemic circulation, so cerebral toxins such as ammonia are not cleared by the liver: the brain suffers: this complication is found 25%.
  3. Others: bleeding, bile duct injury, tip dislocation.
- Hepatic hydrothorax: pleural fluid over 500 cc in a cirrhotic patient with no cardiopulmonary disease; found often in the right pleural cavity; believed ascites seeps through the diaphragm. Treatment: 1. treat like liver ascites; 2. if failed, put a TIPS.
- Hepatopulmonary syndrome: results from intrapulmonary hypertension causing right-to-left shunt, making gas exchange worse.
- Hepatorenal syndrome: from many hemodynamic changes in cirrhotic patients causing renal arterial vasoconstriction and hypoperfusion; the kidney ends up failing without intrinsic renal disease: type 1 = acute renal failure; type 2 = chronic renal failure, in which TIPS has a role.
- Portal hypertensive gastropathy: diffuse gastric vein dilatation under an inflamed, friable gastric mucosa that bleeds easily. Treatment: treat portal HT; TIPS is useful by reducing portal pressure.
- Results of TIPS: 1. rebleeding falls to about 4% per year; 2. ascites reduced 50-80%, clearly seen 1 month after TIPS; 3. in hepatorenal syndrome type 2, TIPS improves renal function about 60%.

## Surgery for Portal Hypertension
- History: began 140 years ago with Nikolai Eck (ECK fistula): in Russia he joined the portal vein to the IVC in dogs; 20 years later Pavlov found that dogs with the Eck fistula developed abnormal behavior: foolish, aggressive, senseless after meat meals.
- 10 years after, portocaval shunt was done in humans: some survived, some died: the result was that bleeding from varices stopped, but life was not longer, because patients died of liver failure.
- In 1900-1990 dozens of operations to reduce pressure in the portal system were devised; in residency 20-30 years ago, shunts were done a lot since EVS and EVL did not work then: the author did end-to-side portocaval shunt (PCS) at night, not fewer than 5 cases, never saw encephalopathy because the patients died.
- Until Dean Warren recommended the selective shunt with very good results; but by this era the role of surgeons: OLT is the main treatment, and surgical shunt is an option for end-stage liver disease patients.
- Though portal hypertension surgery is done less, residents still must know, since it is still used in patients with contraindications for TIPS, or where TIPS is not available, or TIPS often stenoses and cannot be fixed.
- Surgery is divided into 3 big groups: 1. surgical shunts: 1.1 total shunt: shunt between the portal system and IVC with diameter over 10 mm, e.g. portacaval shunt; 1.2 partial shunt: shunt diameter 8 mm or less, e.g. Sarfeh shunt; 1.3 selective shunt: e.g. Warren shunt; 2. devascularization: e.g. esophageal transection; 3. OLT.
- Principles of choosing the operation: 1. no single best procedure for every patient; 2. the best, most expensive procedure may not suit that patient; 3. the surgeon's duty is to choose a procedure that fits that patient, at the right place and time.

### Total Shunts
- Complete diversion of portal blood flow so blood does not pass the liver: a non-selective shunt.
- Popular in 1950-1965 because it stops bleeding most effectively, in all shunt series; but the problem was encephalopathy up to 30%, so the cause of death changed from bleeding to encephalopathy and liver failure.
- In the past most big names in total shunt were Marshall J. Orloff, the rival of Dean Warren, who said total shunt is the shunt of choice in emergencies.
- Now EVL gives good results and the practice has changed: Orloff's own followers do not get results as good; total shunt popularity declined; Sarfeh and Rypins' small-diameter portacaval partial shunt reduces portal pressure while blood can still enter the liver, so bleeding stops with much lower risk of encephalopathy; the Sarfeh partial shunt is widely accepted.
- Examples of total shunts: 1. portacaval shunt, both end-to-side and side-to-side; 2. mesocaval shunt, usually using a vascular graft as conduit; 3. proximal splenorenal shunt, some call it central shunt or Linton shunt.

#### Portocaval Shunt (PCS)
- Surgery taking the portal vein to the IVC: connecting the end of the PV to the side of the IVC, or the side of the PV to the side of the IVC. Figure: end-to-side portocaval shunt (divided portal v., IVC, SMV, IMV, splenic v., gastroepiploic v., short gastric veins, varices, L. gastric (coronary) v.) (skip).
- When the anastomosis opening is over 12 mm it becomes a total shunt: blood from the PV does not enter the liver but runs into the IVC to the right heart.
- Side-to-side PCS is interesting because besides lowering portal pressure it also lowers sinusoidal pressure in the liver, so it can treat refractory ascites well. Figure: side-to-side portocaval shunt (skip).
- Contraindications of PCS: 1. previous biliary tract surgery (many adhesions, difficult); 2. portal vein thrombosis; 3. encephalopathy; 4. large caudate lobe: PCS difficult, exposure poor; 5. on the OLT waiting list: TIPS is better than shunt.

#### Mesocaval Shunt (MCS)
- Connecting the SMV to the IVC side-to-side using a ring graft of PTFE 14-16 mm.
- Directly connected like an H is called Drapanas shunt; connected in a C shape is called Cameron shunt. Figure: C shunt (skip). The Cameron shunt is the one the author likes: he found it not hard and needing few helpers.
- Advantages of MCS: 1. easy, quick, effective, can be done in emergency, operative mortality not high; 2. side-to-side so sinusoidal pressure falls, so usable in refractory ascites; 3. suits transplant candidates since it does not disturb the portal triad area.
- Disadvantages: 1. like PCS: encephalopathy; 2. shunt thrombosis.

#### Proximal or Central Splenorenal Shunt (Linton shunt)
- A procedure suited to cases where the spleen must be sacrificed together, i.e. portal HT with hypersplenism: remove the spleen then connect the proximal end of the splenic vein to the left renal vein. Figure: Linton shunt (skip).
- Linton shunt is difficult and has much encephalopathy, so it is not popular.

#### Partial Shunt
- Since a side-to-side PCS over 12 mm is a total shunt with blood not entering the liver and much encephalopathy: James Sarfeh and Eric Rypins reduced shunt size to 8 mm and used a ring graft such as PTFE joined side-to-side PCS.
- In the long run the anastomosis opening with a ring graft does not expand further, unlike native vein such as saphenous vein: this is called the partial shunt, the Sarfeh shunt. Figure: partial portocaval shunt (skip).
- Partial shunt, besides lowering portal pressure, still lets blood go into the liver, so encephalopathy is less.
- Rebleeding 2-12%, operative mortality under 4.5%, mild encephalopathy 14%; if good patients are chosen, 5-year survival 63%: impressive. Long-term patency of Sarfeh shunt up to 90%.
- The big name who cheers the Sarfeh shunt is Henri Bismuth (the author's aside on the Schwartz 8th ed liver chapter author, who after reading Schwartz 9th-11th with the shunt removed, is non-clinical); in Thailand the shunt operations by Dr. Warin's team at the Police hospital are an example (non-clinical aside).

#### Selective Shunts
- Shunts that decompress esophagogastric varices without disturbing portal blood flow, so encephalopathy is rare; they give long-term survival like the Sarfeh partial shunt.
- Examples: the distal splenorenal shunt (Warren shunt) and the Inokuchi shunt.

#### Distal Splenorenal Shunt (DSRS)
- Proposed by Warren: dissect the splenic vein free from the pancreas (this step needs a little patience, since bleeding and oozing are troublesome); the splenic vein size should be 7 mm or more.
- Then take the distal end of the splenic vein and anastomose it to the left renal vein, together with ligating the left gastric (coronary) vein, right gastric vein, gastroepiploic vein, left adrenal vein and IMA.
- The SMV is not ligated; varices usually collapse 4-8 weeks after surgery. Figure: DSRS schematic: blood from varices drains into splenic and gastric veins which then flow into the distal splenorenal shunt (skip).
- Problem of DSRS: following patients over time, new collaterals form from the remaining proximal splenic vein, creating the pancreatic siphon effect: loss of shunt selectivity: blood is drawn out of the portal system to the EG varices again. Figure: pancreatic siphon (skip).
- Warren advised fixing the pancreatic siphon by dissecting the splenic vein free up to the portal vein, called splenopancreatic disconnection (SPD), together with cutting the splenocolic ligament. Figure: DSRS + SPD (skip).
- The weak point of Warren is ascites that increases after surgery: believed because many collaterals are ligated, raising sinusoidal pressure; some believe the dissection around the left renal vein cuts lymphatic circulation (do not bother with the mechanism): if ascites is hard to control, do not choose a Warren shunt.

#### Inokuchi Shunt
- Proposed by Inokuchi of Kyushu University in 1968: connects the left gastric vein or coronary vein to the IVC using the great saphenous vein as a graft. Figure: Inokuchi shunt (skip).
- Blood still enters the liver: little encephalopathy.
- Results very good: rebleeding only 7%, no encephalopathy; the problem is that good results were found only in Japanese patients where Inokuchi himself did them; others outside Japan doing the same did not get good results.

### Devascularization Procedures
- No anastomosis of dark blood vessels: simply ligate and cut veins around the esophagogastric junction (EGJ) to reduce the blood supply feeding the EGJ, hoping varices collapse and stop bleeding.
- Advantages: 1. simple, suits high-risk cases such as Child C bleeding; 2. no TIPS available in hospital, no liver for transplant, or the hospital is not ready, blood and plasma are hard to get, the surgeon is without strength and cannot refer.
- Four devascularization procedures to present: Tanner, Transection, Hassab and Sugiura.

#### Tanner's Operation
- British surgeon, reported 1961. Figure (skip).
- Method: first ligate the coronary vein and do devascularization by ligating and cutting veins from just below the EGJ down about 15 cm (called "clearing" veins around the proximal stomach until clean); not enough yet: also gastric transection then reanastomosis at a position about 5 cm below the EGJ to be sure blood flow to varices falls.
- But results are not good: after surgery new collaterals appear quickly, then EG varices fill again.

#### Esophageal Transection with EEA Stapler
- Use an EEA stapler to cut then rejoin: using the stapler is easy and quick to close the circle; the aim is to interrupt blood flow so it does not go to the EGJ. Figure: esophageal transection (skip).
- Result: not good, like Tanner: rebleeding up to 76%.

#### Hassab's Operation
- Egyptian surgeon, reported 1970: devascularization of the abdominal esophagus and upper stomach with splenectomy as a package. Figure (skip).
- He said it suits Egyptians, whose portal HT is often from schistosomiasis: he did it with very good results; the problem as before: results by others are not good.

#### Sugiura's Operation
- A procedure said to suit Japanese especially children whose portal HT is from extrahepatic portal vein thrombosis.
- Formerly 2 stages: first open the chest, then 6 weeks later open the abdomen; now modified Sugiura: one stage through the abdomen only. Figure: Sugiura procedure (esophageal transection and reanastomosis, selective vagotomy, pyloroplasty, paraesophagogastric devascularization, splenectomy) (skip).
- Sugiura method: 1. devascularization of the esophagus: ligate and cut veins going upward about 7 cm above the EGJ; 2. then devascularize the stomach: greater curve entirely, lesser curve only 2/3; 3. next, esophageal transection and reanastomosis with an EEA stapler; 4. splenectomy; 5. selective vagotomy with pyloroplasty.
- Advantage: still preserves portoazygos flow going upward while flow to varices is wholly interrupted; since portal flow is maintained, encephalopathy is very little.
- In Japan results are very good (the best): mortality under 5%, encephalopathy 5%, rebleeding 5%, 5-year survival 60%.
- But John H.C. Ranson (of the Ranson criteria in acute pancreatitis) tried Sugiura in the US: mortality 10%, rebleeding 37%, alive at 2 years only 38%; the operation took about 10 hours (the author tried 1 case, nearly 12 hours, the patient died: mortality 100%: stopped).

### Liver Transplantation (OLT)
- The procedure of choice for bleeding varices in end-stage liver disease. Figure: liver transplantation (skip).
- A liver transplant solves the portal HT problem completely, especially CPS class C with refractory ascites.

### Sinistral Portal HT
- Some call it segmental portal HT or left-sided portal HT: portal HT following splenic vein thrombosis, often found in patients with frequent pancreatitis.
- A pre-hepatic lesion: the liver size is normal but the spleen enlarges; blood from the spleen drains via the short gastric veins forming gastric varices, seen at gastroscopy. Figure: splenic vein occlusion with gastric varices (GV, SGV, LGV, GEV, SPO) (skip).
- Treatment: most do not bleed; if gastric varices bleed: splenectomy alone: no shunt or devascularization.

### Portal Hypertensive Gastropathy (PHG)
- Inflammation of the stomach from cirrhosis and portal HT.
- Patients usually have chronic blood loss and anemia more than acute UGIB.
- Gastroscope: gastric mucosa in a mosaic pattern or snake-skin appearance; some places have red spots. Figure (skip).
- Treatment: treat as portal HT.

### Ectopic Varices
- Varices found outside the EGJ, from the antrum to the rectum; found not over 5%: since few are found, a suitable summary treatment is hard; know only what might be asked.

#### Jejuno-ileal varices
- The most common ectopic varices: 1-8% present with bleeding (LGIB). Since it is small bowel bleeding, diagnosis is hard: expensive tools like capsule enteroscopy may be needed.
- Treatment: conservative as for general LGIB; if it fails, segmental resection of the small bowel with varices.

#### Rectal varices
- Not hemorrhoids found at the anus: rectal varices arise from portal HT, usually higher in position but may extend down to the anal canal, so that we think they are internal hemorrhoids. Figure: rectal varices vs internal hemorrhoid (skip).
- Treatment: 1. do not do a hemorrhoidectomy: definitely wrong; 2. if bleeding: suture, inject sclerosant, or ligate as convenient to stop the bleeding first; 3. continue treating as portal HT.

#### Parastomal varices
- Found up to 50% after ileostomy in portal HT patients: after some time new portosystemic collaterals form between skin and mucocutaneous junction: tortuous bulging vessels like caput medusae.
- If bleeding: suture, ligate, or try injecting sclerosant first; for long-term treatment revision of the ileostomy to disconnect the portosystemic channel may be needed: most bleed in small amounts and are controlled easily.
- Some cases may need portal HT treatment too.

### Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE)
- A neuropsychiatric syndrome from liver disease: cerebral toxins from the gut, e.g. ammonia, or some drugs not metabolized, are not cleared by the liver first (e.g. cirrhosis, or portacaval shunt); once cerebral toxins leak into the brain, the brain swells, with symptoms: restlessness, confusion, then coma, possibly death.
- Ammonia is made from blood broken down by bacteria in the GI tract and absorbed mostly at the colon.
- Precipitating factors: 1. conditions raising ammonia: GI bleeding, excessive dietary protein, constipation; 2. infection or sepsis; 3. electrolyte imbalance, especially hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, dehydration; 4. CNS depressant drugs such as morphine, hypnotics, antidepressants; 5. renal failure.
- Treatment: 1. reduce ammonia production: treat GI bleeding, control infection with antibiotic; 2. correct electrolyte imbalance; 3. try to reduce protein diet; branched-chain amino acids may be used; 4. cleansing enema with lactulose, which besides washing out retained blood is a mild laxative that also blocks colonic mucosa absorption of ammonia; 5. avoid drugs or substances metabolized by the liver.
- Remember: in the past right hemicolectomy or subtotal colectomy was advised to improve HE by reducing the site of ammonia absorption; now no one believes or uses it.

## Surgical Management of Ascites
- A normal person has fluid in the abdomen under 150 cc.
- Shifting dullness needs fluid over 1.5 liters; fluid thrill positive needs over 10 liters.
- The first sign of ascites is unexpected weight gain +/- peripheral edema.
- Residents should know 4 types of ascites: 1. liver ascites; 2. pancreatic ascites; 3. infected ascites; 4. chylous ascites.
- When meeting a patient with ascites, work out where the abdominal fluid comes from: paracentesis and sending fluid examination is very important.
- Paracentesis: 1. look at gross appearance: color, smell, turbid or clear; 2. send cell count, differential count, culture, cytology; 3. send albumin, sugar, amylase. Note: do not do paracentesis in coagulopathy.
- Characteristics of ascitic fluid:
  1. Liver: clear fluid, low protein; compare albumin level in ascites with serum: Serum Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG) = serum albumin minus ascites albumin: 1.1 in cirrhosis (serum protein under 2.5): SAAG over 1.1; 1.2 in malignant (serum protein over 2.5 as printed): SAAG under 1.1.
  2. Pancreas: clear fluid, colorless, odorless, alkaline: 2.1 amylase in fluid about 3 times that of serum; 2.2 protein over 2.5 gm/dL (in cirrhosis under 2.5): so amylase and protein help separate pancreatic ascites from liver ascites; treatment of pancreatic ascites: read in the pancreas chapter.
  3. Chylous: milky fluid, odorless, cells show lots of lymphocytes; TG in chylous ascites over 110: the large amount of fat arises from fat absorbed and formed into small micelles called chylomicrons; so fluid with chylomicron is chylous ascites. Beware: in a patient NPO for a long time, the fat level falls, so milky chylous fluid may turn to clear fluid. Treatment: read in the Lymphatic disease chapter.
  4. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) or primary peritonitis: peritonitis without any surgical disease in the abdomen: found in cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome: 1. turbid fluid: Gram stain or culture shows a single organism; 2. neutrophil count over 250; 3. protein over 1, sugar under 30; 4. LDH in ascites higher than in serum.
  5. TB peritonitis: common in HIV patients; ascites is usual: 1. high lymphocytes; 2. low protein; 3. low sugar (under 30 mg/dL).
  6. CAPD peritonitis: in kidney failure patients treated by peritoneal dialysis: when infection occurs the patient has fever and abdominal pain, the fluid flowing out, once clear, is turbid with infection, and a single organism like SBP.
  - Treatment of items 4, 5, 6: read in the Peritonitis chapter.

### Liver Ascites
- The most common type, 85%: fluid from liver disease: number 1 cirrhosis; number 2 Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS).

### Cirrhotic Ascites
- With cirrhosis and portal hypertension, ascites follows: fluid leaks and collects in the peritoneal cavity or 3rd space, intravascular volume decreases, stimulating the renin-angiotensin mechanism to hold sodium and water.
- This means that to treat cirrhotic ascites one must restrict sodium intake and also restrict water.
- SAAG (Harrison 20e) figure: SAAG 1.1 g/dL or more: ascitic protein under 2.5 g/dL: cirrhosis, late Budd-Chiari syndrome, massive liver metastases; ascitic protein 2.5 g/dL or more: heart failure/constrictive pericarditis, early Budd-Chiari syndrome, IVC obstruction, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome. SAAG under 1.1 g/dL: biliary leak, nephrotic syndrome, pancreatitis, peritoneal carcinomatosis, tuberculosis.
- Medical management: first-line treatment, effective up to 90%: so initial treatment of cirrhotic ascites: 1. restrict dietary sodium to under 2 gm/day (45-90 mEq/d); 2. water intake restriction: 1-1.5 liters per day; 3. bed rest in upright position (believed to reduce renin and angiotensin activity, increasing urine); 4. diuretic: main drug spironolactone, second drug Lasix.
- Diuretics commonly used (2):
  1. Spironolactone: aldosterone antagonist, the main drug, start at 100 mg/day, can increase up to 400 mg/day, effective in about 48-72 hours.
  2. Furosemide (Lasix): a loop diuretic used in addition to aldactone, start 40 mg/day, can increase up to 370 mg/day; advantage: acts fast, within 1 hour; if overdone, watch for frequent urination and dehydration.
- Second-line treatment: about 10% of cirrhotic ascites patients after aggressive medical treatment over 2 weeks still have ascites increasing and abdomen distended: a refractory case; if urine sodium is under 50 it shows the kidney is retaining sodium but not letting it out; when drugs do not work or are not taken, they need procedures: LVP, TIPS, or shunt surgery.
- Large volume paracentesis (LVP): tapping the abdomen to remove fluid. Figure: old image of paracentesis, National Museum, Nuremberg, Germany (skip). A method about 350 years old.
  - LVP is the 2nd-line treatment: recommended site LLQ superior and medial to the ASIS about 3 cm; remove 4-6 liters each time, and for every 5 liters of ascites removed, give albumin replacement 6-10 gm per liter of fluid removed. Figure: preferred needle entry site for paracentesis (3 cm from ASIS, inferior epigastric artery) (skip).
  - LVP once a month is tolerable; but if needed every 5-7 days it does not work: find other help.
- TIPS or surgical shunt: which to choose first? Know the mechanism of ascites: ascites results from raised intrasinusoidal pressure in the liver: high pressure damages the sinusoidal endothelium, separating it, so fluid slowly leaks from the liver surface; with this knowledge choose a surgical shunt:
  1. Extrahepatic portal vein thrombosis, a presinusoidal lesion: these patients have little ascites since little blood enters the liver, so sinusoidal pressure is not high; while BCS with hepatic vein obstruction has blood stuck in the liver, sinusoidal pressure very high, hence massive ascites.
  2. A side-to-side portosystemic shunt, besides lowering portal pressure, also lowers sinusoidal pressure, so it suits portal hypertension with ascites; but an end-to-side shunt lowers only portal pressure, not intrasinusoidal pressure: not suitable for ascites patients.
  - TIPS, a side-to-side intrahepatic portocaval shunt, can reduce sinusoidal pressure of the liver, so TIPS is another tool to treat ascites: after TIPS about 2-4 weeks ascites resolves.
  - For all these reasons: TIPS should be treatment for patients failing medical treatment; with today's good TIPS patency and being less invasive than surgical shunt, results are about as effective, so surgical shunt has disappeared from the set.
- Surgical shunt: 2 types lowering both portal pressure and ascites: 1. side-to-side portocaval shunt; 2. side-to-side mesocaval shunt. Figure (skip).
- OLT: the definitive treatment for refractory ascites, very suited to irreversible decompensated liver disease: transplant clearly raises survival.
- Peritoneovenous shunt or LeVeen shunt: a tube used in the old days; nowadays the Denver shunt is used. Figure: LeVeen and Denver shunts (skip).
  - A silicone tube placed in the high-pressure peritoneal cavity, the other end with a 1-way valve inserted into the internal jugular vein where pressure is low, to drain fluid into the SVC and the heart, pumping fluid into the systemic circulation to be excreted by the kidney; the principle is like neurosurgeons draining hydrocephalus: so LeVeen is only palliative treatment.
  - Contraindications: ascitic fluid protein over 4.5 g/L (fluid too thick, tube blocks); loculated ascites; advanced renal disease; advanced cardiac disease; GI malignancy. Complications are high: CHF, pulmonary emboli, DIC, infection, liver failure, shunt thrombosis, hemorrhage, wound infection, ascites leak.
  - Now TIPS works well, so the LeVeen/Denver shunt is no longer popular: poor long-term result, no increase in survival; and if a patient needs liver transplantation it makes it harder. AASLD recommends peritoneovenous shunting only for refractory ascites patients who are not candidates for TIPS or transplant, and cannot have repeated paracentesis. Figure (skip).
  - The author's experience: 20 years ago, once placed a Denver shunt under spinal (patient unfit for GA): put the distal end in the abdomen first, like a TK catheter wash-out, then took the tail, laid it in the subcutaneous layer, and threaded it into the great saphenous vein at the groin; ascites fell nicely but long-term he did not know since the patient died of other causes; the problem is that ordering a Denver tube is troublesome, so sending for TIPS is more comfortable.
- Guideline treatment of ascites (Shackelford 2019 p.1588), as steps: ascites: mild/moderate: 1. diet, sodium restriction (2 g/d); 2. spironolactone (100 mg/d); 3. may add Lasix (40 mg/d): persistent ascites -> refractory: large-volume paracentesis +/- albumin infusion -> increased frequency: TIPS -> ? transplant.
- Guideline (Harrison 20e), treatment of refractory ascites: LVP + albumin -> dietary sodium restriction + diuretics -> ascites reaccumulation -> consider TIPS / continue LVP with albumin as needed / consider liver transplantation.

### Budd-Chiari Syndrome (BCS)
- Thrombosis of the hepatic veins at the IVC level causing portal hypertension of the postsinusoidal obstruction type; 20% have PV and IVC thrombosis together; in the USA found often in young women, while in Asia in middle age, women and men about equal.
- Etiology of BCS: 1. hematological disease: the most common in the USA, e.g. polycythemia vera, myeloproliferative disorder, oral contraceptive use, hypercoagulable state; 2. others: SLE, pregnancy, estrogen exposure, Behcet's disease, tumor, trauma, abscess, hepatitis; 3. in Japan the common cause is IVC web; 4. cause not found (idiopathic) is possible; prior RT is not a cause.
- Clinical sign: the most common is ascites since intrasinusoidal pressure is high; jaundice and hepatomegaly, abdominal bloating, anorexia, splenomegaly; some have no symptoms, some are severe to liver failure. Figure: patient with dilated abdominal wall veins (skip).
- Diagnostic study: Doppler US (duplex), CT or MRI show hepatic vein thrombosis and large collateral veins running into the IVC near the caudate lobe: sensitivity up to 90%. Duplex US consists of real-time US with pulsed Doppler flow meter: hepatic vasculature is seen but no hepatic venous outflow.
- CT: caudate lobe hypertrophy, because blood cannot go through the HV and has to use the short hepatic veins of the caudate lobe, so with much blood passing, the caudate lobe grows; CT or MRI now also show intrahepatic vascular anatomy to use for interventions. Figure: IVC compressed by enlarged caudate lobe; ascites (skip). Hepatic venography is used in cases where an intervention is done.
- Treatment of BCS:
  1. Anticoagulant: the immediate standard therapy to prevent clot propagation.
  2. Treat ascites: restrict sodium intake together with diuretics.
  3. If the liver is bad:
     - 3.1 First-line treatment: intervention by percutaneous angioplasty with or without stenting, suitable for short stenosis; if no result the next step is TIPS together with long-term anticoagulant; now using PTFE-covered stents as a shunt gives patency of 80-90% at 1 year; even if TIPS occludes, dilatation or thrombolytic gives very good results; 5-year patency of TIPS 74%, with little encephalopathy since the liver is good in BCS patients; in some BCS patients whose hepatic vein stump is so sealed that it cannot be dilated for TIPS, a stent can be placed via the IVC through the caudate lobe into the portal vein: this TIPS route is called DIPS (direct intrahepatic portosystemic shunt). Figure: A = TIPS, B = DIPS (skip).
     - 3.2 Thrombolysis: not very effective, high reocclusion rate; if tried, should be an acute case; chronic BCS should not.
     - 3.3 Shunt surgery, whether side-to-side mesocaval or portocaval or mesoatrial, done since 1978: now probably history. Figure: mesoatrial shunt (skip).
     - 3.4 Patients with TIPS followed long term: about 1/3 develop fulminant liver failure or progress to end-stage liver disease and need OLT.
  4. IVC obstruction from a web, found often in Japan: besides ascites, the patient's legs swell, with cramps, calf pain, or venous claudication. Figure: membranous web on MRI (skip). Treatment: 1. conservative with compression garments; 2. if no better, try angioplasty with an IVC stent, self-expanding Gianturco-Z stent; 3. if the stent fails, then consider mesoatrial shunt.
