## Definition
- **Transplantation** — moving an organ or tissue from one place to another; receiver = **recipient**, giver = **donor**, the organ given = **graft**.
- **Autograft** — graft moved within the same person.
- **Allograft** — graft taken from one person and given to another of the same species; the procedure is allotransplantation.
- **Xenograft** — graft from a different species, e.g. monkey or pig organ into a human; xenotransplantation.
- **Composite graft** — graft containing several tissue types.
- **Orthotopic transplantation** — the failed organ is removed and the graft placed in its original position, e.g. liver transplantation = OLT (orthotopic liver transplantation).
- **Heterotopic transplantation** — the failed organ is not removed; the graft is placed in a new site, e.g. kidney or pancreas transplant, usually placed in the lower abdomen.

## Transplantation Immunobiology
- Normally human cell surfaces carry proteins called **major histocompatibility complex (MHC)**; 2 important ones:
  1. **ABO** and Rh blood group
  2. **HLA** (human leukocyte antigen) — on WBC, controlled by genes on the short arm of chromosome 6
- **HLA** has 2 important classes; class 3 is minor, ignore it.
  - Class 1 = HLA-A, B, C — expressed on the cell membrane of every nucleated cell
  - Class 2 = HLA-DR, DP, DQ
- Key HLA: A, B and DR — if all 3 match and ABO is compatible, the graft is guaranteed to do well.
- **Indication** in general: end stage organ failure not treatable otherwise. Now more people wait than organs are available — of 3 on the waiting list only 1 gets transplanted; some die waiting.
- **Basic absolute contraindication** (สนใจหน่อย)
  1. Presence of malignancy within 5 years, except 3 that are acceptable:
     - 1.1 Primary of CNS
     - 1.2 Non-melanotic skin cancer, e.g. BCC, SCC
     - 1.3 In situ malignancy, e.g. cervix
  2. Cancer in a disseminated stage
  3. Lung or heart disease so severe that anesthesia would surely kill
  4. Sepsis or active infection
  5. HIV +ve — immunosuppression cannot be given
  6. Others, e.g. intellectual disability, psychiatric illness, drug addiction, alcoholism

## Organ Donation
- After organs are donated from a brain-dead patient: keep donor body temperature 36-37.5 °C, MAP > 70 mmHg, SBP > 100 mmHg and Hb level 7-10 g/dL. The harvest team removes liver, heart, kidney, lung and quickly perfuses the organs with cold preservation fluid 0-4 °C to reduce warm ischemic time and graft acidosis.
- **Preservation solution** — several types; most used now is UW solution (University of Wisconsin solution), containing raffinose and lactobionate (reduce cell swelling from hypothermia) and hydroxyethyl starch.
- **Safe maximum storage time**

| Organ | Time |
|---|---|
| Kidney | 24 hr |
| Liver | 12 hr |
| Pancreas | 17 hr |
| Heart | 4 hr |
| Lung | 6 hr |

## Brain Death
- The person is dead, with the brainstem permanently destroyed.
- Declaring brain death must be careful: after diagnosis, repeat the examination within the next 6 hours.
- **Criteria of brain death**
  1. **Deep coma** — no response to deep central pain, GCS < 3 (as printed); reversible causes of coma must be excluded first to be sure:
     - 1.1 Not from narcotics, sleeping pills, or anticonvulsant barbiturates
     - 1.2 Not from correctable metabolic or endocrine disturbance
     - 1.3 No hypothermia
  2. **Apnea** — no spontaneous respiration, and not from muscle relaxant. Apnea test: stop the ventilator about 3 minutes, check blood gas; if PaCO2 > 60 and still no respiratory effort → the spirit is not coming back (as printed).
  3. **No brainstem reflexes**
     - 3.1 Both pupils dilated and fixed
     - 3.2 No corneal reflex
     - 3.3 No gag reflex, no cough reflex
     - 3.4 No oculovestibular reflex or vestibular response to cold caloric stimuli
     - 3.5 No oculocephalic reflex or Doll head phenomenon
     - 3.6 No motor response within the cranial nerve distribution
- Remember what the teacher taught: spinal reflexes, e.g. a jerk on tapping, are not a criterion of brain death.
- Decorticate or decerebrate rigidity movements are also not criteria of brain death.
- **Donor criteria**
  1. Physically healthy and strong; good hemodynamic status without inotropic drugs
  2. None of these diseases:
     - 2.1 Any cancer — except primary brain tumor, in situ cervix, BCC or SCC, which can still donate
     - 2.2 Septicemia
     - 2.3 HIV +ve
     - 2.4 Suspected rabies, encephalitis, myelitis
     - 2.5 HBV infection
     - 2.6 Active TB
  3. Age
     - 3.1 Liver and kidney ≤ 60 years
     - 3.2 Heart ≤ 45 years, lung ≤ 65 years
     - 3.3 Pancreas ≤ 45 years
  4. The donated organ must be good
- **Confirmatory test** (spelled "Comfirmatory" as printed) — not routine to prove brain death, except:
  1. Children < 1 year
  2. Clinically unsure whether truly dead
- **Tests used**
  1. CT brain with IV contrast — no intracranial blood flow
  2. EEG — no electrical activity
  3. Transcranial doppler US — no intracranial signal flow
  4. Radionuclide brain scan — no uptake

## Rejection
- Occurs when recipient and donor allograft differ.
- Rejection is an immunological reaction from B and T lymphocytes with many cytokines → inflammatory injury to the graft: graft endothelial and parenchymal cells break down until the graft is progressively destroyed.
- Now rejection is thought to come more from the cell mediated system than the humoral or antibody system.
- **Cellular immunity** — T cell and B cell lymphocyte.
- **Humoral immunity** — circulating antibody.
- **Warning signs**
  1. Fever and influenza-like symptom
  2. Tachycardia, hypertension, shortness of breathing
  3. Edema and wt. gain
  4. Pain over graft site
- **Classification** — by time of onset. Most divide into 3 phases. The older Schwartz split acute rejection into accelerated acute and acute; the new Schwartz went back to 3 phases, no longer splitting acute into 2, as treatment is the same. The author summarises 4 phases to read first, but in practice 3 works better.
- Figure: rejection mechanisms — hyperacute (circulating alloantigen-specific antibody → complement activation, endothelial damage, inflammation, thrombosis); acute (alloantigen-specific CD8+ T cells and alloreactive antibody → endothelialitis, parenchymal cell damage, interstitial inflammation); chronic (alloantigen-specific CD4+ T cell, cytokines, macrophages → chronic DTH reaction in vessel wall, intimal smooth muscle proliferation, vessel occlusion).
  1. **Hyperacute rejection** — almost immediately, within 24 hours; recipient already has circulating preformed antibody to graft Ag. On reperfusion, instead of turning pink like a baby's bottom, the graft turns dark purple-black at once. This is humoral rejection and irreversible.
     - Treatment: remove the graft at once. Now with good matching it is very rare.
  2. **Accelerated acute rejection** — within 1 week; mixed humoral and cellular.
     - Treatment: treat as acute rejection.
  3. **Acute rejection** — the most common; from 1 week to 6 months; caused by recipient cell mediated T cells infiltrating the graft.
     - Symptoms: graft function worsens, fever, tenderness at the graft site.
     - Diagnosis: biopsy shows heavy lymphocyte infiltration; if from infection, neutrophil infiltration instead.
     - Treatment: responds very well to drugs; about 95% of grafts can be saved. Start with high dose pulse steroid — controls 75%. If not better, add ALG and OKT3 to suppress T cells and stop rejection. A patient who has had acute rejection, even if treated, has a higher chance of chronic rejection.
  4. **Chronic rejection** — after 6 months or more; less common than acute but the commonest cause of graft loss; thought due to progressive intimal hypertrophy of small and medium sized arteries.
     - Diagnosis: graft biopsy.
     - Treatment: adjust or change drugs, and prepare for likely graft loss so as to plan re-transplant.

## Immunosuppressive Drugs
- Aim: prevent rejection by suppressing production or function of T and B cells.
- Highest drug need is in the first 3 months after transplant; then reduce to the lowest dose to lower the risk of infection and cancer.
- Drugs are usually given in combination to suppress both T and B cells; use steroid as little as possible, and stop it if possible.
- **Two phases of drug use** — residents should remember which drug is used when:
  1. **Induction** — early phase, usually high dose
     - 1.1 ATG, ALG
     - 1.2 OKT3
     - 1.3 IL2 receptor antagonist — two commonly used: Basiliximab (Simulcet), an anti-CD 25, and Daclizumab. A newer one, Alemtuzumab (Campath), is anti-CD 52 and suppresses lymphocytes to very low counts; drugs in this group carry a high infection risk, so are not popular.
  2. **Maintenance**
     - 2.1 Cyclosporine (neoral)
     - 2.2 Tacrolimus (prograft, as printed) or FK 506
     - 2.3 MMF
     - 2.4 Sirolimus (rapamycin)
     - 2.5 Belatacept — a T-cell blocker used in kidney transplantation
- Some drugs, e.g. steroid, are used in both induction and maintenance.
- Many anti-rejection drugs exist; the author covers only the famous ones, with their action and notable toxicity, especially 2.1 and 2.2.
- Residents: remembering what's written here is enough — within 4 years another 10 drugs will appear, guaranteed to fry your nerves, so pass the exam before Schwartz 11th ed. comes out.

## Antithymocyte & Antilymphocyte Globulin (ATG & ALG)
- **Mechanism of action** — polyclonal antibodies to T cells that compete for Ag directly on lymphocytes, keeping lymphocytes so busy they have no time to cause rejection.
- **Side effect**
  1. Serum sickness — made from rabbit or horse serum, so anaphylaxis can occur; mild allergy just nausea and rash; some react severely and may die if not treated in time
  2. Low WBC and plt.
  3. Increased risk of herpes and CMV infection

## OKT-3
- Monoclonal antibody used for induction, not maintenance.
- **Mechanism of action** — anti T cell; blocks T cell function.
- **Side effect**
  1. Pulmonary edema followed by CHF — serious, may be fatal
  2. Fever, chills, headache

## Basiliximab & Daclizumab
- **Mechanism of action** — anti IL2 receptor. IL2 is a crucial cytokine in rejection; with its receptor blocked, IL2 is paralysed, so this arm of the rejection process does not occur.
- **Side effect** — very few; does not increase risk of infection or PTLD (lymphoma).

## Steroid
- **Mechanism of action** — blocks cytokine gene transcription, so IL1, IL2, IL6, TNF and T cells are knocked out and too weak to reject the graft; also inhibits lymphokine production.
- **Side effect** — many: HT, DM, DLP, PU, cataract, myopathy, osteoporosis to the point of fracture.

## Calcineurin Inhibitor
- Inhibits calcineurin → selectively inhibits T-cell activation by blocking production of IL2, a key cytokine in rejection.
- Two drugs: cyclosporine (CSA) and tacrolimus (FK 506); notable shared side effect is nephrotoxicity. Choose one of the two — never use them together.

## Cyclosporine (CSA)
- **Side effect**
  1. Nephrotoxic (interstitial fibrosis of renal parenchyma)
  2. HT, DM, DLP — but less DM than tacrolimus (FK 506), so diabetic patients needing a calcineurin inhibitor should get cyclosporine
  3. Tremor
  4. Gingival hypertrophy
  5. Hirsutism
  6. Hyperkalemia
- **Advantages of CSA** vs FK 506
  1. Suits diabetic patients
  2. Does not suppress bone marrow like FK 506, so no added CMV infection risk
  3. Very little effect on wound healing, so CSA does not impair wound healing

## Tacrolimus (FK 506)
- Newer drug in this group; 100 times more potent than cyclosporine (CSA), so rejection problems are much reduced.
- **Side effect**
  1. Nephrotoxic
  2. HT, DM, DLP — as noted under CSA, DM patients should not use FK 506; but tacrolimus causes less HT and DLP than CSA, so transplant patients with HT and DLP needing this group should get tacrolimus
  3. Leukopenia → risk of CMV, polyoma virus infection
  4. Neurological complication — tremor or seizures should bring tacrolimus to mind
- **Advantages of tacrolimus** vs CSA
  1. 100 times more potent than CSA → better, longer graft survival
  2. Suited to patients with HT and DLP
  3. No gum swelling, no excess hair like CSA

## Antimetabolite
- Act by interfering with DNA and RNA synthesis; especially the newer MMF, which blocks purine synthesis — a starting material for T cell and B cell DNA — so T and B cells are knocked out.
- Two drugs: azathioprine (imuran) and mycophenolate mafetil (as printed) or MMF.
- **Disadvantages of this group**
  1. Bone marrow suppression and CMV infection
  2. Imuran in particular also causes hepatitis and pancreatitis
- Role today: imuran is old, less effective in transplant patients than MMF and has more drawbacks, so it is little used.

## Sirolimus (Rapamycin)
- **Mechanism of action** — blocks IL2; 10 times more potent than cyclosporine (CSA).
- **Side effect**
  1. Thrombocytopenia and leukopenia
  2. Potentiates renal toxicity, so should not be given with CSA or tacrolimus
  3. Hypertriglyceridemia — a significant side effect
  4. Leg swelling
  5. Heart disease
  6. Diarrhoea

## Novel Immunosuppressive Drugs
- Belatacept (anti CD 28) — T cell blocker for maintenance in kidney transplantation only. Compared with cyclosporine, results are similar; its advantage is fewer side effects in almost every respect, except risk of malignancy, which seems higher in the long term.
- For humoral rejection, several new drugs exist, e.g. retuximab, bortezomib, eculizumzb (as printed). Knowing this much is enough, unless you are hungry for more or forced to do a topic — see Schwartz edition 11 p. 362-363.

## Opportunistic Infections
Figure: Timeline of infection after transplantation (months 0-6+) —
- 0-1 month (conventional nosocomial infections): HSV; onset of hepatitis B or C; wound infections, catheter-related infections, pneumonia; Candida.
- 1-6 months (unconventional or opportunistic infections): onset of CMV; EBV, VZV (shingles), influenza, RSV, adenovirus; Nocardia; Listeria, tuberculosis; Pneumocystis; Aspergillus; Cryptococcus (from ~4 months); Strongyloides; Toxoplasma; Leishmania; Trypanosoma cruzi.
- > 6 months (community-acquired or persistent infections): CMV retinitis or colitis; papillomavirus, PTLD; geographically restricted endemic fungi.
- Opportunistic infection is a complication of immunosuppressive drugs.
- First month: bacteria dominate; then viral infection is most common; fungus and protozoa drop in now and then.
- **Viral infection** — mostly 1-6 months after transplant: CMV > herpes simplex > herpes zoster.

## CMV Infection
- Most important, as it causes high graft loss, especially in kidney transplantation.
- Symptoms: fever with abdominal complaints, e.g. abdominal pain, diarrhoea, hepatitis, pancreatitis; graft function worsens — in kidney, creatinine rises.
- **Diagnosis**
  1. Serology for CMV
  2. Upper endoscopy with biopsy to tell infection from rejection
  3. Diarrhoea suggesting CMV colitis → colonoscopy with biopsy
- Now CMV prophylaxis is routine → CMV infection reduced 50%.

## Fungal Infection
- Mostly in the first 3 months after transplant; most common fungi are candida and aspergillus.
- Symptoms: fever, cough, sore throat not improving with antibiotics.
- Sputum or blood shows fungal colonies.
- **Chest x-ray**: aspergillus → upper lobe cavity; unlike protozoal pneumocystis carinii → alveolar infiltration.

## Protozoa Infection
- Organism: pneumocystis carinii; fever, cough, chest pain; treat with bactrim.

## Malignancy
- Patients on immunosuppressive drugs have 400 times the cancer risk of normal people.
- **No. 1: skin cancer**, especially SCC, appearing > 10 years after transplant: a skin lump, discharge, bleeding, wound not healing > 2 weeks → skin biopsy; if SCC → wide local excision (see skin chapter). Reduce immunosuppression to the lowest dose that maintains good graft function.
- **No. 2: lymphoproliferative disorder** — written in more detail below, as it may be examined.
- **No. 3, 4, 5**: gyne, especially cervix; KUB cancer; and Kaposi sarcoma.
- GIST and plasmacytoma are not found after transplant (a wrong choice in exams).

## Posttransplant Lymphoproliferative Disorder (PTLD)
- Abnormal proliferation of B lymphocytes, usually after EBV infection.
- 80% of PTLD are diffuse large B-cell lymphoma — so PTLD essentially is lymphoma.
- Children > adults; kidney transplant recipients more than liver; heart transplant 4%.
- Immunosuppressants linked to PTLD: tacrolimus, OKT-3, antithymocyte globulin.
- Suspect in patients > 1 year after transplant with sudden fever, abdominal pain, some with SBO from GI lymphoma. In heart transplant patients (abdomen never opened) presenting with SBO, adhesion is unlikely — think obstruction from GI lymphoma.
- Other findings: palpable LN, tenderness around the spleen; mouth exam shows red throat and inflamed tonsils; patient thin, wasted, anorexic, weight loss; PTLD often has lymphoma involving the CNS.
- Gold standard for diagnosis: FNA or excisional biopsy.
- **Treatment**
  1. Rather difficult: must reduce immunosuppression and give chemo (CHOP regimen) to treat lymphoma → higher chance of graft loss.
  2. Some try new drugs, e.g. retuximab, but results not very good.
  3. EBV +ve → antiviral treatment.

## Liver Transplantation
- Now the standard treatment in end stage liver disease. **MELD score** (Model of End Stage Liver Disease), calculated from creatinine, bilirubin and INR, ranks urgency — who should get a liver first. Higher MELD → need a liver sooner.
- The surgeons who opened the world of liver transplantation to become standard treatment: Thomas Starzel (as printed) from Denver USA and Sir Roy Calne from UK. Before 1980 1 year survival < 40%; after 1980 1 year survival > 90%.
- Major cause of death after transplant: infection.
- **Indication for liver transplant**
  1. Cirrhosis
     - 1.1 From alcohol: needs psychiatric evaluation and abstinence > 6 months
     - 1.2 HBV: post-op HBIG, interferon and lamivudine → reinfection rate < 20%
  2. HCV — the most common indication in the USA; even with post-op interferon most get HCV reinfection — prevention does not work as for HBV; by 5 years about 30% develop cirrhosis in the graft; so graft survival in HCV is worse than HBV.
  3. HCC — in the USA now 30% of OLT is for HCC; with well-selected patients, recurrence of HCC only 10%.
  4. Autoimmune hepatitis
  5. Fulminant hepatic failure from paracetamol, INH, rifampin
  6. Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)
  7. Primary biliary cirrhosis — this disease gives the best transplant result (Greenfield 5th P. 548); residents should remember
  8. Metabolic disease — e.g. Wilson disease (abnormal copper metabolism), hemochromatosis and alpha 1 antitrypsin deficiency
  9. Biliary atresia — most common indication in children
  10. Polycystic liver disease
  11. Tumor, especially HCC — residents must remember the Milan criteria
- **Milan criteria** (จำให้ได้)
  1. Single tumor < 5 cm (figure label: 1 lesion ≤ 5 cm)
  2. Multiple tumors: < 3 lesions, each < 3 cm (figure label: 2 to 3, none > 3 cm)
  3. No lymphovascular invasion
  4. No extrahepatic disease
- Yao et al. expanded selection criteria (single < 6.5 cm, or < 3 lesions with the largest ≤ 4.5 cm and total of 3 lesions < 8 cm), but this is not yet standard; Milan is still the reference.
- UNOS in the USA: HCC without cirrhosis or portal hypertension → resect if resectable, because donor livers are few and the waiting list is long. Patients getting a transplant should have end staged liver disese (as printed) with MELD score 22. Simply: Child C → transplant; Child A → resect; Child B → transplant only with cirrhosis.
- **Contraindication** (สนใจหน่อย)
  1. HIV +ve — some have transplanted when antivirals left HIV RNA < 50 copies/ml with normal CD4, but post-op immunosuppression soon brings HIV back; so HIV should not be transplanted (remember again).
  2. Existing cancer — except HCC within Milan criteria; other cancers must be cured for > 2-5 years.
  3. Uncontrolled infection outside liver and biliary tree
  4. Heart disease or lung disease with severe pulmonary HT — would surely die under anesthesia
  5. PSC with active acute ulcerative colitis
  6. Cannot take immunosuppressants
  7. Still drinking, cannot stop
- Age is not a contraindication, but recipients should preferably be < 60 years.
- Diabetes and PV thrombosis can still be transplanted.
- Cholangiocarcinoma was said to be unsuitable, but Mayo data: well-selected cases given neoadjuvant treatment then transplant reach 5 year survival 82%; see the biliary chapter on cholangiocarcinoma treatment.
- **Donor selection**
  - Figure: donor procurement — chilled cardioplegia solution through ascending aorta; supraceliac cross-clamp; UW through splenic vein or inferior mesenteric vein; UW through terminal aorta.
  1. ABO matching compatible; Rh not considered as liver cells have no Rh antigen. HLA typing not needed for liver, unlike kidney — kidney must match both ABO and HLA.
  2. No liver from donors with HIV, HBV, HCV.
  3. Donor liver must be disease-free, with steatosis ≤ 30% of liver tissue; steatosis > 50% gives graft nonfunction up to 50%. CT liver shows fatty liver, but the % fat needs liver biopsy.
  4. Living donor — extra selection criteria:
     - 4.1 Donor age 18-55 years and directly blood-related to the patient, e.g. mother giving part of her liver to her child
     - 4.2 Donor strong, with enough liver to share
     - 4.3 No prior major abdominal surgery, except appendectomy or TR only

## OLT Technique
- Three phases:
  1. **Hepatectomy phase** — remove the patient's liver; bleeds heavily. Venous blood returning to the heart is diverted from the liver bed with extracorporeal venovenous bypass; without bypass, use the Piggy back technique — how it is done residents need not know.
  2. **Anenhepatic phase** (as printed) — place the donor liver in the original position; anastomose IVC above and below, hepatic artery, PV, then bile duct: adults usually duct to duct; children usually donor bile duct to jejunum. Bile duct anastomosis healing depends on intact hepatic arterial flow.
  3. **Posthepatic phase** — reperfuse the liver and stop bleeding.
- Figure: recipient procedure — anastomoses of IVC, portal vein, hepatic artery (via celiac axis), with T-tube in common duct.
- When the donor liver is too big to fit, especially for child recipients, the liver can be managed thus:
  1. Liver from the donor soaked in UW solution → **bench work**: trim the liver to suit the recipient before placing it in the abdomen.
  2. **Cadaveric donor**
     - 2.1 Reduced size — cut it smaller before placing
     - 2.2 Split liver — divide into right lobe and left lobe for 2 recipients: right side to an adult, left lobe to a child
  3. **Living donor** — take the lateral segment of the left lobe, e.g. mother to child; the recipient should get at least 30% of liver.
  - Figure: donor liver immediately after donation (left lobe remaining) and two months after donation (regenerated).

## Postoperative Complications
- If the transplanted liver works well: bile flows freely, EV bleeding stops, and consciousness returns.
- Complications are like other major abdominal surgery; those specific to liver transplant:
- **Bile leak**
  - Most common (No. 1), usually in the first month; think of underlying hepatic artery thrombosis too — besides treating the leak, check by doppler whether the anastomosed hepatic artery is occluded.
  - Treatment:
    1. Hepatic artery patent → PTC with stent across the leak, place drain
    2. Hepatic artery occluded → in trouble (ซวยแล้ว)
- **Hepatic artery thrombosis (HAT)**
  - Most common vascular complication, very serious; 3-5%, usually early PO. The hepatic artery gives only 25% of blood flow but up to 50% of oxygen supply.
  - Early PO usually from thrombosis; late PO usually from stenosis and pseudoaneurysm.
  - Symptoms: from mild LFT worsening and reduced bile output, to liver failure.
  - Diagnosis: doppler US, sensitivity and specificity > 90%.
  - Treatment:
    1. **Acute thrombosis** — liver fails fast; once diagnosed, reopen quickly for thrombectomy and revise the anastomosis; if extensive necrosis already, urgently find a new liver.
    2. **Late thrombosis** — usually fever, high liver enzymes; try balloon angioplasty with stent first; if failed, re-do the hepatic artery anastomosis, or if not fixable, re-transplant.
- **Primary nonfunction** (สนใจหน่อย)
  - Most serious — even the toughest surgeon nearly dies: the transplanted liver does not work at all; found within 24 hr.
  - Diagnosis: CNS change, bilirubin > 10, T tube bile output < 20 cc in 12 hr — sometimes scanty from the operation itself; PT and PTT prolong > 1.5 times, OT and PT > 3000 (as printed); hyperkalemia; then hemodynamic instability, refractory metabolic acidosis (not alkalosis), hypothermia, coagulopathy, hepatic encephalopathy, renal failure, then MOF; without a new liver, certain death.
  - Causes:
    1. Graft problem — e.g. liver from an old donor, a CVA death, too much fat, or warm ischemia time too long (usually > 12 hours, as printed)
    2. Recipient problem — many diseases, shocked, or renal failure before operation; even a good graft fails.
  - Treatment: no drug yet treats graft nonfunction, whether prostaglandin or N-acetylcystein; once diagnosed, re-transplant at once. If no liver is available yet, buy a little time with graft hepatectomy with temporary portocaval shunt, or liver dialysis (picture); a new liver can wait about 15 hours, at most ≤ 3 days.
- **Acute rejection**
  - Usually from about day 7 on; symptoms in the first days suggest nonfunction rather than rejection, since the liver rejects less and tolerates rejection better than other organs; no hyperacute rejection as in kidney.
  - Suspect when LFT suddenly rises with doppler US showing patent vessels; confirm by needle biopsy — portal lymphocytosis infiltration, no neutrophils as in cholangitis.
  - Treatment: high dose steroid first; if not controlled, add ALG.
  - Chronic rejection may come from recurrence of the old disease, e.g. HCV reinfection or PSC.
- **Hepatic vein & IVC thrombosis** — presents like Budd Chiari: ascites, edema, deteriorating renal function; luckily rare.
- **Recurrent HCC after OLT** — 15-70%, about 1.5 years after transplant; recurrence in the graft grows very fast since immunosuppressants are taken lifelong.
  - Treatment: resect the tumor in the graft if resectable — better survival; reduce immunosuppression about 3 times. 5 year survival with recurrent tumor in the graft drops from 64% to 22%.

## Kidney Transplantation
- The most commonly performed solid organ transplant, with the highest success: 1-year survival 92-97%.
- **Indications** — end stage renal disease, e.g. chronic glomerulonephritis, chronic pyelonephritis, diabetic nephropathy.
- End stage renal patients who get a kidney have better long term survival and QoL than staying on dialysis.
- **Donor sources** (figure: en bloc kidney procurement with aorta stapled, adrenal glands and perinephric fat)
  1. Brain death donor (cadaveric)
  2. Living donor
  3. No serious infectious disease, e.g. HIV; a donor with mild UTI can still be used.
- **Recipient evaluation**
  1. **Patient evaluation**
     - 1.1 Fit for surgery, not psychiatric, good compliance — e.g. CVA patients, as printed: bedridden with failing kidneys — let them be reborn instead
     - 1.2 No active infection
     - 1.3 No severe uncorrectable aortoiliac occlusive disease
     - 1.4 Cancer patients: disease free > 2-5 years, depending on type — aggressive ones like melanoma and breast wait 5 years; low grade or in situ 2 years is enough
  2. **Immunological evaluation**
     - 2.1 ABO blood group must be identical or compatible
     - 2.2 Tissue typing by HLA matching — HLA-A, B, DR Ag should match between donor and recipient
     - 2.3 Check serum antibodies to HLA
- Preop: transplant patients have many comorbidities — control diabetes and BP, treat heart disease well, clear hidden infection sources such as rotten teeth, UTI, sinusitis first; otherwise post-op immunosuppression will hit the patient hard.
- **Surgical technique** (figure: incision, layers — external and internal oblique, transversus abdominis, transversalis fascia, peritoneum; ureter, hypogastric artery, spermatic cord, inferior epigastric vessels divided; external iliac artery and vein; ureteroneocystostomy)
  1. Supine; curved incision on the right from the pubic tubercle to the tip of rib 11; right side preferred because the right iliac artery and vein are shallower than the left.
  2. When dissecting retroperitoneum over the iliac artery, there are many lymphatic vessels — ligate rather than cauterize to prevent lymphocele.
  3. Place the kidney retroperitoneally in the right iliac fossa without removing the native kidney.
  4. Anastomose graft renal vein to external iliac vein.
  5. Anastomose graft renal artery to external iliac artery; both end to side.
  6. The author sutures vessels with prolene 5-0.
  7. After the artery and vein are done, release the vascular clamps for reperfusion; the kidney turns a lovely pink and urine spurts out at once — the surgeon is happy.
  8. Anastomose ureter to bladder (ureteroneocystostomy) — the author leaves this to the urologist.
- **Likely exam question: no or little urine after surgery** — follow the approach to low urine output post transplantation, Greenfield 6th ed. P 548.
- Figure: Low urine output post kidney transplant, as steps —
  1. No initial urine or drop in urine output → assess catheter patency → assess volume status.
  2. Hypovolemic → saline bolus → urine improved? Yes → continue fluid. No → diuretic challenge.
  3. Euvolemic or overloaded → diuretic challenge → urine improved? Yes → continue fluid, consider lasix infusion. No → doppler ultrasound.
  4. Doppler: adequate blood flow? No → emergent exploration. Yes → delayed graft function → minimize fluids, assess dialysis needs, early biopsy.
- In words:
  1. Flush the Foley's cath — blocked by clot? Or change to a larger Foley. If the Foley is not blocked but no urine:
  2. Measure CVP — CVP > 12 means not dry; try lasix; if still no urine:
  3. Doppler US for renal blood flow:
     - 3.1 Good flow → probably graft dysfunction (ATN) → restrict fluid, with kidney biopsy to rule out rejection
     - 3.2 No flow → probably renal vessel thrombosis — early on, urine is dark and drops off at once; operate urgently before the kidney is lost
     - 3.3 Drug induced nephrotoxicity → check drug blood levels
- **Immunosuppressive drug** — usually triple therapy: CSA or tacrolimus with low dose steroid and MMF or imuran.
- **Result**
  1. Graft survival at 5 years:
     - 1.1 Cadaveric donor survival 80%
     - 1.2 Living donor survival 95%
  2. About 85% of patients no longer need antihypertensive drugs.
- Most common cause of death after surgery: CVS, e.g. MI or CVA; then sepsis and cancer.

## Kidney Rejection
- **Hyperacute rejection** — you see with your own eyes the kidney turn dark purple-black rapidly, right at reperfusion; caused by the recipient already having circulating toxic antibody.
  - Treatment: nephrectomy at once, stop, and go back to dialysis.
- **Acute rejection** — from day 7 to 3 months after surgery; cell mediated rejection.
  - Symptoms:
    1. Graft function worsens: creatinine rises > 25% from baseline, leg swelling, less urine, uremic symptoms begin
    2. Tenderness over the graft
    3. Fever, with infection ruled out
  - Investigation:
    1. US or CT: kidney looks swollen
    2. Best is FNA, to tell rejection from CSA toxicity
  - Treatment:
    1. High dose steroid or pulse steroid 500-1,000 mg per 24 hr for 3 days; if not controlled, step up to:
    2. ATG
    3. OKT3
    4. Tacrolimus
- **Chronic rejection** — about 3 months or more after kidney transplant; from intimal fibrosis of arteries; the commonest cause of graft loss.
  - Symptoms:
    1. Graft function worsens: creatinine slowly rises, proteinuria, then uremia — tired, weak, kidney failing again
    2. BP rises again after having been controlled
  - Treatment:
    1. Results not good — may try MMF in place of CSA or tacrolimus
    2. If MMF fails, return to dialysis and wait for another kidney

## Renal Vein Thrombosis
- Remember: whether renal artery or vein is occluded, fix it quickly before the kidney is lost. Suspect when, 2-3 days after transplant, good urine output suddenly falls, or urine is bloody → doppler US at once.
- Renal vein thrombosis about 5%; the condition most often requiring graft nephrectomy.
- Fast onset: good urine output suddenly stops.
- Urgent doppler US to tell whether artery or vein is occluded.
- **Treatment**: reopen quickly for thrombectomy; if too late and the kidney is lost, nephrectomy.

## Renal Artery Thrombosis
- About 10% of kidney transplants.
- Acute phase: treat like renal vein thrombosis.
- Late: usually an anastomotic stricture problem, mostly in the first 6 months — new HT that is hard to control, high creatinine, possibly a bruit over the graft.
- **Treatment**: balloon angioplasty with stent first; if failed, bypass.

## Lymphocele
- 0.6-1.8%; think of it after 2 weeks.
- Cause: lymph fluid collection, as the pelvis — especially around the iliac artery — has very many lymphatic channels.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Mostly none
  2. If symptomatic, from mass effect compressing neighbours:
     - 2.1 Ureter → obstruction, no urine
     - 2.2 Iliac vein → unilateral leg swelling
     - 2.3 Graft renal artery → HT
- **Investigation**
  1. Serum creatinine rises
  2. US: fluid collection with fine linear septation, not seen in urinoma or abscess; aspirate and send — many lymphocytes confirms it
- **Treatment** — no magic number of cc of lymph needing treatment; most agree asymptomatic or small → US follow-up; growing or with mass effect → treat:
  1. Aspirate once in a while — acceptable
  2. Try percutaneous drainage first; if it fails and lymph keeps flowing, the best method is laparoscopic peritoneal window or marsupialization — internal drainage into the peritoneal cavity where the fluid is absorbed; success rate up to 95%.
  3. Sclerosing agent or pigtail catheter as external drainage — absolutely forbidden (ห้ามทำเด็ดขาด): high infection risk, possibly graft loss.

## Ureter Complication
- **Urine leakage** — most common; usually leaks at the ureter-bladder anastomosis.
  - Symptoms: less urine, lower abdominal pain.
  - US: fluid collection; aspirate and check creatine (as printed) — if high, it is urine.
  - Treatment: drainage with stent; if failed, re-operate.
- **Ureter stenosis** — suspect with hydronephrosis and rising creatinine.
  - Treatment:
    1. Stricture < 2 cm → balloon dilate and stent
    2. Stricture > 2 cm → re-operate

## Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism (HPT)
- 2-5% after kidney transplant, in renal failure patients who had 2ry HPT.
- **Treatment**: subtotal, or total with autotransplant to the forearm (forarm, as printed).
- Indications: see the parathyroid chapter.

## Pancreas Transplantation
- In pancreas transplant we want only endocrine function.
- Now with good immunosuppressants and better surgical technique: graft survival 1 year up to 85%, 5 years 60-70%.
- **Candidate for pancreas transplant** — choose well: insulin dependent DM or type 1 DM, age ≤ 45 years, low C-peptide level, BMI < 32, and above all low operative risk — not bedridden or barely breathing.
- If the patient survives surgery with good graft function, results are excellent: no insulin needed, little diet control, can even cheat with sweets a bit.
- **Not candidates**
  1. DM type 2 or adult onset DM with insulin resistance
  2. Coronary artery disease — PO. deaths are usually MI and CVA
  3. Cancer with disease-free < 2 years
  4. Very obese — usually insulin resistant
  5. HIV, HBV, HCV +ve
- **Donor** (figure: pancreas graft with duodenal segment — superior mesenteric artery, splenic artery, portal vein, proximal duodenal closure, stapled mesentery)
  1. Cadaveric donor — most common
  2. Living donor — lap. distal pancreatectomy
- **Donor criteria**
  1. Not a drinker with attacks of pancreatitis; heavy drinkers with CP — no
  2. Pancreas never injured and never operated on
  3. Donor not DM
- **Three operation types**
  1. **Simultaneous pancreas-kidney transplantation (SPK)** — pancreas and kidney transplanted together, for DM type 1 with chronic renal failure. 1 year kidney graft survival 90%; 1 year pancreas graft survival 75%.
     - Advantage: one hurt, solves everything — so the most common type.
     - Disadvantage: rejection hits both organs.
  2. **Pancreas after kidney transplantation (PAK)** — kidney first, as kidneys are easier to find, especially from living donors such as relatives; when a cadaveric pancreas becomes available, transplant it; results not as good as simultaneous.
  3. **Pancreas transplant alone (PTA)** — DM type 1 only, with kidneys still good.
- **Pancreas transplant technique**
  1. SPK: pancreas usually on the right, kidney on the left; organs usually placed intraperitoneal rather than retroperitoneal.
  2. Anastomose graft SMA and splenic artery to ext. iliac artery.
  3. Anastomose graft portal vein to ext. iliac vein.
  4. Exocrine drainage:
     - 4.1 **Bladder drainage**
       - Advantages: urine amylase can show whether the graft is inflamed or rejecting; leaks are less dangerous than with enteric drainage.
       - Disadvantages: UTI, bladder irritation, fluid & bicarb. loss.
     - 4.2 **Enteric drainage**
       - Advantages: fewer complications, especially infection; draining into small bowel is more physiologic than bladder.
       - Disadvantages: leaks, usually from rejection, can kill. Now enteric drainage is preferred: good immunosuppressants make rejection much rarer, and it has fewer drawbacks than bladder drainage, especially the troublesome UTI.
- After surgery usually MMF, plus CSA or tacrolimus (one of them), with prednisolone.
- **Result**
  1. Diabetic neuropathy improves
  2. Diabetic retinopathy usually stays as before surgery — does not improve
- **Acute rejection of pancreatic transplant**
  - Symptoms: fever, raised WBC, tenderness in the lower abdomen over the graft, blood sugar rises again.
  - Lab: with bladder drainage, urine amylase falls > 25% → the pancreas is no longer working well.
  - Diagnosis: percutaneous needle biopsy under US or CT guidance shows lymphocytes infiltrating throughout the graft.
- **Treatment** — graft saved in 90%:
  1. Start with high dose steroid, as for rejection of other organs
  2. If not controlled, add ALG or OKT3
- **Living donor plancreatic (as printed) transplantation** — to ease the waiting list, use a pancreas from a matched relative: distal pancreatectomy from the donating relative, then anastomose graft splenic artery to the recipient's common iliac artery and graft splenic vein to common iliac vein; pancreatic drainage into bladder or jejunum. Success reported in 160 cases so far.

## Islet Cell Transplantation
- The human pancreas has about 1 million islet cells.
- Instead of transplanting the whole pancreas — as said, we want only endocrine function — isolating just the islet cells and putting them into DM type 1 patients should work and hit the target.
- Pioneering surgeons perfuse a cadaveric pancreas with UW solution at about 4 °C, then inject a collagen-digesting enzyme (liberase enzyme) so the exocrine part breaks into small fragments; the islets are separated and purified, then injected into the PV to lodge in the liver and secrete insulin.
- Figure: islet lodged in a portal vein branch beside a hepatic artery branch and bile ductule.
- In theory this method has a future: no operative risk for the patient, and surgeons need not stand until their testicles ache (as printed). But problems are long term graft survival, and isolating islet cells for 1 patient needs pancreases from up to 4 donors.
- **Route of islet cell injection** — into the portal vein:
  1. Percutaneous
  2. Transhepatic
  3. Minilaparotomy

- **Isolated small bowel transplantation** — small bowel alone.
- **Intestine and multivisceral transplantation** — patients needing bowel transplant often have evidence of chronic liver damage with portal hypertension, and dysmotility of the remaining stomach and bowel, so other organs must be transplanted too = **multivisceral transplantation**. Some need liver, pancreas, stomach and duodenum as well — the transplant surgeon stands operating until exhausted (as printed). (Figure: combined liver-intestinal transplantation.)
- Problem of intestinal transplantation: very frequent rejection, because the small bowel holds many lymphocytes; the graft itself stimulates antibody against the recipient = GVH or **graft versus host reaction**.
- Besides rejection, risk of CMV infection and PTLD also rises.
- Since tacrolimus (calcineurin inhibitor, 100 times more potent than cyclosporine), rejection has fallen and success risen; graft survival at 5 years now nearly 50%.
- Recent data: small bowel transplant survival is better than chronic home TPN.
- **Donor**
  1. Cadaveric — most used
  2. Living donor — now being done: about 200 cm of ileum from the donor, with inflow and outflow from the iliocolic vessels
- **Symptoms of rejection** — starts with diarrhoea from the anus or pouring out of the ostomy; scope shows graft mucosa swollen, red, ulcerated → biopsy: apoptosis of mucosal crypts, which distinguishes it from CMV enteritis. Treatment: a heavy immunosuppressive regimen.
