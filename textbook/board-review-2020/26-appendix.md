# Appendix 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · volume 2, printed pages 247-(end) (PDF pages 1344-1515; printed page n = PDF page 1097 + n)

## Anatomy
- Part of the midgut, wall layers like the colon, columnar epithelium.
- Length about 5-8 cm; lies in the RLQ at the posteromedial wall of the cecum; the appendix can lie in an abnormal position, e.g. LUQ in midgut volvulus, or LLQ in situs inversus.
- Constant location: where the 3 teniae coli converge at the base of the cecum. Figure: colon anatomy (skip).
- Most common position (variation by type): retrocecal 43-64% (43.5%), pelvic about 30% (9.3% in the figure); figure percentages: 1st retrocecal 43.5%; 2nd subcecal 24.4%; 3rd post-ileal 14.3%; 4th pelvic 9.3%; 5th paracecal 5.8%; 6th pre-ileal 2.4%; 7th others 0.27%.
- The position of the tip decides where the pain is felt: pain is at the place the tip points to.
- Blood supply: appendiceal artery, a branch of the ileocolic artery.
- Congenital absence: very rare.

## Function
- Formerly nobody knew; only guesses.
- The most accepted hypothesis now: the appendix is part of GALT (gut-associated lymphoid tissue), secreting IgA (not IgG); GALT is in the thymus-independent lymphocyte group like tonsil and Peyer's patch, helping immunity.
- Lymphoid tissue appears from about 2 weeks after birth, increases gradually to a peak in adolescence (teen age, around puberty), then gradually declines; after age 60 no lymphoid tissue is found at the appendix.
- Conclusion: the belief that the appendix has no function is not right; but the appendix is not essential GALT, since other lymphoid tissue can do the work, so appendectomy does not increase the risk of sepsis like splenectomy.
- Long-term consequences of removal: reports following patients a long time found appendectomy does not raise the risk of colorectal cancer or Crohn's disease; some reports say appendectomy before age 20 changes the risk of ulcerative colitis (lower); and the risk of inguinal hernia after appendectomy is higher, likely from injury to the iliohypogastric nerve causing weakness of transversus abdominis and internal oblique, which act as the shutter of the deep ring.
- Summary: no need for research or debate: if the appendix is diseased, remove it; do not worry.

## Acute Appendicitis
- Common, but young surgeons should not be fooled: each case has its own type (fat, over 100 kg, hard to operate, or slim, or one with seropositive HIV, etc.).
- Age about 20 years; 7% of the population will have acute appendicitis.
- Pathogenesis: luminal obstruction (closed loop is the most important factor):
  1. Fecalith: a hard stool lump stuck in the lumen until the appendix is blocked; the most common cause in adults: 1.1 simple appendicitis: fecalith found 40%; 1.2 gangrene without rupture: 65%; 1.3 gangrene with rupture: 90%.
  2. Lymphoid hyperplasia: often after viral infection; the common cause in children.
  3. Rare: tumor of the appendix (carcinoid, lymphoma, adenocarcinoma), foreign body, seed, barium, parasite (Ascaris lumbricoides). Figure: fecalith obstructing lumen with inflammation (skip).
- Perforation: occurs just beyond the point of obstruction at the antimesenteric border.
- Bacteria: like the colon: aerobe E. coli; anaerobe B. fragilis.
- Peritoneal fluid culture: 1. non-ruptured: no organism; 2. ruptured: positive; the worst is B. fragilis.
- In HIV patients the organism often found is CMV.
- Can appendicitis resolve spontaneously? Answer: yes, it can, but very rarely; the belief that it never resolves is not right.
- Clinical course is a sequence: begins with anorexia, then periumbilical pain (visceral pain), then nausea and vomiting; after about 4-6 hours the pain gradually migrates to the RLQ; this pattern of pain progression is found in about 75%.
- Small tricks: 1. if pain comes after the vomiting, and abdominal pain follows, appendicitis is less likely (vomiting first, then pain); 2. every patient has anorexia: if a patient is still able to eat a whole table, do not think of appendicitis; 3. even if the cecum is in an abnormal position, e.g. malrotation, the symptoms still follow the same sequence; 4. in an older person with RLQ pain and anemia, think of cancer.
### Physical signs
- Most reliable: localized RLQ pain at McBurney's point (Charles McBurney 1845-1913). Figure (skip).
- Other signs help diagnosis and indicate the position of the appendix; minor: Rovsing, Blumberg, psoas, obturator signs. Figure: psoas and obturator sign (skip).
- Do not forget to answer the pelvic picture: do PR in every case; in a woman with active vaginal intercourse do PV with speculum to look at the cervix, every case (Shackelford advice).
### Diagnosis
- The most effective is history with physical examination. Labs: in simple appendicitis WBC is mostly not over 14,000; beware, some cases have normal WBC; neutrophil over 74% or left shift is found 87%, not in every patient; urinalysis does not diagnose appendicitis but helps separate diseases such as UTI.
- In doubtful cases, some suggest CRP, bilirubin, IL-6, procalcitonin: if higher than usual, likely appendicitis and likely perforation (the author thinks measuring interleukin goes too far).
### Scoring systems
- Because the appendix often does not present typically, scores were made to help diagnosis and reduce unnecessary imaging; 2 systems often mentioned: Alvarado score and Appendicitis Inflammatory Response (AIR) score.
- The author says to keep Alvarado briefly: useful for medical students and GPs, to help correct diagnosis and reduce negative appendectomy rate; the author does not weigh these scores much, since they are rather simple and in the elderly not very reliable; Schwartz 8th and 9th editions do not have the Alvarado score; Schwartz 10th and 11th drop it and use the inflammatory response score instead, believed to have more reliable specificity and accuracy than Alvarado.
- Alvarado score (total 10): symptoms: migratory RLQ (right iliac fossa) pain 1; nausea/vomiting 1; anorexia 1; signs: tenderness in right iliac fossa 2; rebound tenderness 1; elevated temperature 1; labs: leukocytosis 2; shift to the left of neutrophils 1.
  - Fever means above 37.3 C; leukocytosis means over 10,000; left shift means neutrophil over 75%.
  - Score 9-10: appendectomy; 7-8: appendectomy or CT/US; 5-6: CT/US; 0-4: unlikely appendicitis.
  - A half-half case should be observed closely, or investigated by US or CT if necessary.
- AIR score (CRP weighs in): vomiting 1; pain in right inferior fossa 1; rebound tenderness or muscular defense: light 1, medium 2, strong 3; body temperature over 38.5 C 1; polymorphonuclear leukocytes 70-84% 1, 85% or more 2; WBC 10.0-14.9 1, 15.0 or more 2; CRP concentration 1-4.9 mg/L 1, 5 mg/L or more 2; sum 0-12. Sum 0-4 low probability: outpatient follow-up; 5-8 indeterminate: inhospital active observation; 9-12 high probability: surgical exploration is proposed.
  - Score 5-6 (as printed) besides observation needs investigation as well.
- Still not enough for some: other scores confuse, e.g. Izbicki, Christian, Samuel, Lintula and many more; the author's opinion: if you want to remember by theory choose the AIR score; in practice, remember the Alvarado score.
### Diagnostic imaging
- Needed only if symptoms, signs and CBC are unclear.
- Plain abdomen: often the first film sent; sensitivity and specificity low; positive findings: 1. appendicolith (fecalith with calcification in the RLQ) found 5%; 2. distended loop of small bowel in the RLQ (localized ileus); 3. cecal ileus; 4. gas-filled appendix; 5. blurred right psoas shadow; 6. periappendiceal fat stranding. Figure (skip).
- US: suits children and pregnancy to avoid radiation; sensitivity 86%, specificity 82%; also helps exclude gynecologic disease in young women (ectopic, ovarian cyst, PID, TOA); disadvantage: operator dependent, obese patients or much bowel gas give unclear pictures. Positive findings: 1. diameter over 6 mm (5 mm or less and compressible is easily not appendicitis); 2. non-compressible, immobilized, tender on pressing; 3. increased vascularity in the appendix wall on duplex Doppler; 4. complex mass suggesting abscess; 5. wall thickness over 2 mm; 6. appendicolith. Figure: US of normal appendix vs appendicitis with ring-of-fire appearance (skip).
- CT: IV contrast alone is enough, no oral or rectal contrast needed; suits adults and non-pregnant; sensitivity 94%, specificity 95%; clear, easy to interpret, not operator dependent; if cost is ignored CT is better than US; suits cases where US cannot decide and helps exclude gynecologic disease; if US already shows appendicitis, no need for CT to confirm. Positive findings: 1. diameter over 6 mm; 2. appendicolith; 3. periappendiceal inflammatory change: fluid around the appendix or fat stranding; 4. abnormal cecal contour: thick like an arrowhead (arrowhead sign); 5. wall thickness over 2 mm with mucosal enhancement. If CT shows intraluminal contrast clearly filling the appendix, it is not appendicitis. Figure: appendicolith, distended appendix with periappendiceal fluid (skip).
- Colonoscopy: for a patient with an appendiceal mass after treatment when the mass has subsided, to rule out cancer; not during acute or active inflammation.
- Laparoscopy: formerly advised for women with equivocal symptoms (fear of a normal appendix, found often in young women, up to 30%), and if a gynecologic cause is found it can be managed, but with today's imaging this makes laparoscopy for diagnosis used much less.
- MRI: no contrast needed; used in pregnant women and children with unclear US (Sabiston 2017 p.1306, Cameron recommend MRI), while some large-volume centers say a pregnant woman with unclear US should have laparoscopy to look, and if truly appendicitis operate laparoscopically; the author thinks MRI works better. Positive: diameter over 6 mm; high signal intensity fluid in the appendiceal lumen.
- BE, used in the old days: not used any more.
- American College of Radiology 2015: 1. imaging for patients whose symptoms and labs are inconclusive; 2. US first; if inconclusive then CT; 3. comparing US and CT, CT is the most accurate; 4. in children, accuracy of US and CT is similar; 5. in adults, US is inferior to CT; 6. in pregnant women US first, if inconclusive MRI.

## Differential Diagnosis
- Many; only those needed are written.
- Acute mesenteric lymphadenitis: the most common error, confused with appendicitis in children with abdominal pain after URI, GI symptoms, few signs, sometimes cervical nodes; self-limiting; if it cannot be separated from appendicitis, treat as appendicitis; if the abdomen is opened and found to be mesenteric adenitis, take the appendix out anyway. Figure: enlarged nodes (skip).
- UTI and pyelonephritis: fever, pain, back pain, sometimes hard to separate from retrocecal appendicitis; urinalysis helps: UTI shows bacteria; a retrocecal appendicitis irritating the ureter also shows WBC in urine but no bacteria as in UTI.
- Ruptured Graafian follicle: pelvic pain during ovulation; blood and fluid irritate the peritoneum; absorbed within 2 days and the pain goes; classic: midcycle pain (Mittelschmerz, German for middle pain); fever not high, symptoms few; if it cannot be separated from appendicitis, treat as appendicitis.
- Yersiniosis: caused by Yersinia, a bacillus from contaminated food or unpasteurized milk; main symptoms: fever, abdominal pain, diarrhea from ileitis, colitis and lymphadenitis; some have skin rash and joint pain; does not involve the gallbladder (if cholecystitis is found it is not yersiniosis); treatment: doxycycline, aminoglycoside, quinolone.
- Rectus sheath hematoma on the right: read in the abdominal wall chapter.
- Neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis): in immunocompromised patients: leukemia, bone marrow transplantation, solid tumor or colorectal cancer on chemo, HIV: transmural inflammation of the intestinal wall especially terminal ileum, cecum and ascending colon; drugs that can cause typhlitis: taxane, cisplatin, irinotecan, gemcitabine, 5-FU. Pathogenesis unclear: believed (1) toxic effect of chemo on gut mucosa; (2) leukemic cell infiltration of the intestinal wall. RLQ pain, low WBC and neutrophils: consulted at 4 pm in an ALL patient on chemo to rule out appendicitis; hard to examine; imaging: CT shows the appendix looks normal, only a thick cecal wall with pericolic inflammation. Figure: cecal thickening (skip).
  - Treatment: 1. NPO, NG tube, IV antibiotic, observation, stop chemo; the principle: typhlitis without complication: no operation; 2. with complication (perforation, abscess, gas in the bowel wall suggesting rotten bowel): open the abdomen: 2.1 lesion in the colon: resect but do not anastomose, bring out colostomy first; 2.2 lesion in the ileum: resect and anastomose.

## Treatment of Acute Appendicitis
- In a patient with classic symptoms and signs, CT or US does not add accuracy; considering benefit vs harm, besides higher cost, CT exposes the patient to radiation and delays treatment, adding morbidity; if US is done and the appendix is not seen, still operate.
- So once acute appendicitis is diagnosed, treatment is appendectomy: when the patient and OR are ready, operate; before surgery NPO, IV fluid, IV antibiotic.
- Postoperative antibiotics (Cameron 12e p.268): if the appendix is not rotten and not ruptured, postoperative antibiotics may be unnecessary; if gangrenous or ruptured: IV ceftriaxone + metronidazole followed by oral Augmentin for 7 days.
- Counseling: appendectomy in many medical schools is done by a first-year resident, intern or medical student, except VIP cases; the one who talks to the patient and relatives needs skill, to avoid trouble if results do not match expectation.
- What patient and relatives worry about often: 1. "Are you sure it is appendicitis? no x-ray, no scan, just pressing the abdomen?"; 2. "Can't you inject antibiotic alone, no surgery? I fear pain, fear scar, can I wear 2-piece swimwear?"
- What to explain: 1. the disease that brought you is a surgical disease: you have about 90% chance of appendicitis and the remaining 10% are diseases that need surgery anyway, e.g. gynecologic disease such as ruptured or twisted cyst (be careful with male patients: the answer "no children, no infertility" does not apply); 2. appendicitis is diagnosed by symptoms and examination with CBC as the main tools; x-ray or scan is used if unclear or to separate other diseases; 3. treatment by non-operative antibiotics only is used rarely, in selected cases, especially those with an appendiceal mass after inflammation subsided: in 1890 Sir Frederick Treves (London hospital) treated an appendicitis case conservatively and waited until the inflammation subsided, then operated 7 days later; at that time no antibiotics existed; the patient he treated this way was successful, but later the daughter of a person (the surgeon's relative) had appendicitis, not operated by the 2-day standard, then the appendix ruptured with sepsis and she died; since then he concluded: once diagnosed appendicitis, treatment is appendectomy. Nowadays there are successful reports of treating simple (non-rotten, non-ruptured) appendicitis with antibiotics without appendectomy, especially in children, but it is not yet a standard of care.
- Guideline approach in Schwartz 9e (good); Schwartz 11e dropped it. Figure (algorithm), as steps: history and physical examination -> classic presentation (short duration of pain, abdominal rigidity, migration of pain to RLQ, pain centered in the RLQ, RLQ tenderness, anorexia): appendectomy; equivocal presentation: male or nonpregnant female: CT; pregnant: US: appendicitis: appendectomy; indeterminate or appendix not seen: ultrasonography; normal findings or alternative diagnosis: supportive care or treatment.
- Guideline approach in Sabiston 20e p.1298 (better than Schwartz). Male: classic presentation, localized peritonitis: laparoscopic appendectomy; equivocal: CT: positive: lap appendectomy; negative: brief observation: no improvement: diagnostic laparoscopy; improving: discharge; other diagnosis: treat as indicated. Female: pregnant: follow pregnancy algorithm; not pregnant: CT with the same branches as above.
- Appendectomy can be delayed about 12 hours without affecting the patient's outcome.
- History: the first reported appendectomy was in 1735 in England by Claudius Amyand, in a patient with a strangulated indirect inguinal hernia whose sac contained a rotten appendix; he removed it and ligated the stump; unknown whether the patient died of sepsis. Figure (skip).
- Currently there are many ways to remove the appendix, open or laparoscopic.

### Open or laparoscopic?
- Following the laparoscopic trend: ACS NSQIP data: 50-75% of appendectomies in America are done laparoscopically. Conversion to open: uncomplicated appendicitis about 3.5%; complicated appendicitis 18%. Figure: port sites and stapler across the appendix base (skip).
- Advantages of laparoscopic appendectomy vs open:
  1. Less pain (some RCTs say not true, but patients are discharged earlier and return to work sooner).
  2. Less wound infection.
  3. Fewer negative appendectomies, especially in obese patients and pregnancy, where abdominal signs are hard to examine: in doubtful cases with unclear diagnosis, some advise looking first by laparoscope; if truly appendicitis, proceed to lap appendectomy.
  4. Can look at other organs in the abdomen, e.g. sigmoid colon and upper abdomen, better than open.
- Disadvantages: 1. longer operating time; 2. more expensive; 3. in complicated and uncomplicated appendicitis alike, wound infection less but intraabdominal complications such as abscess higher than open (the issue is debated: some RCT groups say not true, but most say true); 4. not suitable for severe sepsis, extensive adhesion in the lower abdomen, severe COPD, third trimester pregnancy; 5. 3 port scars still visible.
- Summary: laparoscopic appendectomy is one option, not yet standard like laparoscopic cholecystectomy; Cameron p.258-259 says use what you want; lap suits women of reproductive age more than the elderly; the author notes that Cameron's author also wrote in the minimally invasive chapter that RCT data show laparoscopic is not better than open; so use per convenience; Schwartz 2019 cheers lap.
- To reduce the scar from ports, new techniques: 1. single port laparoscopic appendectomy: one larger port through the umbilicus holding a camera and instruments; scar hidden in the umbilicus, though it may make the umbilicus a bit distorted; 2. NOTE appendectomy: endoscope through natural orifice, either the stomach or the vagina (posterior vaginal fornix), keeping the abdominal wall clean but the vagina loses its cleanliness. Figures (skip).

### Preoperative orders and antibiotic
- NPO, IV antibiotic, labs ready; in complicated cases (suspected rupture or diffuse peritonitis) insert Foley to monitor urine output; if the abdomen is very distended put NG tube.
- Antibiotic: prophylactic to prevent wound infection and intraabdominal abscess; by operative finding: 1. not ruptured: antibiotic 24 hr; 2. ruptured or rotten: antibiotic until afebrile and at least 24 hr, or until WBC returns to normal, then oral form to complete 7-10 days; 3. immunocompromised: give antibiotic as for ruptured, even if not ruptured.

### Incision
1. Grid iron (McBurney) incision: oblique incision at McBurney's point perpendicular to the line; not so pretty, but wide exposure. Figure (skip).
2. Rockey-Davis and Lanz incision: transverse along the skin crease, a prettier wound, suited to cases where rupture is not suspected. Figure (skip).
3. Lower midline: used with diffuse peritonitis or appendix suspected already ruptured.

### Technique
- Open surgery: enter and find the tenia coli, follow it down to the base, find the appendix; do it slowly, do not be rough, until the appendix ruptures; a trainee assisting 2-3 cases can usually do it alone.
- Beware ligation of the appendix, and cut the appendix at a stump about 5 mm (no longer) from the base of the cecum, because stump appendicitis has been reported; after removal cauterize the stump mucosa; some invaginate with a purse-string suture.
- Do not forget to send every appendix to pathology.
- If the abdomen is opened and a ruptured appendix is found: remove it; if lap finds rupture, an expert can continue by lap without conversion; if the appendix is not ruptured but rotten, remove it and wash the abdomen; the odor is strong.
- If after removal the base has necrosis: formerly advised tube cecostomy, leaving the tube about 2-3 weeks, then pulling it out to control the fistula in not over 7 days; now a cecostomy is not answered; instead assess severity of necrosis: debride at the base of the appendix until good cecal tissue: 1. if the defect is not large: Z-stitch, purse-string or 2-layer closure of the cecum to invert the base, or if the hole is large and purse-string impossible, partial or wedge cecectomy then 2-layer closure like a bowel suture; 2. if extensive necrosis extending to the IC junction or the base looks poor: ileocecectomy; 3. drain around the stump: by theory abdominal drains do not prevent intraabdominal abscess and even have disadvantages such as longer hospital stay, but in practice some surgeons place one.
- Figure: steps A-D: division of the appendiceal artery in the mesoappendix; ligation of the stump; purse-string and invagination (skip).
- Drain: if a drain was placed, a Penrose left 1 piece as the boss did; if no fever, eating, no stool coming through the drain: no leak, then take the drain off.

### Unexpected findings at operation
- Abscess collection: the heart is to drain it; if the appendix can be removed, remove it.
- A hard mass that cannot rule out tumor: do not hurry; right hemicolectomy and treat as tumor.
- Normal appendix: the key: look for other causes in the abdomen that may explain the pain; do not close and then convert to lap; either use a scope to look, or close and hurry to CT; answer like a pro. Things to do:
  1. Check what color and smell the fluid has, which gives a clue to which organ is diseased.
  2. Examine cecum and mesentery: if node enlargement, biopsy (may be TB).
  3. Run the terminal ileum about 2 ft: if Meckel is found and normal, not inflamed, do not fuss with it; if ileum looks like Crohn, note it.
  4. Beware a redundant sigmoid colon lying on the right side: sigmoid diverticulitis can have pain exactly like appendicitis.
  5. In a woman: examine uterus, tubes, ovary: young women (the book jokes about "entertaining" partners) often have PID; if PID is found, treat: appendectomy to prevent future confusion, together with broad-spectrum IV antibiotics for PID.
  6. If the lesion is likely on the lower side but hard to assess, extend the incision Fowler-Weir style medially to open widely and look.
  7. If the lower side is normal, try to examine the upper abdomen with retractors; if turbid fluid is found, pour NSS and suck out with a sponge in the gutter; green bile-colored fluid or food residue may be PU perforation draining to the lower right; also try to see the gallbladder for inflammation.
  8. If a lesion likely lies at the top, close the appendix wound and open an upper midline incision.
  9. If it is not Crohn with cecal base involvement, and the diagnosis before surgery was appendicitis but the appendix is normal: treat the cause of the pain and do appendectomy as well, to prevent confusion if RLQ pain returns.
  10. Example: PID found: appendectomy with leaving SO and PO antibiotics.
- Note from the author: in a surgeon's life we meet unexpected findings often: e.g. preop diagnosis appendicitis but opening finds another pathology unrelated; at such times you may cry for help but it is hard to close and refer; sometimes the situation cannot refer; so a surgeon must have knowledge and be able to decide correctly and quickly.
- Own appendectomy: reported in 1961: Dr. Leonid Rogozov, the only surgeon of the Soviet Antarctic expedition, removed his own inflamed appendix under local anesthesia (about 1 hr 45 min, recovered, back to work after 7 days). Figure (skip).

## Appendiceal Mass and Periappendiceal Abscess
- When an inflammatory mass is found in the RLQ, besides appendicitis, think of other causes, e.g. cancer of appendix or cecum, IBD, cecal diverticulitis.
- US or CT helps separate phlegmon vs abscess: phlegmon is matted bowel loops and omentum around the inflamed appendix but no pus in the mass. Figure (skip).
### Approach
1. If walling-off is good: patients often come late with low-grade fever and RLQ pain with a palpable mass, symptoms not severe, stable: conservative treatment with antibiotics and follow-up of symptoms and mass size: about 90% respond within 48-72 hr; give the drug about 7 days and the mass subsides; conclusion: non-operative management has fewer complications and reoperation than opening immediately, because the appendix often cannot be found, the bowel perforates and fistula follows.
2. If conservative treatment fails: no improvement within 2-3 days, still high fever, mass not shrinking, WBC high:
   - 2.1 Well-delineated abscess on CT: try percutaneous drainage under CT guidance first (principle: avoid surgery as it has many complications).
   - 2.2 If percutaneous drainage is not possible or not working: open carefully to drain the abscess; if the appendix is found and is removable, remove it; if not found, do not hunt.
3. In cases that respond well: about 4-6 weeks later, appoint the patient, especially over 40 years, for colonoscopy to exclude tumor.
4. Interval appendectomy: formerly advised in every case to prevent recurrent abscess; now many oppose: Liu et al. reported 1,012 patients, 854 (85%) with nothing done except 4-year follow-up: only 5% had recurrent appendicitis needing surgery, and if recurrent surgery was needed the morbidity was not higher than standard; recurrence usually within the first 6 months, not over 3 years; another report of over 6,000 patients followed 8 years: recurrence only 8%. Conclusion: routine interval appendectomy is not justified; doctor's duty: advise early return if RLQ pain returns.
   - Groups where interval appendectomy is more attractive: children, and patients with appendicolith on imaging (higher recurrence than adults).
   - Sabiston (older edition) advised interval appendectomy in every case since 15-25% recur; at 6 weeks set for lap appendectomy; Sabiston 20e no longer mandates, using the word "consider".
- Figure (Sabiston algorithm for appendiceal abscess), as steps: appendiceal abscess -> CT or US: abscess over 4-6 cm: antibiotics + drainage; phlegmon +/- small abscess: fever/sepsis: antibiotics + drainage; afebrile: regular diet + oral antibiotics + discharge. Children: improvement: regular diet + oral antibiotics + discharge, interval appendectomy; adults: improvement: regular diet + oral antibiotics + discharge -> colonoscopy 2-4 weeks: no neoplasm: consider interval appendectomy; neoplasm: staging/colectomy; no improvement: operate.
- Figure (Sabiston 20e p.1304, approach to delayed presentation of suspected appendicitis), as steps: diffuse peritonitis present: yes: resuscitation, antibiotics, OR for source control; no: CT: other diagnosis: treat as indicated; appendicitis confirmed: abscess: CT drainage feasible: yes: CT drainage + antibiotics; no: laparoscopic drainage + antibiotics; phlegmon: antibiotics; then adult: colonoscopy after discharge: normal: consider interval appendectomy; neoplasm: follow algorithm for appendiceal neoplasm; child: consider interval appendectomy.
- Summary: no routine interval appendectomy now; but if not removing the appendix, follow up the case well.
- Considerations for percutaneous drainage: 1. mass larger than 4-6 cm (some cut at over 3 cm) together with IV antibiotic (healing faster); 2. must be a well-delineated abscess; 3. conservative treatment failed: still fever, mass not shrinking.

## Incidental Appendectomy
- Removing the appendix during an operation for other disease, hoping to prevent future appendicitis; the concept is accepted less now, except to reduce the risk of diagnostic confusion in the future.
- Indications: 1. children who need chemotherapy; 2. Crohn disease without cecal base involvement; 3. diseases with a chance of recurrent abdominal pain confusion, e.g. mesenteric lymphadenitis, PID, endometriosis; 4. children with malrotation of midgut who have a Ladd procedure: the appendix is placed in the LUQ, so an attack of appendicitis would be very hard to diagnose; 5. going to an area without medical care.

## Complications of Appendectomy
- Wound infection: the most common complication of ruptured appendicitis; in the subcutaneous layer, sometimes deeper to muscle; if fever and increasing wound pain on day 4 after surgery, think wound infection first; organism mixed: E. coli and B. fragilis. Treatment: open the wound completely, irrigate, change dressing often; when clean, consider delayed closure.
- Pylephlebitis: rare now with effective antibiotics, but when found the mortality is high; the most serious complication: septic thrombosis of the ileocolic vein extending to the SMV, then to the liver forming multiple pyogenic liver abscesses; symptoms: high fever with chills, enlarged liver, RUQ pain, then sepsis, DIC and death; CT shows gas or thrombus in the portal vein, or multiple liver abscesses. Figure (skip). Treatment: hurry antibiotic, treat the cause, e.g. remove the appendix if appendicitis, drain liver abscess; whether to give anticoagulation for treatment and to prevent DIC is still debated.
- Fecal fistula: usually leak at the appendiceal stump; luckily the fistula is not toxic or irritating like small bowel fistula unless free perforation spreading in the abdomen; most patients tolerate it; the fistula usually closes within 6 weeks.

## Acute Appendicitis in the Young
- Symptoms in children are not specific: harder to diagnose than adults because: 1. high fever and more GI upset than adults; 2. examination is hard: the child only cries and struggles; 3. CT in children is not specific like adults since they have little body fat, so the inflamed appendix is hard to separate from surrounding bowel; so US works better in children.
- Differential diagnosis is hard: mesenteric adenitis is often missed.
- High rupture rate, especially under 5 years: symptoms are severe because the omentum is short and walling-off is poor.
- In adults, WBC over 18,000 raises suspicion of rupture; in children WBC levels are not specific: often rupture with WBC under 18,000.
- Lap appendectomy in children: acceptable, but know: 1. takes longer; 2. complications of Veress needle puncture seem higher; 3. more expensive, and recovery not faster than adults since children are children.

## Acute Appendicitis in the Elderly
- Hard to diagnose like children because symptoms are not typical: about 18% have no abdominal pain; classic signs and symptoms are present in only 50%; some look like partial bowel obstruction; many only have poor appetite and abdominal bloating, so diagnosis is delayed; WBC in the elderly is often normal.
- In the elderly with appendicitis rupture is common (40-70%) and morbidity high: the omentum, though longer than in children, is inefficient at walling off; when the appendix ruptures pus spreads quickly.
- Elderly patients operated for suspected appendicitis have normal appendix more than other age groups.
- An elderly patient with a phlegmon mass: think of underlying cancer: if a mass is found do not be alarmed: operate like cancer: right hemicolectomy right away.

## Acute Appendicitis during Pregnancy
- The most common non-obstetric surgical condition in pregnancy; found 1 in 1,500 pregnancies, but incidence is not higher than non-pregnant women.
- Pain location depends on the appendix position, which rises and moves laterally with the size of the uterus: in the 3rd trimester pain is at the RUQ or flank. Figure: appendix position by month: 3 mo, 4 mo, 5 mo, 6 mo, 8 mo rising above the iliac crest toward the umbilicus line (skip).
- Most common trimester: 2nd; risk of fetal loss highest in 1st trimester; risk of perforation highest in 3rd trimester (the larger the uterus, the harder the diagnosis).
- CBC: WBC 15,000 may be normal in pregnancy, so look at neutrophil predominance or left shift.
- Because diagnosis is hard, normal appendix is found at operation up to 30%; US is the preferred imaging: do US routinely in pregnant women suspected of appendicitis, since it also looks at the baby; avoid x-ray or CT in the 1st trimester (radiation effect).
- If US is unsure or does not see the appendix: in the 2nd trimester CT can be done, or MRI as Schwartz 11th recommends (American standard: fear that a negative appendectomy exceeds the radiation exposure to the fetus, hence favoring MRI); if CT or MRI is inconclusive and cannot rule out appendicitis, laparoscopy is another choice, since if appendicitis is seen it can be removed at once.
- Since the normal appendix rate at operation is 23-33%, many send US routinely; the problem is that US sensitivity in some reports is only 20%; current recommendation: use MRI, which although a bit costly is cheaper than operating and finding a normal appendix, and avoids ionizing radiation.
- But if history, examination and basic labs fit acute appendicitis, do not worry about the trimester: if it must be operated, operate.
- The author prefers the guideline in Sabiston 20e p.1306 (clearer than Schwartz): in doubtful cases screen with US first; if still unsure, confirm with MRI. Figure (algorithm), as steps: pregnant patient with suspected appendicitis: diffuse peritonitis present: yes: resuscitation, antibiotics, OR for source control; no: US: other diagnosis confirmed: treat as indicated; appendicitis confirmed: appendectomy; US negative: MRI: positive for appendicitis: appendectomy; negative: observe: improves: discharge; worsens: diagnostic laparoscopy.
- Open or laparoscopic appendectomy: abortion risk 10% either way.
- Emergency appendectomy in pregnancy: risk of premature labor 11% (about the same as other emergency operations); if the appendix has ruptured, premature labor up to 30%.
- Pregnant women have a high DVT risk: use a pneumatic compression device during surgery; monitor fetal heart sounds during surgery.
- Open or lap both possible; laparoscopy needs an experienced operator and attention to uterine size; risk and complications similar to open.
- Fetal mortality: appendix not ruptured 2%; ruptured 6%.

## Acute Appendicitis in HIV Patient
- Found in 0.5% of the HIV population, higher than the normal host (0.1-0.2%).
- Rupture rate is higher than the general population, about 45%, because of delayed diagnosis (thinking of ordinary diarrhea or abdominal upset rather than appendicitis); the lower the CD4, the higher the rupture risk.
- HIV patients with appendicitis do not often have leukocytosis; presentation is the same as in general appendicitis.
- Differential diagnosis: CMV, TB, Kaposi, lymphoma, typhlitis.
- With typical presentation, no need for many investigations: operate; except atypical symptoms, e.g. diarrhea more than RLQ pain, then US or CT.
- Operative mortality is not higher than normal hosts, except ruptured appendicitis in HIV has higher morbidity.
- Summary: treatment of acute appendicitis in HIV is appendectomy, not antibiotic.

## Appendiceal Tumors
- Tumors found with appendicitis are very rare; on pathology about 0.9% of appendix specimens are tumors:
  1. Adenocarcinoma: the most common; 2. carcinoid or neuroendocrine tumor: second most common (details in the GI carcinoid chapter); 3. others: mucocele, pseudomyxoma peritonei, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, GIST, leiomyoma, leiomyosarcoma, ganglioneuroma.
- Since most are adenocarcinoma, WHO classifies appendiceal cancers of mucosal origin as (the author finds the WHO naming confusing):
  1. Non-mucinous adenocarcinoma: behaves like colon and rectal adenocarcinoma.
  2. Mucinous appendiceal neoplasm: 2.1 low grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (LAMN): before WHO's reclassification called mucocele; 2.2 high grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (HAMN): equals mucinous adenocarcinoma; 2.3 pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP): low grade and high grade.
  3. Signet ring cell carcinoma. 4. Undifferentiated carcinoma.

### Appendiceal adenocarcinoma
- Very rare; adenocarcinoma is found most at the base, while carcinoid is found most at the tip. Figure (skip).
- Presentation: like acute appendicitis, so diagnosis before surgery is very hard; some present perforated; rupture does not make the prognosis worse.
- Treatment: if known before surgery, do right hemicolectomy once, size does not matter.
- Problem: knowing only after surgery from pathology; then complete staging and treat like right colon cancer; for TNM AJCC 2017 appendiceal cancer is staged like colon and rectum, except LAMN classed as Tis.
- Since 30% have synchronous and metachronous neoplasms elsewhere in the GI tract, after surgery work up further, including spread, like CRC.
- Overall 5-year survival 55%.

### Appendiceal mucinous neoplasm
- Found in not over 0.3% of appendix specimens; over 50% were preoperatively diagnosed as appendicitis, the rest found incidentally; symptoms non-specific.
- Causes of mucoid accumulation (4 diseases): 1. retention cyst or simple mucocele; 2. mucosal hyperplasia; 3. cystadenoma, now called LAMN by WHO, which has a chance of becoming cancer in the future; 4. cystadenocarcinoma: the only malignant subtype, 5-year survival about 65%.
- CT helps diagnosis: well-encapsulated cystic mass 2-20 cm in the RLQ, most not over 2 cm, 50% may have calcification; an enhancing nodule on CT suggests cancer. Figure: specimen and CT (skip).
- Treatment:
  1. Appendectomy alone: for an intact lesion (not ruptured), or pathology reports low grade LAMN confined to the appendix (formerly mucocele); HAMN, found rarely, is treated like LAMN but followed closely because of high recurrence risk.
  2. Right hemicolectomy when: 2.1 after appendectomy, pathology finds tumor cells at the specimen margin (positive margin) or at the base of the appendix; 2.2 report of mucinous adenocarcinoma confined to the appendix; 2.3 invasion to muscularis propria (T2); 2.4 high mitotic activity; 2.5 size 2 cm or more; 2.6 positive periappendiceal node; 2.7 tumor rupture.
- If pathology says mucinous adenocarcinoma with peritoneal dissemination: treat as pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP).
- If planning a laparoscopic appendectomy and the appendix looks like a mucocele: convert to open, since rupture during surgery that turns out to be cancer would be a problem; open also lets one see whether mucoid fluid accumulates in other spaces, especially those hard to see by scope, such as retrohepatic or deep pelvic space, or mucinous nodules on the omentum.
- If upon opening a patient with an appendiceal mucocele mucinous fluid is already spread in the abdomen, take the fluid out for examination, and beware: finding epithelial cells outside the appendix shows it is already pseudomyxoma peritonei.

## Pseudomyxoma Peritonei (PMP)
- Very rare, about one in a million of patients who have an appendectomy.
- Believed to arise from neoplastic mucus-secreting cells spreading in the peritoneal cavity; the origin is mostly a ruptured appendiceal mucocele, less often an ovarian mucinous tumor; the mucus implants on the peritoneal surface and omentum.
- PMP from LAMN spreads only inside the abdominal cavity; PMP from HAMN spreads inside the abdomen from the diaphragm down to the pelvis and also invades organs, and rarely lymphatic and hematogenous spread. Figure: operative photo and CT (skip).
- From the look of mucus-secreting cells the histology cannot easily say it is cancer.
- Symptoms: F > M; abdominal pain more than appendicitis, bloating or palpable abdominal mass, rather chronic, often taking years, most known at surgery for appendicitis or gynecologic cancer.
- Preoperative workup (known or suspected): CT abdomen/pelvis to assess operability; laparoscopy to assess peritoneal surface and staging; tumor markers CEA and CA-125 (ovarian cancer).
- Treatment:
  1. Surgical debulking or cytoreductive surgery with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC): long operation, risky, costly, but improves 5- and 10-year survival; now cytoreductive surgery and HIPEC is the standard of care. Cytoreductive surgery consists of: 1.1 remove gross disease and omentum entirely; 1.2 appendectomy, no need for right hemicolectomy since it does not add survival; 1.3 in women TAH + BSO; 1.4 peritonectomy from the diaphragm downward (Sugarbaker advises).
  2. HIPEC, or by some early postoperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy (EPIC).
  3. Recurrence: re-operate if able, but the 2nd operation is usually hard with many morbidities such as fistula and leak.
- The most famous person who does this much: Paul H. Sugarbaker (author's remarks of admiration non-clinical).

### HIPEC (Hyperthermic Intraoperative Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy)
- Figure: HIPEC machine with roller pump, heat exchanger, temperature probes, inflow and outflow catheters (skip).
- Generally, a patient with carcinomatosis gets only supportive care while waiting to die.
- After Sugarbaker's reports: 1. appendix cancer and pseudomyxoma peritonei with carcinomatosis: treated with HIPEC in almost 800 patients over 25 years; some patients lived up to 20 years; 2. mucinous colorectal adenocarcinoma (MCA) with peritoneal carcinomatosis and cancer confined to the abdomen: HIPEC in almost 200 patients over 25 years: about 30% alive at 5 years, operative mortality only 1.8%, morbidity: pancreatitis 7% and pancreatic fistula 47% (others report operative mortality 20% and high complication rates).
- HIPEC is an adjunct aimed at microscopic cancer cells spread in the abdomen: after extensive cytoreductive surgery removing tumor as completely as possible, chemo is put into the abdomen at 200-1,000 times the IV concentration, with the abdominal temperature kept at 41-42 C, which makes the drug penetrate tumor cells 5-10 times more (increased cytotoxic effect).
- Patient selection is as important as the operation: 7 criteria all of which must be met:
  1. ECOG performance status 2 or less. 2. No evidence of extra-abdominal disease. 3. No evidence of biliary or ureteral obstruction. 4. No evidence of intestinal obstruction at more than 1 site. 5. Up to 3 small, resectable liver metastases. 6. No evidence of bulky tumor at the hepatoduodenal ligament. 7. Peritoneal cancer index of 20 or less.
- (Normally solid organ metastasis is a contraindication to peritonectomy, but the author's teacher says it is acceptable.)
- Cytoreductive procedure: 1. peritonectomy removing all peritoneum from the diaphragm to the pelvis; 2. greater omentectomy and splenectomy; 3. cholecystectomy with stripping of hepatoduodenal ligament and floor of omental bursa; 4. hepatectomy; 5. rectosigmoidectomy; 6. TAH + BSO + resection of the cul-de-sac of Douglas. Figures (skip).
- After cytoreduction until R0 is not achievable (accept the best possible), the abdomen is held open with a retractor frame (Coliseum technique), washed with NSS until clean, the temperature adjusted to 41-42 C, chemo put in, and the surgeon stirs the viscera by hand so all surfaces contact the chemo. Figure: Coliseum technique (skip).
- Assessment systems: peritoneal cancer index (PCI) measures the severity of peritoneal metastasis; completeness of cytoreduction (CC) level has 4 levels.
- Recently the American Society of Peritoneal Surface Malignancies issued a staging classification: peritoneal surface disease severity score (PSDSS) in 2014, using clinical symptoms, PCI and histology: if symptoms are severe, PCI over 20 and histology G3 (signet ring), cytoreductive surgery + HIPEC does not add survival: give systemic treatment.
- Standardized HIPEC: 1. method: closed; 2. drug: mitomycin C; 3. dosage: 40 mg; 4. timing: 30 mg at time zero, 10 mg at 60 minutes; 5. volume of perfusate 3 L.
6. Inflow temperature 42 C. 7. Duration of perfusion 90 minutes.
- American Society of Peritoneal Surface Malignancies (ASPSM) staging (PSDSS) table: Clinical: no symptoms 0; mild symptoms 1; severe symptoms 6. CT-PCI: PCI under 10 (low) 1; PCI 10-20 (medium) 3; PCI over 20 (high) 7. Histology: G1 or G2 with N-, L-, V- 1; G2 with N+ and/or L+ and/or V+ 3; G3 or signet ring 9. Total score: 2-3 stage I; 4-7 stage II; 8-10 stage III; over 10 stage IV.
  - Mild symptoms = weight loss under 10% of body weight, mild abdominal pain, some ascites. Severe symptoms = weight loss over 10%, unremitting pain, bowel obstruction, symptomatic ascites.
  - Peritoneal cancer index (PCI): by imaging (CT, PET, MRI) or exploration (laparoscopy, or at the first operation in synchronous peritoneal carcinomatosis).   - This detail need not be memorized; read Cameron 12e 2017 p.262-264 if needed.
- Guideline for appendiceal tumors (Sabiston 20e p.1309), as steps: tumor detected at operation: tumor over 2 cm: right hemicolectomy; 2 cm or less: base involved? yes: right hemicolectomy; no: perforation? no: appendectomy; yes: evidence of mucin spillage or mucinous ascites? no: appendectomy; yes: appendectomy + peritoneal lavage, consider referral for cytoreductive surgery/intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC). Tumor found after appendectomy: adenocarcinoma: right hemicolectomy; carcinoid: under 1 cm: observation; over 2 cm: right hemicolectomy; 1-2 cm: lymphovascular invasion yes: right hemicolectomy; no: observation. Note: all patients with appendiceal neoplasm should undergo routine colonoscopy.
