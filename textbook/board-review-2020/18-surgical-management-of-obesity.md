# Surgical Management of Obesity 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 973-986 (PDF pages 977-990)

- Morbid obesity: weight beyond the standard until it endangers the body continuously; use BMI of 30 or more; besides being teased as "fat", obesity carries a chance of many associated diseases.
- The resident under exam stress eats more, the more stress, the more eating, which wrecks body shape; the author jokes that some people look so bloated that they need surgery more than treatment of their fever.
- Bariatric surgery: surgery that can reduce weight and cure metabolic disease.
- Metabolic surgery: surgery to cure metabolic disease, especially type 2 DM, without the purpose of reducing weight, e.g. duodenojejunal bypass.
- Surgeons who operate on obesity often get fat quickly, the operation fee is rather high, so statement numbers soar; surgery for weight reduction is therefore one field that residents with limited funds who have many bills should consider.
- Classification of obesity has been newly revised by WHO (given because it is the reference in Schwartz 2019): underweight, normal, pre-obese, obese, still using BMI as before.
  - Notes on WHO classification: 1. obese is divided into class 1, 2, 3 to choose treatment; 2. additional cut-off points (right-hand column) are the classification of obesity for Asians, who have smaller numbers than Europeans or Americans.

- (WHO 2004.) The American society of bariatric surgeons sets its own class using race, age, education, smoking and more; no need to know in detail.
- BMI = weight in kg / height in meters squared (BW (Kg) / Height (m)^2).
- BMI over 30 carries a 2,000 times higher chance of death from metabolic syndrome [as printed].
- Cancers found more often in obese people: uterus, breast, colon, prostate.
- Treatment of obesity can be divided into 2 big groups: non-operative and operative.
- Non-operative management: weight loss not over 5%; consists of dietary management, exercise, and drugs that reduce fat absorption and appetite, e.g. orlistat, lorcaserin and liraglutide; the drugs can reduce weight 6-10% in 1 year; behavioral therapy usually works well early, but long term does not work well; once the promotion ends, people eat as before and become obese again.
- To lose 1 pound per week, caloric intake must be reduced by 3,500 calories.
- Surgical management is the treatment with the best long-term results.

## Indications for bariatric surgery (American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery, ASMBS 2016)
1. BMI 40 or more, without needing comorbid medical conditions from obesity.
2. BMI over 35 but with comorbidities, e.g. type 2 DM, HT, GERD, high cholesterol, high triglyceride, sleep apnea, joint pain from weight bearing when working or pain disturbing daily life, non alcoholic fatty liver disease, pseudotumor cerebri, venous stasis, severe urinary incontinence.
3. A long history of obesity and trying to treat by non-surgical methods for more than 6 months without result.
4. The patient is determined to lose weight, has good mental health, can follow the doctor's instructions; those who cannot help themselves or with weak intellect should not be operated on.
5. Age between 16-65 years; no surgery in younger children, for fear of problems with development and growth (nowadays obese children are many, the age is being lowered to 12 years; if it meets the indications, it is done).
6. The cause of obesity must not be hereditary or endocrine disease such as Cushing that needs treatment of the cause first, after which the obesity will improve.
- Note: for type 2 DM, the American diabetes society's DM surgery 2016 issued a guideline advising consideration of surgery in type 2 DM patients with BMI 30-34.9 (class 1 obesity).
- Following Schwartz, DM + obesity is a topic emphasised a lot in edition 11, which recommends treating diabetic patients by the guideline from the 2nd Diabetes Surgery Summit 2016. Figure (algorithm), as steps:
  1. Patients with type 2 diabetes: nonobese (BMI under 30 kg/m2, or under 27.5 for Asians): nonsurgical treatment.
  2. Obese (BMI 30 or more, or 27.5 or more for Asians):
     - Class III obese (BMI 40 or more, or 37.5 or more for Asians): expedited assessment for metabolic surgery; recommend metabolic surgery.
     - Class II obese (BMI 35.0-39.9, or 32.5-37.4 for Asians) and Class I obese (BMI 30.0-34.9, or 27.5-32.4 for Asians): optimal lifestyle and medical Rx (including injectable meds and insulin). With poor glycemic control: class II: recommend metabolic surgery; class I: consider metabolic surgery. With adequate glycemic control: class II: consider metabolic surgery; class I: nonsurgical treatment.
- In Asia, type 2 DM is common and has severe comorbid disease more than in the West; so the specification of patients who will be operated on has been reduced, using BMI 37.5, or BMI 32.5 + 1 comorbid disease, or BMI over 30 + 2 comorbid diseases as indications for surgery; so in Asians the BMI used is lower than in Western reviews; the latest review lowered the spec again: BMI 35 or more should be operated.
- Besides that, the indication is widened further: BMI of 30 or more + DM type 2 + another comorbidity should be operated too.
- Absolute contraindications to bariatric surgery:
  1. Cannot tolerate anesthesia, cannot do pneumoperitoneum (ASA class 4).
  2. Hereditary diseases.
  3. Endocrine diseases such as Cushing, hypothyroid: treat the underlying disease until better, the patient will slim down by themselves.
  4. End stage cancer (will slim down soon anyway).
  5. Psychiatric disease, drug addiction, alcoholism; (except addiction to LINE, addiction to women, which is not prohibited).
- Men who lose weight by surgery have more risk than women, because their fat is mostly visceral and they need a longer operation.
- Success: weight loss from excess weight of 50% or more.
- Key to success: the cooperation of the patient, more than the procedure done.
- Before surgery the patient must know the rules after surgery:
  1. Must eat solid food as the main, so the pouch is stretched, and the patient gets full quickly and has to stop eating.
  2. Avoid high energy liquid diet such as hot chocolate.
  3. When full, do not drink water immediately, because the water pushes solid food out of the pouch quickly, and when the pouch is empty the patient feels hungry again.
  4. Exercise 45 minutes a day, about 5 days/week, to prevent muscle loss from fast weight reduction.
  5. Must follow up continually, trust the doctor, no excuses.

## Preoperative evaluation
- Besides usual checks: look at kidney, heart, lungs, coagulogram, breathing exercise, stop smoking, beware sleep apnea, eat a low carbohydrate diet so the liver shrinks a bit; also add:
  1. Upper endoscopy: see whether there is GERD or Barrett, peptic ulcer, or H. pylori; if present, treat first, otherwise after the operation a pouch ulcer arises.
  2. US upper abdomen: see whether the fatty liver is large, liver hard, has a mass or not, and the gallbladder has stones; if gallstone, do cholecystectomy together with the bariatric surgery.
  3. Endocrine evaluation: check A1c, TSH.
  4. Psychological-behavioral evaluation.
  5. Diet restriction: lose weight 10% before surgery so the liver shrinks, which helps the surgeon operate comfortably.
- In cases without gallstone before or after surgery, Schwartz 9th p.955 advises taking ursodiol 300 mg bid to prevent gallstone formation.
- In patients with recent VTE on warfarin, stop the drug 5 days before surgery, then change to LMWH injection instead, without needing an IVC filter before bariatric surgery, because studies reported that placing a filter does not reduce PE risk and also raises morbidity.
- Schwartz 2019 advises preparing for surgery with the preoperative checklist for bariatric surgery (p.1179), taken from the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE) 2013 consensus. Figure (table: AACE Bariatric Surgery Preoperative Checklist):

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Complete history and physical examination | obesity-related comorbidities, causes of obesity, weight, BMI, weight loss history, commitment, exclusions related to surgical risk |
| Routine labs | FPG and lipid panel, kidney function, liver profile, lipid profile, urine analysis, prothrombin time/INR, blood type, CBC |
| Nutrient screening | iron studies, B12 and folic acid (RBC folate, homocysteine, methylmalonic acid optional), 25-vitamin D (vitamins A and E optional); consider more extensive testing in patients undergoing malabsorptive procedures based on symptoms and risks |
| Cardiopulmonary evaluation with sleep apnea screening | ECG, CXR, echocardiography if cardiac disease or pulmonary hypertension suspected; DVT evaluation if clinically indicated |
| GI evaluation | H pylori screening in high-prevalence areas; gallbladder evaluation and upper endoscopy if clinically indicated |
| Endocrine evaluation | A1C with suspected or diagnosed prediabetes or diabetes; TSH with symptoms or increased risk of thyroid disease; androgens with PCOS suspicion; screening for Cushing's syndrome if clinically suspected |
| Clinical nutrition | evaluated by registered dietitian |

## Types of Bariatric operations (by mechanism of action)
1. Restrictive: surgery to shrink the stomach's surface area so the patient feels full quickly: 1.1 Lap. adjustable gastric banding (LAGB); 1.2 Lap. sleeve gastrectomy (LSG); 1.3 Vertical banded gastroplasty (VBG).
2. Malabsorptive: surgery to make less absorption of food: jejunoileal bypass.
3. Combined 1 + 2: 3.1 Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB); 3.2 BPD +/- duodenal switch (DS).
- Jejunoileal bypass (JB): abandoned now: the patient eats and passes it out through the anus at once, loses water, loses salt, weight falls fast until thin, loss of appetite, liver failure, early death; few cases left; if met in the exam, mark it wrong. Figure: jejunoileal bypass procedure (skip).
- Vertical banded gastroplasty (VBG): soon to be abandoned. Figure: VBG (skip).
- Currently the most popular is LSG, especially in the USA, next RYGB; LAGB seems popular around Europe and Australia.
- BPD is hard to do and has many complications; currently done under 1%; it suits the super obese (BMI 50 or more).
- The operative route should be lap rather than open, because wound complications in lap are much fewer than open; robot is used more now, under RCT: whether the figure will be better than lap.
- Most common complication of bariatric surgery: respiratory complications such as atelectasis and pulmonary emboli; so before surgery must practice breathing exercise and prevent VTE with mechanical compression together with LMWH injection.
- Currently the number 1 cause of death after surgery is PE.

### Laparoscopic Adjustable Gastric Banding (LAGB)
- The simplest: uses a balloon silicone band placed at the proximal stomach 1 cm below the EGJ, leaving a pouch volume of about 15 cc; then use a tube to connect the balloon with a reservoir buried at the abdominal wall, so that fluid can be put through the reservoir into the balloon; add 0.5-1 cc each time to adjust the band as tight as desired; tighten enough to make the patient full quickly; beware of overtightening: the patient will vomit, cannot swallow, and drools saliva (let drain out). Figure: band with subcutaneous port, Huber needle injection port (skip).
- In the first 2 years follow up every 4-6 weeks; weight loss every 2 weeks; if weight drops about 2 pounds/week and not hungry, there is no need to adjust the band; patients who cooperate well can be seen at longer intervals after 2 years.
- If weight does not fall, adjust the band by injecting more fluid to tighten it.
- Advantages: cheap, a single band, no bowel anastomosis, simple technique, quick to do, so suits high risk obese patients; this method has a very low mortality rate, about 0.05%, while RYGB has a mortality rate of 1%.
- Disadvantages:
  1. Not suitable for GERD, since the esophagus clears acid poorly; GERD cases should use RYGB.
  2. BMI over 50.
  3. Previous upper gastric surgery, e.g. previous Nissen fundoplication.
  4. Poor mobility.
  5. Lack of ability to exercise.
  6. Large hiatal hernia.
- Complications:
  1. Gastric prolapse: found in 4.7%, both the most common and most serious complication, because part of the stomach wall slips between the band and the band squeezes it, so the blood supply is cut, that part necroses and perforates; suspect when the patient has sudden symptoms of difficulty swallowing, or vomiting; on diagnosis, urgent re-operation, by lap first; if not possible open and take out the strangled stomach part with the band. Prevention: at LAGB, suture the stomach to wrap part of the band, called gastrogastric imbrication (figure, skip).
  2. Band erosion or slip: weight loss fails; treatment is to take it out, redo, or change to another method.

### Laparoscopic Sleeve Gastrectomy (LSG)
- Sleeve means "sleeve of a garment"; LSG is cutting the stomach into a tube-like structure; currently this is the gold standard in America; use lap, with endo GIA along the greater curvature starting from the angle of His to the antrum 4-6 cm above the pylorus; to keep antral peristalsis so there is no gastric outlet obstruction.
- The operation reduces the stomach to a gastric pouch along the lesser curvature of about 50 cc; since there is no anastomosis, it is easy, quick, with few operative complications. Figure: new stomach pouch and portion removed (skip).
- In the past LSG was used as the first stage or bridging procedure of lap. duodenal switch (DS) BPD, to lose weight before DS so there are fewer complications after surgery; but luckily for those who wait for the second stage, the patient's weight dropped well, excess weight loss over 50% in 1 year, so DSBPD was not needed.
- Currently LSG is a stand-alone bariatric procedure in the restrictive group, better at reducing weight than LAGB; compared with LRYGB, the weight loss in the first 3 years appears the same as RYGB; LSG has resolution of comorbidities 45-90% and mortality only 0.3%.
- LSG's greatest risk complication is gastric leak at the staple line, found in 0.9%; so check the staple line several times during the operation; the less common complication is stricture, treated with balloon dilation.
- Indications: 1. BMI over 60; 2. very large liver; 3. patients not suitable for long operations.

### Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass (RYGB)
- A surgical method that lowers weight well; still used, being both restrictive and malabsorptive; uses lap, drill in, staple and close proximal stomach to leave a volume of about 15-20 cc: this is the restrictive part, which gives a little; then make a Roux-en-Y limb 75 cm long to join the proximal stomach; if more weight loss is wanted, make the Roux limb 150 cm long, which lets less food be absorbed. Figure: RYGB (skip).
- Age that should have RYGB: 16-65 years; under 16 would lose bone density, affecting the child's growth; over 65 is old enough.
- RYGB suits those with GERD too, because acid cannot reflux up, while acid from the proximal stomach area can drain through the Roux limb.
- 5-year weight reduction of 60%; loses to BPD, which loses more; but the advantage over BPD is less malnutrition and less operative mortality than BPD.
- Relative contraindications to RYGB: 1. previous gastric or antireflux surgery; 2. severe iron deficiency anemia; 3. Barrett esophagus with severe dysplasia; 4. future surveillance of stomach or duodenum.
- Most common post-op death: death from PE; the patient has respiratory distress, hypoxia, fast HR, leg swelling like DVT, Homan's sign positive, not much fever, only panting; if suspected, do a duplex.
- Most serious complication in early post-op: leak, usually found after surgery day 2; suspect when at rest the patient develops tachycardia, followed by agitation, not from MI or pulmonary embolus; these obese patients have thick fat and pressing the abdomen shows no guarding; so suspect it always: why is the heart rate fast while resting? followed by fever then sepsis, whereas pulmonary emboli have panting, hypoxia, leg swelling but tachycardia can be found in 60%.
  - When leakage is suspected, if possible have the patient swallow gastrografin and x-ray to look; but accept it is hard, since the patient is very obese, squeezing into the CT is hard, and the chance of positive is only 22%, so some people advise that if within 24-28 hr [as printed] after surgery the patient has fever, little urine, WBC up, tachycardia, then re-laparoscopy or open without hesitation; if a small leak hole is found, a figure-of-8 stitch is enough, but if diagnosis is delayed with much tissue inflammation, give drain + TPN. Figure: contrast study showing leak (skip).
- To prevent leakage, it is advised to inject air via the NG tube into the pouch during the operation; if air bubbles are seen, reinforce with stitches.
- Newer options for closing the leak without opening, or by lap: endoscopic treatment, e.g. stent, clip, glue injection.
- A few patients with a fever who are hemodynamically stable and have no peritonitis can be treated conservatively with NPO, IV antibiotics, TPN and percutaneous drainage.
- Most common complication within 30 days: anastomotic stricture at the jejunojejunostomy, which is a technical error; treatment is dilation; but after more than 30 days SBO can be found, though not from adhesion, because with laparoscopic surgery adhesion is less likely; SBO after LRYGB comes back from incisional hernia more (2-5%).
- Internal hernia: the most common cause of SBO after RYGB; found in lap more often than open; occurs when the patient loses weight, the intraperitoneal fat shrinks, and the spaces all widen; especially the space between the Roux limb and the mesocolon, called Peterson defect, the jejunojejunostomy mesenteric defect, and if the Roux limb is placed up to join the stomach in a retrocolic fashion, bowel can slip in as well. Figure (post RYGB hernia types): mesocolic space (through an iatrogenic opening in the mesocolon); Peterson hernia (behind the Roux/alimentary limb); distal mesenteric space (between the 2 leaves of mesentery at the distal anastomosis).
  - So if the patient has abdominal pain that cannot be explained, investigate with endoscopy and laparoscopy, because CT can diagnose SBO from internal hernia in only 48%; and once diagnosed as a true internal hernia, operate quickly (laparoscopic re-exploration and closure of the mesenteric defect) before the bowel necroses.
- Marginal ulcer is a late complication.
- Nutritional sequelae after surgery:
  1. Lipid absorption: fat does not pass the duodenum to mix with bile first to form micelles, so absorption decreases; undigested fat passes to the colon, causing fat malabsorption and steatorrhea.
  2. Intolerance of protein-rich food such as meat or milk after surgery; patients should get protein 60-120 g/day.
  3. Deficiency of iron, B12, calcium, vitamin D, folate (vitamin B9), thiamine (vitamin B1): supplements are necessary after surgery always: iron deficiency microcytic anemia is common; give ferrous sulphate (320 mg) twice a day; B12 deficiency is found late in the first years since the body has a large store in the liver; calcium: monitor bone density, serum calcium, vitamin D, PTH.

### Biliopancreatic Diversion (BPD)
- In the past suited to the super obese with BMI 60 or more; BPD is harder than RYGB; BPD is the operation that reduces weight the most, but BPD also has the most nutritional morbidity and operative mortality; BPD has mean excess weight loss more than 70%.
- Compared with operative mortality: LRYGB has an average risk of 0.1-0.5% while BPD has about 0.5-1.1%; as a hard operation, now done under 1%; for BMI over 50 most do only LSG.
- If BPD is done alone, food does not pass the duodenum, causing postgastrectomy problems, including problems of iron and calcium absorption, which occurs at the duodenum and proximal small bowel, decreasing. Figure: BPD diagrams (250 cm, 50 cm limbs); BPD with duodenal switch: resected stomach, gastric sleeve, cholecystectomy, pylorus, duodenoileostomy, 150-cm alimentary limb, 100-cm common channel, biliopancreatic limb, appendectomy (skip).
- Most common complication of BPD: anemia.
- BPD with Switch: food passes the small stomach through the duodenum as usual, then goes down to the jejunum; the aim is to reduce the problem of iron and calcium absorption.

## Vitamin & Mineral Deficiency
- B1 or thiamine deficiency (Wernicke's encephalopathy or beriberi): 2 forms: cardiac and neurological beriberi.
  - Symptoms: 1 month after RYGB, intermittent emesis, nausea all the time up to 6 months; both legs numb and walking with difficulty (unstable gait); may have nystagmus from ocular motor dysfunction.
  - Investigation: CT or upper GI swallow finds no anatomical problem that would cause the vomiting.
  - Treatment: IV thiamine 100 mg three times a day, then give 50-100 mg orally morning and evening; if not treated there will be progressive weakness of both legs.
- Iron deficiency: from iron absorbed at the duodenum and proximal jejunum; this is the most common nutrient deficiency (14-16%).
  - Symptoms: microcytic anemia, brittle nails, headache; treatment is FeSO4.
- B12 deficiency: found in 12%.
  - Symptoms found late; may take months or a year after surgery, because the body uses B12 2 micrograms per day while our body stores up to 2,000 micrograms; B12 deficiency symptoms: anemia, glossitis, numbness and tingling of hands and feet, mental change; so check B12 level every 6 months in the first year, then once a year.
- Folate: absorbed mostly at the proximal small bowel, especially at the duodenum; symptoms: anemia from folate deficiency, found less than B12.
- Note: anemia from B12 or folate deficiency is megaloblastic anemia, not micro.
- Calcium: absorbed most at the duodenum, so hypocalcemia occurs; beware, not hypercalcemia.
- Other possible deficiencies: magnesium and fat soluble vitamins, especially vitamin A, giving delayed wound healing and night blindness.

## Plastic Surgery after Weight Loss
- After surgery, weight falls, the skin that was stretched, bulging with fat, becomes loose, hanging, tired, small breasts, wrinkled skin, looks unpleasant; reconstruction by plastic surgeons is regarded as part of caring for patients who have had bariatric surgery.
- The suitable time for reconstructive surgery is about 1-2 years after bariatric surgery, waiting for the patient's weight to be stable first; how the plastic surgeons pull and where is not our business.

## Investigational Bariatric Procedures
- New weight loss methods are under study (investigational procedures):
1. Endoscopic procedures:
   - 1.1 Intragastric balloon placement: fill with NSS about 200 cc; used little now, since weight reduction is only 6-10% and there are still problems of balloon migration and bowel obstruction. Figure: weight-loss balloon (skip).
   - 1.2 Endoscopic stapled or sutured gastric pouch, called transoral gastroplasty (TOGA): use EGD then insert a stapling device to staple the stomach from within. Figure: TOGA steps (skip).
2. Duodenojejunal bypass: cut the distal stomach and bring up a Roux-en-Y jejunum to join; the hope is that this bypass reduces absorption of starch and fat; people with a taste for it do it easily; suits treating DM type 2; but from patients' follow-up it was found that reducing weight and lowering sugar is not better than lifestyle modification. Figure: duodenojejunal bypass (skip).
3. Ileal transposition: a procedure used to treat DM type 2 by cutting a short segment of ileum and interposing it at the jejunum; believed to cause secretion of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), which reduces appetite, muscles use more sugar, so blood sugar falls. Figure: ileal transposition and mechanism: transposed ileum -> lower gut stimulation (GLP-1, PYY, bile acids) -> decreased food intake and body weight; decreased blood glucose; increased muscle, adipose and liver glucose metabolism -> diabetes improvements.
- Wait for the results of the new procedures to be accepted as standard in surgical textbooks before writing about them.
