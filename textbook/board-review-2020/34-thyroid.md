# THYROID 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 758-806 (PDF pages 1855-1903)

## Introduction
- Little new in thyroid over the past 3 years; data cited here come mainly from ATA 2015, as in every textbook.
- The ATA 2019 meeting (Chicago, late 2019) will surely bring new data; the author will wait for the official summary of strong recommendations / high-quality evidence and update in the next edition rather than rush it out to students.
- **Thyroid**: from "thyrose" = shield; lies at the front of the neck, H-shaped.
- **Emil Theodore Kocher**: Swiss general surgeon (1841-1917), > 9,000 thyroid operations; explained thyroid pathophysiology and operative technique until widely accepted; first surgeon to win the Nobel prize (1909). Besides thyroid surgery he operated head to toe, so his name is on many instruments (clamp, retractor, bladder spatula) and techniques (thyroid incision, RUQ incision, and mobilizing the duodenal C-loop = kocherization).
- His close friends included William Stewart Halsted and Harvey Cushing, who often visited Berne to watch him operate.
- Kocher had chronic kidney disease; on the morning of 23 July 1917 (as printed; the caption gives 27.07.1917), while preparing to do a gastrectomy for gastric cancer, he collapsed and died — working as a surgeon to his last day.

## Anatomy
- Know it well enough for an idea (รู้พอเป็น idea).
- 2 lobes joined by the isthmus, lying on tracheal rings 2-4; there may be an upward midline extension = pyramidal lobe. Lies in the pretracheal fascia, covered by the strap muscles.
- Size (length × width × thickness) = 5 × 3 × 2 cm; weight about 20 gm.
- **Strap muscles**: sternohyoid, sternothyroid and superior belly of omohyoid; supplied by ansa hypoglossi (C1-3), entering from below. If strap muscles must be divided (especially for a giant thyroid), cut at the upper 1/3 to preserve ansa hypoglossi.
- Thyroid is wrapped by deep cervical fascia with a thin capsule.
- **Suspensory ligament of Berry**: posterior thickening of the thyroid fascia into a ligament that fixes the gland to the cricoid cartilage and tracheal rings. Importance: here the recurrent laryngeal nerve lies closest to the gland, so RLN injury is common at this point.
- **Tubercle of Zuckerkandl**: thyroid tissue extending laterally and posteriorly; the RLN lies close to it — beware injury during thyroidectomy.

### Blood supply
1. **Superior thyroid artery**: 1st branch of the external carotid artery; enters the upper pole, closely related to the superior laryngeal nerve (SLN). Supplies the thyroid only, not the parathyroid.
2. **Inferior thyroid artery**: from the thyrocervical trunk of the subclavian A.; supplies both thyroid and parathyroid — when ligating, beware parathyroid injury and RLN injury.
3. **Thyroidea ima**: found in 1%; branch from the aorta or innominate A.; enters the thyroid in the middle.

### Venous drainage
- 3 branches; unusually, the veins do not follow the arteries, but most drain into the internal jugular vein.
  1. Superior and middle thyroid veins → internal jugular vein
  2. Inferior thyroid vein → brachiocephalic vein

### Recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN)
- Branch of the vagus nerve; variation up to 25% (not small). Landmark during surgery: the inferior thyroid A. in the tracheoesophageal groove. The distal RLN runs close to Berry's ligament into the larynx; it controls the intrinsic muscles of the larynx except cricothyroid.
- **Rt. RLN**: 50-60% lie posterior to the branches of the inferior thyroid A.; below, it loops around the right subclavian A.; 0.5% on the right do not loop down around the A.
- **Lt. RLN**: 50-55% lie posterior to the inferior thyroid A.; like the right, but loops around the aortic arch at the ligamentum arteriosum.
- **Non-recurrent**: found only on the right — does not loop around the right subclavian but runs straight into the larynx at the inferior horn of the thyroid cartilage. Fortunately < 1%. A non-recurrent nerve on the left means the patient must have situs inversus.

### Superior laryngeal nerve (SLN)
- Also called the Amelita Galla Curci nerve; branch of the vagus running down along the internal carotid artery, close to the superior thyroid artery.
- Figure: SLN related to superior thyroid artery — three types drawn (Type 1, Type 2a, Type 2b), with a 1 cm mark above the upper pole.
1. **Internal branch**: about 2 cm above the upper pole; sensory to the supraglottic larynx. Lies high, rarely cut; if cut → numb throat, easy choking/aspiration.
2. **External branch**: runs with the superior thyroid artery; motor branch to cricothyroid, which tenses the vocal cord to make high-pitched voice.

## Physiology
### Basic Steps in Hormone Synthesis
- Should be remembered and understood — useful in treating hyperthyroidism.
- **Step 1 — diet iodide uptake & trapping**: oral iodine is converted to iodide in the stomach, absorbed in the jejunum, enters the circulation and is stored in thyroid follicles (90% of body iodine is in the thyroid); hormone synthesis then begins.
- **Step 2 — oxidation & TG binding**: iodide is oxidized and bound to TG (thyroglobulin) forming MIT and DIT; uses thyroid peroxidase (TPO) enzyme.
- **Step 3 — coupling** (combining to form the hormone):
  - MIT + DIT → T3
  - DIT + DIT → T4
- **Step 4 — secretion** (the "use" step): after coupling, T3 and T4 remain bound to TG; when needed TG is hydrolysed, releasing free T3 and free T4, which are active.
- T3 is short-acting, half life 1-3 days; T4 half life 7 days.
- Secretion is controlled by the hypothalamic pituitary thyroid axis (HPTA): hypothalamus TRH → pituitary TSH → thyroid T3 and T4; T3/T4 give negative feedback (long loop inhibition to hypothalamus, short loop inhibition to pituitary).
- **T3**: 80% made outside the thyroid by deiodination of T4 in liver, muscle, kidney and pituitary; only 20% made in the thyroid. T3 is about 4 times as potent as T4.
- **T4**: made only in the thyroid.
- **TSH**: from the anterior pituitary; controls thyroid hormone level.

## Investigation
- Written for reading, as appropriate.
- **TSH**: single most sensitive and specific test for diagnosing hyper- and hypothyroidism.
- **Total T4 and Total T3**: total = free + bound hormone; total T4 reflects thyroid gland function, total T3 reflects peripheral thyroid hormone metabolism.
- **Free T4 and Free T3**: measure the active thyroid hormone.
- Figure: TSH algorithm, as steps —
  1. Measure TSH.
  2. TSH normal → euthyroid.
  3. TSH low → hyperthyroid? → measure FT3 or T3 → high → hyperthyroid confirmed.
  4. TSH high → hypothyroid? → measure FT4 or T4 → low → hypothyroid confirmed.
- **Thyroid antibody**: diagnoses autoimmune disease — Hashimoto and Graves' disease.
- **Thyroglobulin (TG)**: made only by thyroid tissue; used to detect recurrence in thyroid cancer after total thyroidectomy plus RAI ablation. Should be -ve after surgery; becoming +ve again means cancer recurrence; sensitivity about 90%.
- **Calcitonin**: made and secreted by thyroid C cells; lowers calcium to keep it balanced. Serum calcitonin is the tumor marker for diagnosing medullary thyroid cancer.
- **Radionuclide imaging**: oral I 123 or I 131, or injected Tc 99 m pertechnetate, then scan — cold or hot nodule; also used to look for metastasis in thyroid cancer and where it is.
- **US** — useful for:
  1. Cystic vs solid nodule
  2. Size, multicentricity
  3. Guided FNA
  4. Assess cervical lymphadenopathy
  5. Follow up
- **CT or MRI**: not needed in every case; use for a large fixed mass suspected of invading nearby organs, substernal goiter, and compressive symptoms.
- **FNA**: diagnostic procedure of choice for a solitary nodule.
  1. Very reliable for PTC and colloid goiter
  2. FTC, HTC, MTC, ATC are hard to diagnose
  3. False positive very rare

## Embryology
- From primitive foregut (endoderm), 1st and 2nd pharyngeal pouches, in the third week of gestation; develops into follicular cells that make thyroid hormone.
- Starts at the base of tongue at the foramen cecum; in weeks 7-8 the thyroid gradually descends to lie in front of the trachea, and the tract between foramen cecum and thyroid closes in week 5 after the hyoid bone forms (as printed).
- **Developmental abnormalities**: residents must know the following — thyroglossal duct cyst, ectopic thyroid, especially lingual thyroid.

## Thyroglossal Duct Cyst (TDC)
- Arises from the thyroglossal duct, which starts at the foramen cecum at the tongue base and should close; when it fails to close it forms a cyst.
- Can be found anywhere along the migratory pathway; usually midline but may be lateral (slightly off to one side) in about 25%; never found at the SCM.
- **Position**: 80% juxtaposed to the hyoid bone, between the jaw and the 2nd tracheal ring, close to the hyoid.
- Usually asymptomatic; often presents around age 20; functioning thyroid tissue found in 1%.
- **Diagnosis**: smooth, well-defined midline neck mass that moves up when the tongue is protruded.
- Thyroid imaging is not needed for diagnosis, but neck US should be sent to confirm the diagnosis and to check whether there is thyroid tissue elsewhere (ectopic thyroid).
- **Differential diagnosis**
  1. **Branchial cleft cyst** — presents in childhood or adulthood; under the upper 1/3 of the SCM
  2. **Carotid body tumor** — slightly lower than a branchial cleft cyst, at the level of the thyroid cartilage
  3. **Lymph node** enlargement
  4. **Cystic hygroma** — mostly from birth or by age 2-3, but some are diagnosed in adolescence because a very small mass is missed until infection enlarges it; usually at the base of the posterior triangle
  5. **Pyramidal lobe** — found in 50% of normal people; thought to be the distal end of the thyroglossal duct joined to the thyroid; pyramid-shaped projection up from the isthmus; midline, left or right
  6. **Dermoid cyst** — from skin sequestration along lines of embryonic closure; round, soft mass fixed to deep tissue or bone; besides the neck, common at the eyelid, scalp, nose tip
  7. **Lipoma, epidermal cyst**
- **Treatment**: once TDC is diagnosed, operate, because of infection risk from saliva draining into the cyst; some TDCs become obvious during a URI. Also association with papillary thyroid cancer (PTC).
- **Sistrunk operation under GA**
  1. En bloc cystectomy
  2. Excision of central hyoid bone including tissue above the hyoid bone to the base of tongue
  3. No need to remove the isthmus or pyramidal lobe
  4. No need for preop antibiotic
- Cancer found in 1%, mostly PTC; rare ones such as FTC, HTC; never MTC (definitely).
- If TDC pathology shows PTC: observe + follow up vs total thyroidectomy is still debated.
- Schwartz 2019 p.1626 recommends total thyroidectomy (as printed context) when:
  1. Large gland (size not stated)
  2. Coexisting thyroid nodule
  3. Evidence of cyst wall invasion
  4. Evidence of lymph node metastasis
  5. (Author's addition) aggressive cell type, e.g. tall cell
- Infected cyst: antibiotic plus aspiration; avoid open drainage — it seeds cells outside the cyst and raises recurrence risk.

## Lingual Thyroid
- Most common form of functional ectopic thyroid; incidence 1 : 100,000 live births.
- Failure to descend to the normal position; most often at the tongue base, often found by chance when examining a child's mouth.
- Very, very low risk of malignancy (< 2%).
- Most lingual thyroids are hypothyroid; hyperthyroidism is extremely rare.
- 70% of lingual thyroid cases are the only functioning thyroid — no thyroid gland in the neck.
- **Remember** (จำไว้): before removing a mass from the base of the tongue, do a thyroid scan first.
- **Treatment**
  1. No obstructive symptoms → leave it, observe.
  2. To shrink it: try thyroid hormone suppression (levothyroxine) or RAI first — so diagnosis does not mean surgery for every case.
  3. **Indications for surgery** for lingual thyroid:
     - 3.1 Obstructive symptoms, e.g. unclear speech, dyspnea, dysphagia
     - 3.2 Hemorrhage
     - 3.3 Uncontrolled hyperthyroidism (very rare)
     - 3.4 Suspicious of malignancy
     - 3.5 Degeneration and necrosis
- Operation: excision via intraoral route or lateral pharyngotomy route.

## Thyroiditis
- Inflammation of the thyroid; women > men. Causes: bacterial or viral infection, autoimmune, RT, or radioactive iodine treatment.
- Early in the inflammation, hormone may be released in large amounts, giving hyperthyroid symptoms.
- A patient presenting with a painful thyroid → think of:
  1. Thyroiditis
  2. Anaplastic thyroid cancer
  3. Thyroid lymphoma
  4. Bleeding colloid goiter
- **Classification**
  1. Acute thyroiditis
  2. Subacute thyroiditis (De Quervian, as printed)
  3. Chronic thyroiditis
     - 3.1 Chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis (Hashimoto)
     - 3.2 Chronic fibrous thyroiditis (Riedel)

## Acute Thyroiditis
- Acute inflammation of the thyroid caused by bacteria, usually strep., then staph.; also seen in debilitated patients, HIV, or long-term antibiotic use.
- Other names: acute bacterial thyroiditis, acute suppurative thyroiditis, acute pyogenic thyroiditis.
- Least common thyroiditis.
- Patients are usually young (20 years), often with a piriform sinus fistula; children may have prior URI or otitis.
- Risk factors: pre-existing thyroid disease (e.g. Hashimoto, thyroid cancer), immunodeficiency such as HIV.
- **Symptoms**: fever with chills, sudden onset anterior neck pain radiating to the jaw, dysphagia, odynophagia; neck skin swollen, red, hot and very tender. Lasting > 2 days → beware abscess.
- **Investigation**
  1. CBC: WBC up, N up
  2. TFT: usually normal
  3. US: if abscess found, aspirate pus for examination
  4. CT neck: like US — confirms diagnosis and extent of infection
- **Treatment**
  1. Start with IV antibiotic first — draining straight away is wrong.
  2. If not improving or an abscess forms, then drain; may try US-guided aspiration first.
- Symptoms usually improve within 72 hours.

## Subacute Thyroiditis
- Thyroid inflammation probably from viruses such as mumps, measles, influenza.
- Other names: De Guervain thyroiditis (as printed), granulomatous thyroiditis, giant cell thyroiditis.
- Usually women aged 30-50; F : M = 5 : 1; flu-like illness 2-3 weeks before.
- **Symptoms**: sudden or gradual onset of neck pain — the most common cause of patients presenting with thyroid pain. Often with low-grade fever and fatigue, but less severe than acute; thyroid pain may come and go, and some resolve on their own. 4 phases:
  1. Hyperthyroid phase about 1-3 weeks; in some may last up to 3 months
  2. Euthyroid phase about 1 month
  3. Hypothyroid phase about 1-2 months
  4. Return to euthyroid phase
- **Investigation**
  1. Classic: high ESR; FNA shows giant cells with plasma cell & lymphocyte infiltration and focal granuloma
  2. CBC and TFT usually normal; I131 uptake decreased
- **Treatment**: self-limiting within 2-6 months, so drugs plus symptomatic treatment.
  1. NSAID is the main drug — give until thyroid pain resolves, then 2-3 wk more. In the hyperthyroid phase, PTU is not needed; give beta-blocker to slow the heart rate for about 1-3 weeks. In the hypo phase give eltroxin. Most end euthyroid, needing no further treatment.
  2. Prednisolone in severe cases, with NSAIDs; physicians believe steroid gives faster remission.

## Hashimoto's Thyroiditis
- Autoimmune inflammatory thyroid disease: autoantibody against thyroglobulin → inflammation with many lymphocytes in the thyroid, then slow destruction of the gland.
- Most common thyroiditis. Other names: chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, struma lymphomatosa.
- F : M = 10 : 1, age 30-50 years.
- **Symptoms**: early transient hyperthyroid, then euthyroid, ending hypothyroid.
- Gland enlarges slowly to about 2-5 times normal, rubbery consistency, not very tender.
- Raised risk of PTC and lymphoma — any nodule in Hashimoto needs FNA to rule out malignancy.
- Associated with hyperlipidemia.
- **Diagnosis**: +ve antithyroid peroxidase antibody (anti TPO Ab) — peroxidase links iodine to tyrosine; ESR usually normal.
- **US**: diffuse hypoechogenic thyroid enlargement.
- **TFT**: not very helpful — depends on timing; early it shows hyper, late it shows hypo.
- **Thyroid scan**: usually decreased, patchy uptake.
- **Histology**: done only when there is a nodule; FNA shows lymphocytic infiltration.
- **Treatment**
  1. Most patients come late with a generalized, slightly hypothyroid gland → just thyroid hormone replacement.
  2. **Indications for surgery**
     - 2.1 Pressure symptom
     - 2.2 Suspicious of malignancy

## Riedel's Thyroiditis
- Still unclear how fibrous tissue replaces the thyroid; cannot be called autoimmune because no antibody is found. Other names: chronic fibrous thyroiditis, struma thyroiditis (as printed: strumal thyroiditis).
- Thought to be part of a multiple sclerosing syndrome — fibrosis at many sites, e.g. around the eye, in the abdomen (sclerosing cholangitis), retroperitoneum or mediastinum.
- F : M = 4 : 1, age > 40-50 years.
- **Symptoms**: mass enlarges, rock hard like anaplastic cancer; pressure symptoms and hypothyroidism.
- FNA gets only fibrous tissue; the hard tissue is hard to aspirate, so FNA rarely gives the answer — better choose open biopsy.
- **Treatment**
  1. Thyroid hormone replacement long term (best)
  2. Surgery for pressure symptoms or suspicion of malignancy
  3. Some give prednisolone or tamoxifen as a try (แก้กลุ้ม)

## Goiter
- From Latin "Guttur" = throat.
- Abnormal enlargement of the thyroid, diffuse or nodular, from response to TSH from the pituitary gland.
- **Non-toxic or nodular goiter** — causes:
  1. Iodine deficiency — common in developing/underdeveloped countries such as Thailand; goiter common in a geographic area is called endemic goiter
  2. Inadequate thyroid hormone synthesis
  3. Familial goiter — inherited enzyme defect in iodine metabolism
  4. Goitrogens, e.g. cabbage, PAS, sulfonamide
- **Toxic goiter**: hyperthyroid group — toxic multinodular goiter.

## Non-Toxic Goiter
- Iodine or thyroid hormone deficiency → pituitary releases more TSH → thyroid works harder, enlarges and accumulates colloid in follicles = **colloid goiter**. Over time adenomatous change occurs = **adenomatous goiter**, enlarging lumpy (multiple nodules).
- Risk of thyroid cancer is very low, mostly in goiters enlarged > 5 years.
- **Symptoms**: mostly a thyroid swelling with few symptoms; enlarges slowly; trachea may be deviated, with or without compression.
  - Sudden enlargement → think of bleeding into the goiter.
- **Diagnosis**: patient looks up, inspect the neck — diagnosis can be made from across the room (as printed: "มอเตอร์ไซค์วิน"). TFT usually euthyroid. Film or CT kept for substernal goiter.
- **FNA-B** recommended for:
  1. Dominant nodule
  2. Painful or enlarging nodule
- **Treatment**
  1. Small, euthyroid, no pressure symptoms → usually no treatment needed, but doctors often give thyroid hormone to reduce TSH stimulation. Hormone is best for early goiter (colloid goiter), when the gland can still shrink. For multinodular goiter, hormone suits hypo- or euthyroid; toxic multinodular goiter is better sent for surgery.
  2. **Indications for surgery**
     - 2.1 Obstructive symptom
     - 2.2 Substernal extension
     - 2.3 Continues to increase in size while on thyroid hormone suppression
     - 2.4 FNA malignant or just suspicious → operate
     - 2.5 Cosmetic problem
- **Surgical procedure of choice**: total lobectomy on the side with the dominant nodule, subtotal lobectomy on the other side.
- **Postop**: thyroid hormone to stop the remnant enlarging; in practice lifelong hormone is hard (heart and bone problems), but in theory answer that it should be given.

## Substernal Goiter
- Goiter with > 50% of thyroid tissue extending into the thoracic inlet; secondary and primary types; mostly anterior mediastinum, posterior is uncommon.
- **Secondary**: 99%; a neck goiter grows down into the chest, keeping the neck thyroid's blood supply — mainly the inferior thyroid A., helped by the superior thyroid A.
- **Primary**: very rare, ≤ 1%; from ectopic thyroid with no connection to the neck thyroid; blood supply from the innominate artery.
- **Symptoms**: often subclinical hypothyroid and a mass as the main complaint; as it grows → airway obstruction and dysphagia. When the patient swallows, the substernal pole cannot be palpated.
- **Pemberton sign**: an enlarging substernal goiter compresses venous return at the thoracic inlet; raising the arms above the head causes facial flushing and cervical vein dilatation.
- **Risk of malignancy** in substernal goiter is very low, ≤ 2%; when malignant, most common is papillary thyroid cancer, usually < 1 cm. Most substernal goiters are benign (remember — จำหน่อย); risk is no higher or lower than a neck goiter.
- **Diagnosis**
  1. **CT scan neck and chest or MRI** — best imaging, shows depth and shape of the mass; plain film only shows a shadow in the upper chest.
  2. **FNA** — most do not recommend it: bleeding is hard to control.
- **Differential diagnosis** with anterior mediastinal tumors:
  1. Thymoma
  2. Teratoma
  3. Lymphoma
- **Treatment**
  1. Surgery plus postop thyroid replacement = treatment of choice; for "one best" answer, choose surgery.
  2. Thyroid hormone suppression does not work well, but may be tried with faint hope (หวังฟลุ๊ค) — shrinking even a little is good enough to reduce pressure symptoms.
     - Indications for thyroid hormone alone:
       - 2.1 Poor risk — old, frail, dragged in to the OR barely making it (as printed)
       - 2.2 Asymptomatic, mass not very large
       - 2.3 Looks benign, not like cancer
- Remember: thyroid hormone suppression is rarely the right answer.
- Residents should remember: substernal goiter should NOT get I131 — the gland swells with inflammation and can cause airway obstruction. So once substernal goiter is diagnosed, answering "surgery" is usually correct.
- Intubation at anesthesia may be difficult; fiberoptic bronchoscopy may help in some.
- **Surgical management**
  - Advantages of surgery:
    1. Rapid relief of symptoms or growth complications
    2. 1-3% of patients die of airway obstruction
    3. Tissue for pathology
    4. Avoids complications of drugs or I131
- **Incision**
  1. **Cervical incision** — about 99% of substernal goiters can be removed via a neck incision. At surgery, hook a finger in: if the lower edge of the thyroid can be felt, it will come out easily via the neck.
     - Cases suited to cervical incision:
       - 1.1 Benign lesion
       - 1.2 No hyperthyroid symptoms
       - 1.3 CT shape not dumbbell
  2. **Sternotomy incision** — may split only the manubrium; if the mass goes very deep, full sternotomy.
     - Indications:
       - 2.1 CT: mass extends to the aortic arch or atrium
       - 2.2 CT: dumbbell shape
       - 2.3 Hyperthyroid symptoms
       - 2.4 Suspected malignancy, e.g. SVC obstruction, hoarseness
       - 2.5 Short neck or previous neck surgery
       - 2.6 1ry substernal goiter
       - 2.7 Mass larger than the thoracic inlet
  3. **Thoracotomy** — suits posterior mediastinal goiter.
- **Procedure**: still debated — Hartley-Dunhill subtotal, near total or total thyroidectomy.
  - Total camp: leaving gland risks recurrence, and reoperation is hard; after total, thyroid replacement can be given.
  - Subtotal camp: goiter is benign — do a standard operation and add thyroid hormone as needed; the price of fashion is high (ราคาต่อรอง). The author looks at subtotal as the half-ball (ต่อครึ่งลูก, betting idiom), plus 10-9 (as printed). If only one choice is allowed, the one best answer the author picks is subtotal.

## Hyperthyroidism
- **Thyrotoxicosis**: toxic symptoms from causes outside the thyroid, e.g. trophoblastic disease, struma ovarii, pituitary secreting tumor, Jodbasedow disease, drug induced (amiodarone).
- **Jod-Basedow disease**: a patient with multinodular goiter whose thyroid becomes overactive after iodine stimulation — whether goiter or cough medicine (iodine-containing).
- **Hyperthyroidism**: toxic symptoms from disease of the thyroid itself; besides thyroiditis, GPs should know the main causes:
  1. **Increased thyroid hormone synthesis**
     - 1.1 **Graves' disease** — classic: bulging eyes plus many extrathyroid manifestations; toxic symptoms are clearest and most severe.
     - 1.2 **Toxic multinodular goiter** — middle-aged patient with a long-standing lumpy goiter that suddenly overproduces hormone; less toxic than Graves' and no extrathyroid manifestations.
     - 1.3 **Toxic adenoma** — younger patients; nodule not large like a goiter, mostly ≤ 3 cm, and does not turn malignant.
  2. **Increased hormonal release** — normal synthesis but abnormal release, usually from thyroid inflammation, e.g. acute phase of subacute thyroiditis and Hashimoto's thyroiditis.
- Symptoms result from increased circulating thyroid hormone.

## Graves' Disease
- Most common cause of hyperthyroidism (80%); women 5 times more than men; age about 40-60 years.
- **Cause**: thought autoimmune — thyroid stimulating antibody (TSHR-Ab, an IgG) binds the TSH receptor (TSH-R) and stimulates the thyroid continuously.
- **Triad of Graves'**
  1. Hyperthyroidism
  2. Diffuse toxic goiter
  3. Extrathyroidal manifestations
     - 3.1 Exophthalmos
     - 3.2 Pretibial myxedema — thick skin over the shin and dorsum of the foot from deposits of glycosaminoglycans
     - 3.3 Proximal muscle weakness
     - 3.4 Depression
     - 3.5 Thyroid acropachy (joint pain — ปวดข้อ)
     - 3.6 Gynecomastia
- **Lid lag or von Graefe's sign**: from spasm of the upper eyelid.
- **Dalrymple's sign**: sclera visible above the corneoscleral limbus.
- Joint pain occurs in Graves', but the joints look normal — no arthropathy.
- **Exophthalmos**: even with PTU or thyroidectomy it does not resolve; to reduce it somewhat, physicians give prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg/day; for surgery, eye surgeons do tarsorrhaphy.
- **In children**: early bone maturation → short stature. In women: amenorrhea, infertility; if pregnant, risk of premature labor. In the elderly: cardiovascular complications such as AF, CHF.
- **Diagnosis**: TSHR-Ab in serum (diagnostic). Anti-TPO is also high but not specific (also high in Hashimoto). TFT: TSH is key — TSH low and FT4 high. Thyroid scan only when toxic adenoma is suspected.
- **Marine Leuhart syndrome** (as printed): Graves' with a nodule → FNA, because malignancy is possible; if FNA malignant → total thyroidectomy. Unlike toxic adenoma, where malignancy is extremely rare.
- **Treatment** — aim to bring hormone level to normal. 3 main methods:
  1. Medical treatment
  2. Radioactive iodine
  3. Surgical treatment

### Medical treatment
- First line treatment.
1. **Antithyroid drugs** — 2 commonly used, PTU and Methimazole, as definitive treatment or as bridging to prepare for surgery.
   - **Mechanism**: block binding and coupling of iodine; PTU also blocks peripheral T4 → T3 conversion.
   - **Indications**
     1. Women with small gland (≤ 40 gm)
     2. Mild symptoms, short duration, little toxicity; blood thyroid hormone and antibody levels not very high
     3. Patient can come for follow-up (drug taken for a year or more)
     4. 1st line treatment in pregnancy (PTU)
     5. Patients who should not have surgery — debilitated, short life expectancy, or refusing RAI (as printed: scared of radiation even though already balding — หมอรังสีเค้ายังส่ายหัว)
     6. Preoperative preparation before surgery
   - **PTU** (50 mg/tab), dose 100-300 mg, 3-4 times a day. Preferred in pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta (used in the 1st trimester), and little enters breast milk so it can be used after delivery. In pregnancy use the lowest dose that controls symptoms; patients usually deliver safely.
   - PTU in the 2nd and 3rd trimester: beware adverse drug reaction, especially hepatitis; at this stage usually switch from PTU to methimazole.
   - **Methimazole** (5 mg/tab), dose 10-30 mg/day once daily — easy like a contraceptive pill; now used more than PTU and in practice the first choice. Drawback of tapazole: crosses the placenta readily → teratogenic. Remember: **no Tapazole in the 1st trimester or right after delivery** (causes cretinism in the newborn); also not for thyroid crisis because its effect is slower than PTU.
   - **Adverse drug reactions** → stop the drug or switch to I131 or surgery:
     1. Agranulocytosis — fever, sore throat, rapid fall in WBC; stop the drug; both PTU and tapazole are then forbidden; switch to I131 or surgery.
     2. Severe pruritus, severe vasculitis
     3. Hepatitis — stop if enzymes rise > 2 times normal
   - **Results**: symptoms improve in about 2 weeks; labs become euthyroid by about week 6, then reduce tapazole to 5-10 mg/day; follow up with TFT every 3 months; total course 1-1.5 years — shorter courses rarely cure. After stopping, 50-70% recur, mostly relapsing within 6 months. Patients who fail medical cure tend to be < 40 years, male smokers, large mass, high baseline T3, T4, TSHR-Ab.
2. **Other drugs**
   - 2.1 **Beta-blocker**: does not reduce hormone synthesis, but blocks the target so circulating hormone cannot act; fastest symptom control → suits thyroid storm or crisis.
   - 2.2 **Steroid**: blocks peripheral conversion of T4 to T3.

### Radioactive iodine therapy (RAI)
- **Advantages**: safe, simple, no anesthetic risk.
- **Indications**
  1. Elderly & high risk, e.g. heart disease CHF or AF
  2. Relapse after stopping drugs or after surgery
  3. Contraindication to drugs or surgery
- **Contraindications**
  1. Pregnancy or plan for pregnancy within 6 months
  2. Breast milk feeding, especially within 6 weeks after RAI
  3. Age < 5 years
  4. Graves' ophthalmopathy — eyes swell and bulge more
  5. Thyroid nodule
  6. Patient refuses or wants rapid results
- After I131, wanting children: wait at least 6 months after I131; to be safe, wait 1 year.
- **Results**: slower response than other methods — 3-4 months, some up to 6 months; not good for those wanting rapid results (ปรู๊ดปร๊าด). Response is sure: remission rate 80-98%; some need a repeat dose. Overall results still not as good as surgery.
- **Caution** (ข้อควรระวัง): early after RAI the thyroid becomes inflamed and hormone levels rise → possible thyroid storm. So before RAI, give antithyroid drug for about 2-3 weeks.
- **Complications**
  1. Hypothyroid — depends on RAI dose; 50% by 10 years; if patient and doctor live long enough, hypothyroid 100%.
  2. Risk of cancer, thyroid or other systems — no longer believed.
- **Remember: no RAI in**
  1. Pregnancy or plan for pregnancy within 6 months
  2. Breastfeeding — and the father must not suckle either (as printed joke)
  3. Graves' ophthalmopathy
  4. Substernal goiter

### Surgical treatment
- **Indications**
  1. Children or young adults, especially male
  2. Large gland > 80 gm, or very high thyroid hormone and antibody levels
  3. Patient wants quick cure, cannot tolerate or is tired of drugs, recurrence after stopping drugs, or antibody still high after a full drug course
  4. Drug allergy, cannot take drugs, and a condition forbidding RAI
  5. Graves' with nodule and FNA suspicious of malignancy (usually PTC)
  6. Pregnancy — usually start with PTU first; if problems or disease cannot be controlled → operate in the 2nd trimester
  7. Planning pregnancy in 6-12 months
  8. Severe Graves' ophthalmopathy: lid retraction > 2 mm, diplopia, corneal exposure
  9. Pressure symptom
  10. Cosmetic problem
- **Advantages**: rapid disease control, fast relief of pressure symptoms; high cure rate if the surgeon does not botch it (ไม่ปู้ยี่ปู้ยำ).
- **Disadvantages**: operative complications and thyroid storm.
- **Preoperative preparation**: key is getting the patient euthyroid — PTU plus beta blocker; some add lugol solution (saturated KI) 3 drops T.I.D. for 10 days, believed to reduce gland vascularity. In hard-to-control cases, plasmapheresis on the day of surgery: draw blood, separate out plasma, return RBC.
- **Extent for surgery**
  1. **Subtotal thyroidectomy** — leave 4-7 gm of gland, using the Hartley Dunhill operation: total lobectomy on 1 side and subtotal lobectomy on the other.
  2. Since 2013 the trend favours **total thyroidectomy**, or at least near-total leaving < 2 gm: low recurrence; hypothyroidism needs only thyroid extract, far easier to treat than recurrent hyperthyroidism; and a surgeon doing ≥ 30 Graves' cases a year has complications no higher than subtotal. Suits males, young patients with large glands, severe ophthalmopathy or pressure symptoms, refusal of RAI, or life threatening reaction to antithyroid drugs.
  - ATA guideline 2015 recommends near total or total; Schwartz 2019 follows the new ATA; the author agrees. **Conclusion** (สรุปว่า): total is best, with lifelong thyroid hormone after surgery.
- **Results**: fastest results; relapse < 10%, so better than drugs — but surgery carries risk, like buying mutual fund shares (as printed).

## Graves' Disease in Pregnancy
- Medical treatment is the main treatment; no RAI; avoid surgery unless drugs are contraindicated.
- Surgical treatment: in the late second trimester; suits pregnant patients who cannot take drugs, e.g. agranulocytosis.

## Graves' Disease in Children
- Medical treatment is first line, but drugs alone relapse in 80% after stopping, so consider ablative treatment = surgery; wait until age > 5 years. RAI should not be used in children.

## Toxic Adenoma
- Autonomous thyroid nodule overproducing hormone, independent of TSH control, so normal thyroid tissue is suppressed into inactive tissue; about 10%.
- Age 30-50; symptoms usually when size > 3 cm, with a palpable prominent nodule.
- Thyroid scan: single hot nodule; no FNA needed (unlike a Graves' nodule).
- **Treatment**
  1. Lobectomy is the main treatment; Schwartz adds isthmectomy (the author follows Schwartz).
  2. Antithyroid drug — to prepare for surgery. RAI may be used for small nodules but is generally unpopular because it also suppresses normal tissue.

## Toxic Multinodular Goiter (Plummer's Disease)
- 30-50%; age > 50, fairly elderly; enlarged gland in iodine-deficient areas, with many lumpy nodules for a long time; one day it becomes autonomous, independent of TSH, each nodule making lots of hormone.
- No exophthalmos; TFT like Graves'; thyroid scan shows heterogeneous patchy uptake.
- Large glands respond poorly to drugs or RAI, so surgery is the best treatment, as for Graves' — near total or total thyroidectomy.

### Summary: choosing treatment (สรุปแนวทางการเลือกวิธีรักษา)

| | Antithyroid drug | RAI | Surgery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age | Any | Older generation (รุ่นแง้มฝาโลง — "lid of the coffin ajar") | Young |
| Size | Small | Small & medium | Medium & large |
| Severity | Mild | Moderate to severe | Same as RAI |
| Duration of illness | Short | Long | No effect |

| Social factor | Regular F.U. | Tired of seeing the doctor | Tired of the doctor, wants to get back to work fast |

- **Notes** (หมายเหตุ)
  1. Hyperthyroidism from thyroiditis responds quickly to antithyroid drugs — euthyroid almost at once; Graves' takes 6 wk to become euthyroid.
  2. Duration: thyroiditis-related hyperthyroidism is short; toxic multinodular goiter takes much longer — usually goiter for > 17 years before turning toxic.

## Thyroid Crisis
- Many precipitating factors: trauma, infection, pregnancy, metabolic stress (e.g. DKA), drugs (e.g. amiodarone, ASA).
- **Symptoms**: fever, palpitations, fast heart rate, dehydration, sweating, nervousness, then seizures, coma.
- **Diagnosis**: TFT — high circulating thyroid hormone, low TSH.
- **Remember** (จำไว้): drug of choice = beta-blocker, the first drug, to reduce peripheral effects of thyroid hormone; to reduce synthesis give high dose PTU via NG tube; to reduce hormone release give iodine.
- Order of drugs per the table:

### Pharmaceutical Management of Hyperthyroidism/Thyroid Storm

| Drug | Characteristics | Order of administration |
|---|---|---|
| Beta-blockers | Decrease sympathetic hyperactivity; partially block peripheral T4 → T3 conversion (propranolol) | 1 |
| Propylthiouracil (PTU) | Blocks hormone synthesis; decreases T4 → T3 conversion; faster onset than methimazole; preferred in pregnancy | 2 |
| Methimazole | Blocks hormone synthesis; teratogenic | 2 |
| Iodide | Inhibits release of stored hormone; give only after synthesis is blocked by PTU or methimazole | 3 or 4 |
| Steroids | Decrease peripheral T4 → T3 conversion; may treat the autoimmune process in Graves' | 3 or 4 |

- Steroid is a supplement, because thyroid crisis raises steroid requirement; it also blocks peripheral T4 → T3.
- Endocrine (medicine) textbooks favour high dose PTU as the drug of choice; as surgeons we follow the surgical texts — beta-blocker is better.
- **Contraindicated drug: ASA** — displaces T3 and T4 from protein, increasing free T3 and free T4 → worse crisis.

### Management of Thyroid Crisis
- Identify and treat the precipitating factor.
- Supportive care: oxygen; IV fluid therapy; sedation (chlorpromazine); venous thromboembolism prophylaxis with heparin; dexamethasone.
- Fever: antipyretics and cooling.
- Heart failure: digoxin and diuretics.
- Atrial fibrillation: IV heparin.
- Beta blockers: oral propranolol 60-80 mg/4 hr (or diltiazem) to bring HR below 100/min; in very sick patients esmolol IV; reserpine for patients refractory to large doses of propranolol.
- Propylthiouracil or methimazole.
- Lugol's solution 4 hr after propylthiouracil.
- Plasmapheresis and charcoal plasma perfusion or exchange transfusion reserved for recalcitrant cases with no response in 24-48 hr.
- Once euthyroid, consider definitive therapy to prevent a second crisis.

## Hypothyroid
- Decreased circulating thyroid hormone.
- **Cretinism**: hypothyroid in the neonate — face/appearance somewhat like Down syndrome, with neurologic impairment, mental retardation and failure to thrive.
- Hypothyroid in children and adolescence: delayed development.
- **Hypothyroidism in adults**: opposite of hyperthyroidism — slow, weak, easily tired, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, bradycardia, cardiomegaly, reduced cardiac output.
- Figure: symptoms of hypothyroidism — tiredness, depression, inability to concentrate, puffy eyes, loss of body hair, dry patchy skin, weight gain, cold intolerance, elevated cholesterol, family history of thyroid disease or diabetes; forgetfulness/slower thinking, moodiness/irritability, thinning hair/hair loss, swelling (goiter), hoarseness/deepening of voice, persistent dry or sore throat, difficulty swallowing, slower heartbeat, menstrual irregularities/heavy period, infertility, constipation, muscle weakness/cramps.
- Classic face: apathetic puffy face, bilateral ptosis, thinned eyebrows (ขนคิ้วร่วง), periorbital edema.
- TFT: TSH very high, T4 and T3 low.
- **Treatment**: thyroid extract T4 replacement, dose 50-200 microgram/day; start low and adjust gradually to the patient.

## Solitary Thyroid Nodule (STN)
- Thyroid disease seen more and more; although mostly benign, a definite diagnosis is needed for correct treatment.
- **Solitary thyroid nodule (STN)**: a single palpable mass in one lobe, separable from normal thyroid on US, with the rest of the gland normal.
- **Dominant thyroid nodule**: also a single palpable mass in one lobe, but the rest of the thyroid contains small nodules too.
- **Thyroid nodule incidentaloma**: nonpalpable nodule found on US or other imaging; malignancy risk same as a palpable nodule.
- **Differential diagnosis** of a thyroid nodule:
  1. Non-toxic nodular goiter
  2. Thyroiditis, infection or abscess
  3. Thyroid cyst
  4. Benign tumor, e.g. thyroid adenoma
  5. Malignant tumors
- For STN questions, especially in oral exams, residents must answer:
  1. Malignant or not — generally STN malignancy is about 10%; in young patients it rises to 15-50%.
  2. Toxic nodule or not.
- **Risk assessment approach** (แนวทางการประเมินความเสี่ยง): history, physical exam, TSH, then neck US, then needle aspiration (FNA) — no different from triple assessment in a breast lump.
- **History**: how long the mass has been there, how fast it grows, pressure symptoms (dysphagia, dyspnea). Focus on malignancy risk: age < 20 or > 60 (bad at both ends), male (bad), rapid growth. Euthyroid and hypothyroid carry more malignancy risk than hyperthyroid; a toxic (hot) thyroid is less likely malignant. Invasive symptoms such as hoarseness; previous irradiation to the neck (e.g. RT for head and neck malignancies); family history of thyroid cancer.
- **Physical exam**: patient sits and swallows to confirm the mass is thyroid; check pulse fast or slow; doctor stands behind and palpates — hard or not, surface, fixed or not, tender or not, palpable neck nodes; the rest practise yourself (as printed: 4th-year student knowledge).
- Every patient with a thyroid nodule should have serum TSH, the single best TFT.
  1. TSH low → suspect toxic adenoma → radionuclide thyroid scan to see if it is truly a hot nodule.
  2. TSH normal or high → neck US.
  3. Besides TSH, good practice is to check serum calcium in every case — in thyroid nodule patients coming for surgery, concomitant primary HPT is found in 3-5%.
- Initial evaluation of a thyroid nodule does not need serum thyroglobulin (Tg).
- A hot nodule has very low malignancy risk, so FNA is not needed. STN in a euthyroid (warm) or hypothyroid (cold nodule) patient → neck US first, then needle.
- **Neck US**: most useful imaging test — size, texture (cystic vs solid), number and location of nodules; neck nodes, especially level 6 which is hard to palpate; calcification; guide for biopsy. New machines detect nodules as small as 2 mm. Generally pay attention to nodules > 1 cm; with > 1 nodule > 1 cm, handle each as if it were single.
- Nodules < 1 cm: look closer only with clinical symptoms or associated lymphadenopathy, then US guided FNA.
  - Advantage: available and cheap; although it cannot separate benign from malignant 100%, do US before FNA.
- **US features of thyroid suspicious for malignancy**
  1. Hypoechoic solid nodule
  2. Irregular border
  3. Taller than wide
  4. Absent colloid halo sign
  5. Microcalcification
  6. Increased intranodular vascularity
  7. Evidence of extrathyroid extension
- **ATA guideline 2015** — 5 sonographic pattern groups:
  1. **High suspicion**: solid nodule with ill-defined margin, microcalcification, taller than wide; malignancy 70-90%; US-FNA for nodules ≥ 1 cm.
  2. **Intermediate suspicion**: hypoechoic nodule only; malignancy 10-20%; US-FNA for ≥ 1 cm.
  3. **Low suspicion**: isoechoic or hyperechoic, or with cystic component; malignancy 5-10%; US-FNA for ≥ 1.5 cm.
  4. **Very low suspicion**: spongiform (like a sponge) or with cystic component; malignancy < 3%; US-FNA for ≥ 2 cm.
  5. **Benign**: pure cyst; malignancy < 1%; ATA 2015: no FNA.

1. Suspected thyroid nodule, TSH normal or elevated (R2C) → thyroid/neck sonography (R6, 21).

## Sonographic Patterns and Risk of Malignancy (ATA 2015)

| Sonographic pattern | US features | Estimated risk of malignancy | FNA size cutoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| High suspicion | Solid hypoechoic nodule or solid hypoechoic component of a partially cystic nodule with ≥ 1 of: irregular margins (infiltrative, microlobulated), microcalcifications, taller than wide shape, rim calcifications with small extrusive soft tissue component, evidence of ETE | > 70% to 90% | Recommend FNA at ≥ 1 cm |
| Intermediate suspicion | Hypoechoic solid nodule with smooth margins, without microcalcifications, ETE, or taller than wide shape | 10% to 20% | Recommend FNA at ≥ 1 cm |
| Low suspicion | Isoechoic or hyperechoic solid nodule, or partially cystic nodule with eccentric solid areas, without microcalcification, irregular margin or ETE, or taller than wide shape | 5%-10% | Recommend FNA at ≥ 1.5 cm |
| Very low suspicion | Spongiform or partially cystic nodules without any features of low, intermediate or high suspicion patterns | < 3% | Consider FNA at ≥ 2 cm; observation without FNA also reasonable |
| Benign | Purely cystic nodules (no solid component) | < 1% | No biopsy |

(The ≥ sign prints as "‡" in the table.)
- Suspicious cervical lymph node seen → NCCN: FNA the node directly; same as the guideline in Schwartz 11e.
- **US features of a neck node suspicious for malignancy**
  1. Cystic change
  2. Hypoechogenicity
  3. Loss of fatty hilum
  4. Calcification
  5. Increased peripheral vascularity
  6. Rounded rather than oval shape
  - FNA nodes ≥ 8 mm with suspicious features.
- **CT with IV contrast or MRI**: no role in initial evaluation; use for very large mass (advanced disease), invasive primary tumor, dysphagia, dyspnea, hoarseness, hemoptysis, clinically apparent multiple or bulky lymph nodes, or substernal extension.
- **Laryngoscopy** (printed "Lryngoscopy"): for hoarseness or previous lobectomy.
- **FNA**: most important investigation.
  - Easy, quick, done at OPD: needle 23-25 with 10 cc syringe into the nodule, aspirate cells for cytology, drop specimen and smear on slide, fix with 95% alcohol. A good slide has follicular cells > 6 groups, each group > 10 cells; fewer → cytologist always reads non-diagnostic.
  - False -ve 3-5%; false +ve < 1%.
  - FNA is reliable if the (expert — ต้องเทพ) reader says:
    1. Benign disease
    2. Malignant
       - 2.1 PTC — Orphan Annie eyes
       - 2.2 MTC — amyloid and IHC +ve calcitonin
       - 2.3 ATC — hypercellularity and necrosis
  - FTC and HTC have no classic cellular change; cytologists mostly read follicular neoplasm or Hurthle cell neoplasm only. Diagnosing FTC vs HTC needs histology showing capsular or vascular invasion, not cytology.
- After FNA we wait anxiously for the cyto report. Several reporting systems exist; the popular one is the Bethesda System.

## Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology
- Cyto results are grouped into 6 categories linked to risk of malignancy; Bethesda is used as the treatment guideline by NCCN, ATA 2015 and Schwartz 2019 alike. Our local professors are delighted with this system — you cannot avoid following it.
1. **Nondiagnostic or unsatisfactory** — problem of the person aspirating: too few cells, poor smear or fixed slide. Risk of malignancy 1-4%.
   - **Recommended Rx**: repeated FNA with US guidance, by someone more skilled. A resident who regularly gets nondiagnostic results should stop with facebook or line chatting and spend the time practising until good (as printed). If repeat FNA is still nondiagnostic, bring the patient back for re-aspiration in 3 months.
2. **Benign** — malignancy risk very low, < 1%; cytology usually reads "consistent with benign follicular nodule", e.g. colloid nodule, or consistent with the thyroiditis group. Surgeons are happier with this result.
   - **Recommended Rx**:
     - 2.1 No further diagnostic study; treat the cause. Colloid nodule without iodine deficiency → follow up without thyroid hormone, because suppressive levothyroxine (LT4) for 6-18 months shrinks the nodule only in 5-15%. If the patient lives in an iodine-deficient endemic area, give iodine supplement 150 microgram/day.
     - 2.2 ATA 2015 adds: cyto benign but US in the high suspicion group → no immediate repeat FNA, but repeat FNA once within 1 year; if the 2nd result is still benign → routine FU.
     - 2.3 Surgery: consider for a growing nodule > 4 cm with compressive symptoms.
3. **Atypia of undetermined significance (AUS) or follicular lesion of undetermined significance (FUS)** — follicular cells look abnormal but not enough; the cytologist is confused (like being bitten by a mosquito — as printed) and cannot conclude benign vs neoplasm. Risk of malignancy 5-15%.
   - **Recommended Rx**: repeated FNA.
4. **Suspicious or indeterminate follicular or Hurthle cell neoplasm** — cannot tell adenoma from carcinoma, because diagnosing follicular or Hurthle cell malignancy needs histopathology showing capsular or vascular invasion. Overall risk of malignancy 15-30%.
   - **Recommended Rx**: resection (lobectomy) — treatment and diagnosis at once. A minority suggest a thyroid scan first for suspicious follicular neoplasm: hot nodule → follow up; cold nodule → operate, which is OK.
   - For one best, the author picks surgery: lobectomy with isthmectomy plus pyramidal lobe, sent for frozen section; if frozen is not sure, close and wait for permanent.
   - In suspicious cases the operation is complete unilateral thyroid lobectomy including isthmectomy and pyramidal lobe; never do subtotal on the other side — if it is cancer and total is needed later, it will be difficult.
   - To feel the other lobe after lobectomy, palpate outside the strap muscle; do not reach in to touch the thyroid — adhesions make reoperation difficult.
   - **Molecular and genetic testing of FNA material**: look for abnormal genes/molecules in FNA cells, for Bethesda "suspicious or indeterminate follicular or Hurthle cell neoplasm" — [unclear] BRAF, PIK3CA, AKTI (as printed), TERT mutation and many more; if likely malignant → total thyroidectomy straight away. But on the data so far, molecular diagnosis does not yet work.
5. **Suspicious for malignancy** — cells look malignant (papillary, medullary, metastasis or lymphoma) but the cytologist is not sure; risk of malignancy 60-75%.
   - **Recommended Rx**: resection — lobectomy first with frozen section in the OR; with high-risk conditions, treating as malignant straight away is not wrong. High-risk conditions:
     1. Age < 20 or > 60 in males
     2. Evidence of metastasis — lung, bone, node
     3. History of neck RT, family history of cancer, or patient tired of repeated operations
     4. Size > 4 cm
     5. Intraoperative findings suggest cancer, e.g. hard mass or invasion of nearby organs
   - Advice: if suspicious but unsure and afraid of a mistake, waiting for permanent section is safer than going straight to total.
6. **Malignancy** — cytologist is sure; risk of malignancy 99-100% (as printed in text). If papillary, medullary or anaplastic (printed "annplastic") is read, treat per that type.
   - **Recommended Rx**: treat by the type of cancer.

## Thyroid Nodule in Pregnancy
- Evaluation as in non-pregnant: history, exam, TSH, neck US.
- Avoid radioisotopes.
- **Current practice guideline** (as printed "Currenr") accepted by all:
  1. Mass > 1 cm → FNA in every case
  2. If surgery is needed, do it in the 2nd trimester
  3. If surgery is needed, PO thyroid suppression can be given straight away
  4. No RAI ablation during pregnancy or breastfeeding; if RAI is needed, give it about 6-12 months after delivery

## Thyroid Cancer
- On current data, treatment of thyroid cancer is still debated, but the trend is more and more aggressive: total thyroidectomy is preferred; near total leaving ~1 gm of thyroid at Berry's ligament (printed "Burry") is acceptable, aiming to reduce injury to RLN and upper parathyroid.
- **Well differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC)**: cancers from thyroid cells themselves; 3 types residents should know with eyes closed — PTC, FTC and HTC.
- **DTC** is split into low risk and high risk by TNM, AGES, AMES, MACIS and other systems; this tells risk of recurrence and metastasis affecting survival, and helps decide total vs lobectomy.
- **AGES criteria**: Age, Grading of the tumor, Extrathyroid invasion, Size of the tumor.

| Low risk | High risk |
|---|---|
| Female age < 50 years | Female age ≥ 50 years |
| Male age < 40 years | Male age ≥ 40 years |
| Well diff. tumor | Poorly diff. tumor, e.g. tall cell |
| Diameter < 4 cm | Diameter > 4 cm |
| Confined to thyroid | Local invasion |
| No distant metastasis | Distant metastasis |

- Remember: smoking is not a risk factor for thyroid cancer.
- **MACIS criteria**: (distant) Metastasis, Age, Completeness of resection, (extrathyroid) Invasion, Size of the tumor.
- Both AGES and MACIS depend on postoperative pathology.
- **AMES criteria**: Age, (distant) Metastasis, Extrathyroid invasion, Size of the tumor — does not need postop pathology, can be assessed before surgery, so suits deciding lobectomy vs total thyroidectomy.
- In well differentiated thyroid cancer < 1 cm (usually found incidentally — ฟลุ๊คๆ), prognosis is very good; lobectomy is enough.
- Histologic types considered high risk: tall cell, insular, columnar cell, solid, trabecular and diffuse sclerosis.

## Papillary Thyroid Cancer (PTC)
- Small details to remember: PTC is the most common thyroid cancer; multicentricity 45%; bilateral 75-80% (as printed). Slow-growing painless mass; likes to spread to lymph nodes, but nodes do not affect prognosis the way local invasion does.
- Distant metastasis is uncommon; when it occurs, lung is first.
- **Risk factors**
  1. Post RT to the neck
  2. Long term iodine deficiency
  3. Thyroglossal duct cyst
  4. Gardner's syndrome
  5. Hashimoto thyroiditis — raises risk of lymphoma and PTC
- Common in women aged 30-40 years.
- **Lateral aberrant thyroid**: old name for thyroid tissue found near the carotid sheath and internal jugular vein; now considered cervical lymph node metastasis from PTC, with the thyroid primary still subclinical and no palpable nodule. FNA of the mass usually reads well differentiated thyroid tissue — but engrave it in your skull that it is PTC.
- **US**: punctate calcification in the mass = psammoma body on histology.
- **Histology**
  1. **Psammoma bodies** — calcified clumps in the tumor showing cell slough
  2. **Orphan Annie nuclei** — empty appearing or ground glass nuclei, chromatin like the eyes of the orphan girl in the cartoon Orphan Annie (residents today may not know it — they only watch Korean series)
- **Treatment** — 3 issues to know:
  1. Total thyroidectomy or lobectomy + isthmectomy
  2. Lymph node dissection
  3. Postop care & FU
- **Total thyroidectomy** — most accepted operation because PTC is often bilateral; near total thyroidectomy is still acceptable.
  - **Indications for total thyroidectomy**
    1. Mass > 4 cm — definitely total. Mass > 1 cc (as printed) but < 4 cm should have at least one high-risk criterion from items 2-7 below.
    2. Mass in the other lobe
    3. Lymph node metastasis
    4. Previous RT to the head & neck area
    5. Age > 45 years
    6. High-risk DTC, e.g. tall cell
    7. Familial DTC
- **Lobectomy + isthmectomy** — now less used; if chosen, PTC should be in the low-risk group:
  1. Mass < 1 cm in one lobe — accepted by all; but ATA 2015 and NCCN 2016 expanded to ≤ 4 cm for lobectomy alone, provided no poor prognostic indicators and no BRAF mutation (author's personal view: wants more data).
  2. No poor indicator, e.g. prior RT
  3. No palpable cervical lymph node
  4. Pathology report: no multifocal area, free margin, no angiolymphatic invasion, no aggressive histology, encapsulated PTC without extrathyroid invasion
- After lobectomy + isthmectomy, if pathology shows +ve resection margin, macroscopic multifocal disease or nodal metastasis → return for completion thyroidectomy.
- **Note** (หมายเหตุ): ATA 2015 mentions BRAF mutation (molecular biology of DTC): BRAF +ve → aggressive behaviour; recommends total + CND regardless of tumor size (but it is only category 2B — the author says it does not work).
- **Observation and follow up**: may be used for papillary microcarcinoma (PTC < 1 cm). ATA 2015 says operating is still best, but in some patients watchful waiting is possible — high operative risk, very old with limited life expectancy, no spread to nodes or distant organs, non-aggressive histology; over 5 years nodal spread is only 3%.
- **Recurrence** after surgery:
  1. Men recur more than women
  2. Age > 45 or < 20 years — higher recurrence at both ends, but the very old do worse than the young
- Beware **tall cell variant** — aggressive form of PTC with early node metastasis and poor prognosis; even < 1 cm it needs total thyroidectomy + complete CND only.
  - This principle applies to thyroglossal duct cyst after Sistrunk: PTC in the cyst with low-risk criteria → observe only; high risk → total.

### Management recommendations in ATA DTC patients 2015
- The interesting point is tumors > 1 cm but ≤ 4 cm.

| Clinical presentation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Cancer > 4 cm; gross extrathyroidal extension (clinical T4); clinically apparent lymph node (clinical N1) or distant mets (clinical M1) | Near-total or total thyroidectomy and excision of all primary tumor (unless contraindicated) |
| Cancer > 1 cm or < 4 cm; no extrathyroidal extension; no lymph node mets (clinical N0) | Near-total or total thyroidectomy, or lobectomy (risk stratification/enable RAI) (physician and patient preferences) |
| Cancer < 1 cm; no extrathyroidal extension; no lymph node mets (clinical N0) | Lobectomy |

- The author first meant to skip AJCC/TNM 8th edition, but ATA 2015 uses it as a guide for surgery, so it is included.

### Lymph node dissection (LND)
- Grasp basic lymphatic drainage first, for PTC:
  1. 50-90% have micrometastasis, but only 10-20% are clinically significant; micrometastasis at age > 45 has poor prognosis.
  2. Even with micrometastasis, doing LND upfront or waiting until clinically palpable nodes appear gives — surprisingly — no difference in survival.
  3. Nodal spread of thyroid cancer starts at level 6 (central neck), then levels 2, 3, 4, 5. Remember: it does not go to level 1.
- Level 6 (central neck) = nodes around the thyroid up to the medial edge of the common carotid. Bilateral central neck dissection (CND) clears nodes from the Delphian node above, down along the paratracheal nodes to the superior mediastinal nodes below, laterally carotid to carotid.
- Level 7 (superior mediastinum) can also be involved, but level 7 is not classed as a node to clear.
- **Therapeutic LND**: clearing nodes when there is evidence of nodal metastasis (N1). For spread to level 6 → central neck dissection; if also spread to the lateral group (2, 3, 4, 5) → add lateral neck dissection (2, 3, 4, 5), without level 1.
- Newer reports suggest PTC does not go to IIB and VA, so these need not be cleared either; lateral neck dissection for PTC takes only IIA, III, IV and VB.
- Figure: neck node levels — IA, IB (submandibular area, by anterior digastric), IIA, IIB, III, IV, VA, VB, VI, shown relative to the SCM, jugular vein, spinal accessory nerve, hyoid bone, carotid artery and cricoid cartilage.
- **Prophylactic CND**: clearing nodes preventively when there is no evidence of nodal spread (N0). 3 years earlier the trend was routine CND; in 2015, CND only with indications, not routine.
  - **Indications for prophylactic CND (level 6)**
    1. Tumor > 4 cm (T3 — remember)
    2. Multifocal > 1 cm
    3. Vascular invasion
    4. Gross extrathyroid invasion (T4)
    5. Spread to the lateral group (N1b)
    6. High-risk histologic type, e.g. tall cell
- **Central compartment level 6 lymph nodes**: after removing the thyroid, clear nodes near the RLN and the tracheoesophageal groove (printed "traccheoesophageal").
  1. Lateral: clear to the common carotid artery
  2. Medial: clear right up to the trachea
  3. Upper border: from the hyoid bone, sweeping down — including the prelaryngeal or Delphian node attached to the pyramidal lobe; clear fibrofatty and nodal tissue from top to bottom, behind the RLN, and in front of the prevertebral fascia
  4. Lower: down to level 7 (superior mediastinum), at the level of the innominate vein
- Palpable lateral neck node with biopsy-proven metastatic node or node seen on imaging → MRND is correct. Doing MRND when not palpable is definitely wrong, since residual micrometastatic nodes (printed "micrometasea") can be ablated with RAI.
- **Berry node picking** (printed "Burry"): excising only enlarged nodes — no benefit at all.
- **SLN mapping**: discussed much more in the past 10 years; sensitivity for thyroid cancer is usable, but no clinical reports yet on whether to adopt it — to be followed.
- Adjuvant treatment is written after Follicular Thyroid Cancer (FTC).

## Follicular Thyroid Cancer (FTC)
- In the differentiated thyroid cancer group; second most common; F : M = 3 : 1; age 50 years. Tends to spread via blood to bone and lung; nodes < 10%.
- **FNA or frozen section**: seeing only cells, it is hard to call FTC — diagnosis depends on histology showing capsular or vascular invasion.
- 2 histologic subtypes guide treatment:
  1. **Minimally invasive FTC** (low risk cancer): encapsulated FTC with only focal capsular invasion; grows very slowly.
  2. **Invasive FTC** (high risk cancer): most cases; angiolymphatic invasion or extensive invasion through the tumor capsule → total thyroidectomy.
- **Treatment**: by extent and subtype — low risk: lobectomy + isthmectomy is enough; high risk: total thyroidectomy. In theory, neck dissection is unnecessary because FTC rarely goes to nodes.
- **Postoperative care**: adjuvant treatment as for PTC.
- Case studies the "big tent" surgeons may ask:
  1. FTC after total thyroidectomy, then a solitary pulmonary nodule found → RAI rather than dragging the patient to thoracotomy.
  2. Before treating a metastatic site with RAI (lung or bone), check whether total has been done; if not, do completion thyroidectomy first — while thyroid tissue remains in the neck, RAI goes there, not to the lung or bone.

## Hurthle Thyroid Cancer (HTC)
- Formerly even WHO considered HTC a subtype of FTC, but its clinical behaviour differs, so it is separated.
- HTC is made of oxyphilic cells that stain pink.
- Age 50-60; women > men.
- Diagnosis by capsular or vascular invasion, like FTC.
- **HTC differs from FTC**:
  1. Often multicentric and bilateral
  2. Does not take up radioiodine
  3. Metastasizes via blood or to nodes (versatile — หลายใจ)
  4. Histology: abundant oncocytic or oxyphilic cells
- Older age than PTC and FTC, and the worst prognosis of DTC: multiple foci and bilateral up to 30%; lymph node +ve at diagnosis 25%; distant metastasis (printed "metadstasis") to lung and bone up to 34%; size > 4 cm → malignancy chance 65%.
- Most present with a dominant nodule; FNAC usually reads Hurthle cell neoplasm → lobectomy with isthmectomy to separate Hurthle cell adenoma from HTC, then await permanent section; about 20% of Hurthle cell neoplasms are HTC, which then needs return to the OR for total + CND.
- **Treatment**
  1. Total thyroidectomy — node spread is common and prognosis worse than PTC and FTC, so prophylactic CND is recommended; palpable lateral neck node → MRND straight away.
  2. HTC with minimal capsular invasion (uncommon): lobectomy is enough.
  3. RT may help, but RAI is of little use since HTC takes up very little I131 — so operate generously on HTC (ผ่าเยอะๆ เข้าไว้).

## Postoperative Management or Adjuvant Treatment of DTC
- **Radioactive Iodide Ablation**: in selected DTC (PTC or FTC), PO radioiodine ablates residual tissue and residual microscopic disease, lowers recurrence and cancer mortality, and makes follow-up with TG or total body scan easier.
- Cases for RAI ablation should be at high risk of recurrence; summary to remember:
  1. Size > 4 cm
  2. T4 staging
  3. Incomplete tumor resection
  4. Extrathyroidal tumor spread
  5. Nodal or distant metastasis
  6. Aggressive histology, e.g. tall cell, columnar cell, diffuse sclerosing carcinoma, insular, solid variant, hobnail variants, poorly differentiated
  7. Multifocal disease — often in those with prior RT to the face or neck
  8. Vascular invasion
  9. Postoperative unstimulated Tg > 5-10 ng/mL
- Cases that should not get RAI ablation are low risk: unifocal disease < 1 cm, no extrathyroid spread, no metastasis.
- ATA 2015 splits patients into 3 groups: low risk; intermediate risk (aggressive histology, size > 4 cm, node +ve, microscopic extrathyroid extension); high risk (distant metastasis). RAI after surgery for intermediate and high risk; low risk does not need RAI.
- **Steps of RAI ablation** (worth noting — สนใจหน่อย)
  1. Before RAI the patient should be as hypothyroid as possible: stop thyroid hormone for 2 wk, and low-iodine diet.
  2. Alternatively recombinant human TSH (trade name thymogen) raises TSH and so RAI uptake and storage in thyroid tissue: thymogen IM for 2 days, without stopping thyroid hormone — avoids hormone withdrawal problems.
  3. After RAI ablation dose 30 mCi, total body scan at 3-5 days; if scan +ve, repeat RAI ablation in 6-12 months.
  4. After RAI ablation, avoid pregnancy for 6-12 months; if breastfeeding, stop for 3 months.
- **Thyroid Hormone Suppression**: suppresses TSH, which stimulates cancer growth; levothyroxine (T4) dosed by TSH level. Principle: keep TSH low while the patient stays euthyroid → reduces recurrence.
  1. **Low risk DTC**: TSH 0.1-0.5 microIU/mL
  2. **High risk DTC**: TSH < 0.1 microIU/mL
  3. **Metastatic DTC**: TSH < 0.1 microIU/mL
- **RT**: for high-risk cases not fully removed, residual tumor that does not take up iodine, or palliation of pain from bone or brain metastasis.
- **Chemotherapy**: very limited use. Targeted therapy is still early; ATA 2015 looks keen on several targeted drugs coming out in future.

## Follow Up of DTC
- History, exam, TSH, thyroglobulin, anti TG Ab, neck US every 6 months for 2 years, then yearly. Note: thyroglobulin is a sensitive biomarker of disease recurrence and progression.
- PTC: palpate the neck and look carefully on US — the most common recurrence is in cervical nodes. Suspicious node ≥ 8 mm → needle for cytology plus Tg in the needle washout fluid; washout Tg > 10 ng/mL indicates a malignant node. Neck node recurrence → LND; surgery preferred, but if the patient cannot tolerate surgery consider percutaneous ethanol injection, RFA, stereotactic RT.
- TG high but neck US -ve → radionuclide total body scan to find the recurrence; when found, treat hard with RAI ablation — except brain spread, where surgery or combined stereotactic RT is preferred.

## Medullary Thyroid Cancer (MTC)
- Derived from C-cells (parafollicular cells), neuroendocrine cells that make and secrete calcitonin, which lowers serum calcium by inhibiting osteoclast activity.
- **Sporadic MTC**: 80%; usually age > 50; 68% single mass.
- **Familial MTC**: 20%; younger patients; multiple masses or bilateral up to 94%.
- Several family members with MTC → think of MEN 2, associated with RET oncogene mutation, MTC at a young age; MEN 2 may have pheochromocytoma too, which must be diagnosed and treated first.
- **FNA**: amyloid in stroma, no thyroid follicles, +ve immunohistochemistry for calcitonin. FNA alone is not enough — always confirm.
- **Confirmation**: serum calcitonin — high suggests MTC; also used for early detection and for follow-up after surgery. MTC also makes CEA, but less sensitive than calcitonin.
- Multicentricity 90%; at diagnosis level 6 nodes +ve in 50-70% and distant metastasis in 5%.
- US neck shows enlarged neck nodes, or calcitonin > 400 pg/ml → assess metastases with CT or MRI neck, CT chest, CT abdomen (focus on liver), bone scan.
- **Treatment**
  1. Total thyroidectomy + prophylactic bilateral CND
  2. Spread to the lateral group → clear LN levels 2A-5 on that side
  3. RT has some use for recurrent disease
  4. RAI is useless — MTC does not take up iodine
  5. Chemo also useless
  6. Targeted Rx: the only FDA-approved drug now is vandetanib
- Suspected MEN → screen RET oncogene; RET +ve → total thyroidectomy + CND even in children.
- Figure: MEN 2B features — mucosal neuromas, marfanoid habitus, medullated corneal nerve fibres.
  1. **MEN 2B**: especially codon mutations 883 and 918 — MTC very aggressive and early; total within 6 months (≤ 1 year).
  2. **MEN 2A**: look at which RET codon is mutated; codons 611, 618, 620, 634 → operate before age 5; other codons — no consensus yet on what age.
  3. CND in MEN 1 & MEN 2 (as printed) is not routine; do it with elevated preoperative calcitonin or US neck +ve central node.
  4. **Screening pheochromocytoma**: start at age 8; no prophylactic bilateral adrenalectomy in MEN 2.

## Anaplastic Thyroid Cancer (ATC)
- Extremely aggressive cancer, thought to arise from a well differentiated thyroid cancer; rapidly growing, rock-hard, painful mass, facial swelling, hoarseness; some get airway obstruction.
- Patients are usually very old or have had a goiter for a long time.
- Hard, fixed mass with enlarged neck nodes; at diagnosis > 90% have metastasis — lung first, then bone and brain.
- **Diagnosis**: FNA may be hard (very hard tissue); usually core or open biopsy — multinucleated giant cells.
- **Treatment**: very poor prognosis; mostly palliative care — radiosensitizing dose of doxorubicin with hyperfractionated RT may prolong time to death slightly.
  - Thyroidectomy is rarely possible; surgery's role is only tracheostomy for airway obstruction. Most live about 6 months. Some try expensive drugs, e.g. gefitinib, as a try; on the data so far there is still no good drug for this type.

## Thyroid Lymphoma
- Associated with Hashimoto (chronic inflammation).
- Fairly large, rapidly growing neck mass; some have fever, night sweats, weight loss (B symptoms).
- US: well-defined hypoechoic mass.
- FNA: hard to diagnose — too few cells; needs core or open biopsy.
- **Treatment**: CHOP regimen with RT.

## Thyroid Cancer Invading Aerodigestive Tract
- Uncommon, but big-tent surgeons like to ask. Suspect it with hoarseness, dysphagia, blood-stained sputum; neck US shows extension outside the thyroid → CT/MRI, and ENT panendoscopy to plan surgery.
- **Airway invasion**
  1. Partial invasion not through to the lumen, small area → resect part of the trachea; cover the defect with SCM muscle. Shaving excision alone is not enough — recurs often.
  2. Wide invasion → resect that segment of trachea and do primary anastomosis.
  3. Laryngeal invasion → partial laryngectomy of the invaded part is enough.
- **Esophageal invasion**
  1. Invasion only into the muscle layer, not submucosa → remove only the muscle layer, do not breach the mucosa.
  2. Invasion into submucosa → resect that segment; small defect → suture; large defect → muscle flap cover.

## Complications of Thyroid Surgery
- Many; only those residents must know are written:
  1. Bleeding, seroma
  2. Hypoparathyroidism (the line repeats "nerve injury: recurrent laryngeal nerve and superior laryngeal nerve", as printed)
  3. Nerve injury: recurrent laryngeal nerve and superior laryngeal nerve
  4. Others

## Postoperative Bleeding
- Subcutaneous bleeding — slow, found days after surgery; not dangerous.
- Rapid bleeding = deep hematoma under the platysma → compresses the trachea → upper airway obstruction; late treatment can be fatal. Usually within 2-12 hr.
- Most show only neck swelling; the wound may look dry with no oozing.
- **Treatment**
  1. Bedside immediate re-opening of the incision, scoop out clot, then maintain airway (recommended).
  2. Then to the OR under anesthesia: open the whole wound, wash out clot, find the bleeder, suture or tie.
- **Observation** (ข้อสังเกต) in dyspnea:
  1. From tracheomalacia or bilateral RLN injury → occurs immediately in the OR after extubation (ET-tube off).
  2. From hypoparathyroidism → takes 2-3 days to appear, and may show seizures (tetany).

## Postoperative Seroma
- Mostly small seromas that absorb on their own.
- Aspirate only if symptomatic.

## Hypoparathyroidism
- Most common complication of thyroid surgery; highest risk with total thyroidectomy plus bilateral central neck dissection.
- **Etiology**: parathyroids removed, or their blood supply (inferior thyroid artery) cut. Hypocalcemia symptoms usually 2-3 days after surgery; mostly temporary; permanent hypocalcemia < 2%.
- **Symptoms**: early numbness around the mouth or fingertips; severe cases restless, twitching from muscle cramps (printed "clamp").
- **Physical signs**
  1. **Chvostek's sign** — tap lightly on the cheek in front of the ear → facial nerve irritability, face twitches.
  2. **Trousseau's sign** — BP cuff on the arm above the patient's systolic pressure → hand ischemia; with hypocalcemia the fingers flex (carpal spasm).
- **Treatment**
  1. Mild or no symptoms → observe, with calcium carbonate 500-1000 mg 3 times after meals; elderly patients often absorb calcium poorly, so add vitamin D.
  2. Severe symptoms or seizures → 10% calcium gluconate 20-30 cc IV, preferably via central vein, slowly over the first 10-20 min until calcium rises and seizures stop; then IV drip 1.5 mg/kg/hr, about 50-100 cc IV infusion over 12 hours, until serum calcium is controlled. In practice: 10% calcium gluconate 60 cc in 5% D/W 500 cc IV 1 cc/kg/hr, checking serum calcium every 4 hours. Do not forget serum magnesium (printed "seum") — if low, add MgSO4. Next, assess the type of hypocalcemia:
     - 2.1 Transient → calcium can usually be stopped in 2-3 days
     - 2.2 Persistent → continue calcium, check serum calcium periodically, add Vitamin D
- **Prevention**
  1. Identify and preserve the glands — be very careful; total thyroidectomy can take the parathyroids with it. If not found, dissect the thyroid close to its capsule. If a parathyroid is accidentally removed, before bagging the specimen for pathology cut part of the gland for frozen section; when confirmed parathyroid → autotransplantation into strap or sternocleidomastoid muscle.
  2. Ligate the inferior thyroid artery close to the thyroid capsule.

## Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Injury
- RLN injury usually happens 2-3 cm before the nerve enters the vocal cord.
- RLN injury puts that cord in the paramedian or midline position from abductor laryngeal paralysis.
- Risk rises to 5% in neck re-exploration and total thyroidectomy with CND, and depends on the surgeon's skill.
- **Unilateral RLN injury**: hoarseness (not airway obstruction); cracked voice (timbre), weak voice, cannot shout, cannot cough hard; some choke on liquid diet when swallowing — send for swallow training.
- **Bilateral RLN injury**: airway obstruction, occurring right after the anesthetist removes the tube.
- **Treatment**
  1. Temporary paralysis usually recovers within 6-8 wk, at most 6 months; send for voice assessment about 2 weeks to 2 months after surgery.
  2. No better by 6 months → probably permanent cord paralysis; help the slack cord tighten — inject hyaluronic acid or calcium hydroxylapatite into the paralysed cord so the voice becomes clearer; laryngoplasty.
  3. Bilateral RLN injury is fairly emergent: re-intubate at once, have the anesthetist continue anesthesia, reopen the neck to find and repair the nerve.
     - If you recognise during surgery that the nerve has been cut, do perineurium repair with nonabsorbable suture straight away.
     - To maintain the airway, a permanent tracheostomy may be needed first; after about 6-12 months consult ENT about operating to widen the cord. ENT usually does lateral fixation of the midline cord, pulling it outward to widen the opening — the procedure is arytenoidectomy.
- **Prevention**
  1. Identify the RLN at the inferior thyroid artery in the tracheoesophageal groove; once found, trace it gently upward; do not hastily cut around Berry's ligament — there the RLN is close to the vocal cord and easily injured.
  2. Along the nerve's course, do not cut, tie, suture or cauterize before the RLN is seen.
  3. Thyroid surgery is delicate work — do not rush.
- **Preoperative laryngeal examination**: ENT laryngoscopy before surgery in:
  1. Preoperative voice abnormalities
  2. Previous major neck or upper chest surgery
  3. Known thyroid cancer with posterior extrathyroid extension
  4. Extensive central node metastases

## Superior Laryngeal Nerve Injury
- **External branch**: motor nerve to cricothyroid; injury → cannot speak for long and cannot reach high pitch.
- Injury usually happens when ligating the superior thyroid artery en masse without separating the branch. (As printed joke: a husband with a nagging wife who talks all day might want to injure her external branch — very bad.)
- If lucky enough to see the external branch, preserve it; if not seen, ligate and cut the superior thyroid artery as close to the upper pole of the thyroid as possible.
- **Internal branch**: sensory to the supraglottic larynx; injury → loss of sensation above the vocal cord → easy aspiration.
- **SLN injury**: temporary damage is lucky; permanent is unlucky — the nerve cannot be explored and repaired.

## Tracheomalacia
- Usually where a thyroid mass has compressed the trachea for a long time until the cartilage softens — mostly goiter cases — so the trachea is soft. After removing the goiter, before closing, palpate the trachea with a finger for collapse; if dyspnea, put the ET tube back at once.
- Figure: normal trachea keeps its lumen on inspiration and expiration; in tracheomalacia the lumen flattens and collapses on expiration.
- May need to wait 2-3 days or weeks until fibrosis around the trachea supports it from collapsing; then extubation is possible. Only 1/3 of patients need tracheostomy.

## Endoscopic Thyroidectomy
- The joy of doctors who love port holes (ชอบขุดรู): removing the thyroid with a scope via ports in the axilla or through the nipple.
- Summarized in 2009: still strict criteria for selecting patients.
- As surgeons gain experience (target > 25 cases), the criteria relax the more they do.
- **Criteria up to 2016**
  1. Size from < 3.5 cm, now up to < 6 cm, or thyroid volume < 25 ml
  2. Neck still "virgin" (printed "vergin") — no previous surgery or irradiation
  3. Benign nodule proven by FNA; if malignant, must be low risk
  4. Must not extend into the sternum — substernal goiter loses the right to port surgery
- **Contraindications** — few, mostly relative:
  1. High-risk malignancy — open surgery is better
  2. Hard, tight, fibrotic skin or subcutaneous tissue:
     - 2.1 Scleroderma
     - 2.2 Previous neck surgery or irradiation
  3. Bilateral or both lobe lesions
  4. Chronic thyroiditis
- In the next 2-3 years contraindications will probably shrink further, because:
  1. Even FNA papillary thyroid cancer, if low-risk PTC, has been reported done successfully by ports
  2. Both-lobe disease can use the anterior chest wall approach
- **Approach**
  1. Unilateral: transaxillary or lateral approach
  2. Bilateral: transanterior chest wall or central approach
- **Caution** (ข้อควรระวัง): postop bleeding with upper airway obstruction is very dangerous — there is no wound to split open to scoop out clot.
- Now: ports via the lower gum (transoral) — no visible scar at all.
