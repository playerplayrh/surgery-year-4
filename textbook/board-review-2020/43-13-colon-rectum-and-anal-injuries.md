# Colon, Rectum and Anal Injuries
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 426-454 (PDF pages 2560-2588) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 30 ธันวาคม 2558)

## Introduction
- Injuries to colon, rectum and anus are seen more and more, especially penetrating injury; colon is the second most often injured organ after small bowel, and only by a little.
- Long-held treatment concepts are being re-studied; several points have been shown to be wrong compared with what we used to do.
- Residents should know every treatment option and pick the best one for each patient, weighing the patient's condition, the colon's condition and their own experience.

## Colon Injury
- Colon injury is from penetrating more often than blunt trauma.
- Left colon is injured more often than right; of all segments, transverse colon is injured most.
- Parts of the colon are retroperitoneal → injury there gives few abdominal signs early.
- Injuries of the retroperitoneal colon, splenic flexure and rectosigmoid can be missed if not examined carefully.
- In blunt trauma, sigmoid colon is injured most, then cecum.
- Ascending colon, hepatic flexure, splenic flexure and descending colon are under some tension when anastomosed → mobilize them well so the anastomosis is not tight, or it will leak.

## Diagnosis of Colon Injury
- Most are diagnosed at operation:
  1. Smell of feces during the operation (as printed, a joke about excluding the resident who was scolded until his ears were ruined)
  2. Retrocolic hematoma or blood on the colonic wall
- PR finds blood, or penetrating injury to the back or flank → suspect colon injury, especially left side and rectum → proctoscopy at once.
- Suspected colon injury (e.g. bullet track, seatbelt mark sign) in a stable patient without generalized peritonitis needing urgent laparotomy → CT with IV contrast and water-soluble contrast enema; CT sensitivity 90%, specificity 96%.

## Management of Colon Injury (history)
- First era, World War I: everything was primarily repaired → mortality turned out high.
- Second era, World War II: era of colostomy → fewer deaths, but carrying a stool bag for a long time is tiresome (น่าเบื่อ).
- Later era, around 1979: the classic prospective randomized trial of **Stone and Fabian** set criteria for which patients should be diverted and which repaired; used up to 2000 and still used today, but many later reports/trials showed some of those criteria may not be very correct.
- Present era: RCTs by many leading surgeons try to set new criteria, especially **Demetrios Demetriades** (author of Colon and Rectal Trauma in Trauma 7 ed. 2013); the author recommends following his work, which has many strong scientific challenges.

## Colon Injury Grading Scale
- The most accepted and cited system is the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST); it splits severity into 2 levels: non-destructive and destructive colon injury.

### Nondestructive Colon Injury
- Loss of bowel wall < 50% circumference and no devascularization (AAST grade 1-2).
- Can be safely primarily repaired (1° repair) without regard to risk factors.
- Even GSW: small-caliber through-and-through holes can be closed if there is no risk of leak and closure does not narrow the lumen; if closure would narrow the lumen > 50%, resect and anastomose — easier on the surgeon (as printed).

### Destructive Colon Injury
- Loss of bowel wall > 50% circumference or devascularization needing segmental resection (AAST grade 3-5).
- Before 1990: all such cases got an ostomy.
- After 1990: even destructive injury, in well-selected cases, can still be primarily repaired, depending on surgeon experience and risk factors.

## Management of Colon Injury
- Penetrating injury: same principles whether from GSW or stab wound.
- Blunt trauma: 1° repair is less often possible, but if the patient is stable and contamination is not heavy, 1° repair can still be done.
- Position supine; if rectal injury is suspected too, use lithotomy.
- Explore the colon from IC valve to intraperitoneal rectum; when a hole is found, hold it with a Babcock first; beware missing holes along the mesentery.
- Always think of the **rule of two** (holes come in pairs).
- Current trend favours 1° repair over colostomy.
- Anastomosis: the mucosa to be sewn must be red like a baby's lips (เหมือนตูดเด็ก, as printed); wipe feces off the mucosa and colon wall; remove fat on the serosa; after suturing, do not reuse feces-soiled instruments.
- Bullets in the abdomen that are soiled with feces should be removed, or an intra-abdominal abscess follows.
- Wash all feces out of the abdomen (as printed, faculty feces especially are full of germs); give IV antibiotic before operating.
- Abdominal closure: isolated colon injury has SSI ≤ 5%. Little contamination → close the wound; moderate or severe contamination → leave the wound open and close later. Notably, with concomitant gastric injury, SSI rises to 30%.
- Drain is not needed: it does not reduce abscess — sticky or lumpy feces do not come out through a drain; if unsure, make an ostomy instead.

## Risk Factors for Colon Injury
- The 1979 classic RCT of Stone and Fabian proposed criteria for diversion (ostomy); with other considerations added, colostomy was advised when the following were present.
- **Criteria for considering colostomy (old)**
  1. Preoperative or intraoperative shock — SBP < 80
  2. Intra-abdominal bleeding > 1000 cc, or transfusion > 6 units
  3. Associated injury to > 2 other organs
  4. Massive or severe fecal contamination
  5. Delayed treatment > 6 hours after injury
  6. Destructive colon injury, even colon torn in several places
  7. Debilitated patient with many comorbidities, prior RT to the bowel, or distal narrowing
  8. Major abdominal wall loss needing mesh to close
  9. High colon injury scale (CIS score)
  10. Penetrating abdominal trauma index (PATI) score > 25

### Fecal contamination
- Degree of fecal spillage has been graded (as printed, the author apologises for writing so much about feces).
  1. **George's grading system** — 4 grades:
     - Grade 1: no fecal leak at all (as printed: the smell sticking to the resident)
     - Grade 2: feces confined to the injury site
     - Grade 3: feces spread within one quadrant
     - Grade 4: spread beyond 1 quadrant
  2. **Fecal contamination score**
     - 2.1 Mild: local contamination or within one quadrant
     - 2.2 Moderate: contamination in 2-3 quadrants
     - 2.3 Severe: contamination in all 4 quadrants

### Colon injury scale (CIS score)

| Grade | Injury |
|---|---|
| 1 | Serosal injury only |
| 2 | Single wall injury |
| 3 | Wall injury < 25% |
| 4 | Wall injury > 25% |
| 5 | Wall injury plus blood supply problem |

- Studies over the last 10-15 years do not support that the colostomy criteria forbid 1° repair — CIS score, PATI score, shock on admission or SBP < 80 mmHg, degree of fecal contamination, associated injury to > 2 organs are no longer contraindications to 1° repair. Location or site (left or right) has no effect; mesenteric vs antimesenteric border has no effect; delayed operation of 8 hours, even > 12 hours, is not a contraindication to 1° repair.
- **From 2014 onward, criteria for colostomy are only:**
  1. Preoperative BP < 80 mmHg with no response
  2. Massive fecal contamination
  3. Multiple colonic wounds or severe destructive lesion, especially from blunt trauma
  4. Delayed operation > 12 hour

## Surgical Tips and Techniques
1. As said, 1° repair or resection with anastomosis can now be done safely.
2. Serosal tear → sew with silk 3-0.
3. Perforation → debride, then single- or two-layer closure; reports show no difference. The author uses two layers: inner chromic 3-0, outer silk 3-0.
4. Stapler closure gives the same results as hand-sewn; the author prefers hand-sewn.
5. Resection with anastomosis suits:
   - 5.1 Bruised colon along a destructive lesion
   - 5.2 Several holes close together
   - 5.3 Compromised blood supply
6. Site (left or right) does not matter — both can be resected and anastomosed; healing and leak rate are the same. Small exception: for right-sided injuries, ileocolostomy (terminal ileum to colon) leaks less than colocolostomy.
7. Both colon and small bowel perforated, patient stable → repair both.
8. 1° repair with proximal diversion is not popular for colon injury, since proximal diversion does not lower leak rate — except low injuries that cannot be brought up as a colostomy (e.g. rectosigmoid and rectum); there, Hartmann is better.
9. Exteriorized repair: aim is to avoid colostomy — repair the colon, bring the repaired segment out onto the abdominal wall, cover it with vaseline gauze or NSS-moist gauze, check daily; if no leak, push it back into the abdomen; if it leaks, open it as a colostomy. Leak 80%, because the exteriorized colon dries and gets serositis — so if you bring the colon out, just make it a colostomy.
10. Colostomy with pelvic fracture → make it at the transverse colon, to:
    - 10.1 Avoid loss of tamponade
    - 10.2 Avoid infection of the hematoma below (feces mixed with blood = badly infected)
11. Colostomy closure can be done from 2 weeks to 2-3 months; the author prefers 2-3 months.
12. Antibiotic should cover E. coli and B. fragilis, given before operation and for 24 hours after.
13. Abdominal closure: fascia with monofilament interrupted sutures, irrigate the wound with NSS, leave skin open, then secondary suture after 4-5 days.

## Rectal Injury
- Treatment concepts have changed as for colon injury.
- **Mechanism of injury**
  1. Penetrating injury
  2. Blunt trauma
  3. Transanal injury — e.g. endoscope perforation, something inserted into the anus, or anal intercourse (slang as printed: โดนถั่วดำ)
- **Anatomy**
  - Figure: rectum ~15 cm long, sigmoid ~50 cm, anus ~4 cm; 5 cm marked across the bowel.
  - Rectum runs from the promontory of the sacrum to the anorectal line, length 15 cm; divided into:
    1. **Intraperitoneal rectum** — the part above the peritoneal reflection. Upper 2/3 of the rectum has peritoneum in front; laterally only the upper 1/3 is covered. This part is 1/3 of rectal length and is serosa-covered.
    2. **Extraperitoneal rectum** — continues below the intraperitoneal rectum, within the pelvis; 2/3 of total rectal length; no serosa.

## Diagnosis of Rectal Injury
- Intraperitoneal rectal injury presents like colon injury: rigid abdomen (peritonitis) → laparotomy.
- Extraperitoneal rectal injury is harder: no peritoneal signs.
- **Suspicion in**
  1. Penetrating injury of lower abdomen, hips, buttocks and pelvis
  2. Blunt trauma with fracture pelvis
  3. PR with blood on the glove tip
- PR: feel for a tear, whether sphincter tone is still good, and whether the prostate is floating (high-riding) or not.
- Blood on PR → rigid anoscope, proctoscope or sigmoidoscope; endoscopy is the most important investigation in rectal injury. Author advises not insufflating air — if there really is rectal injury, feces will spread more. Febrile, uncooperative patients (as is usual) → scope in the OR under some sedation; do not force it, or the anus tears.
- PR plus scope diagnoses rectal injury in 95%. So in GSW with blood on PR but no hole seen on scope, still suspect rectal injury; consider (not mandatory) water-soluble contrast enema to look for a hole.
- Contrast enema contributes little to diagnosis of rectal injury → use in selected cases. Suspected associated bladder injury, or GSW to the buttock → CT with or without rectal contrast is better than contrast enema alone.
- Again: rectal contrast should be water-soluble.

## Management of Rectal Injury
- **Intraperitoneal rectal injury** — treat like colon injury.
- **Extraperitoneal rectal injury** — position lithotomy. Old teaching: the **4Ds** = Diversion, Debridement, Distal washout and Drainage.
  - Now proximal diversion is the most important and should be done in every case; reports show the other 3 Ds are not necessary.

## Proximal Diversion
- Formerly "must do"; now "should do" (no longer mandatory).
- Done to keep feces from passing the rectal wound. 3-4 reports say nondestructive rectal injury with wound ≤ ¼ of circumference may not need diversion, but needs close follow-up. Author's view: surgeons are busy — don't overthink it, just do a colostomy.
- First choice: transverse loop colostomy, especially with fracture pelvis or associated intraperitoneal colon injury — reduces perineal sepsis and does not interfere with external fixator placement.
- Laparoscopic assisted colostomy has been reported successful when there is no indication for laparotomy (small wound, fast recovery, less pain), but supporting reports are few; the author finds open works better.
- For this year's exams, answer open colostomy first; for how to get complete diversion, see Trauma 7 ed.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis for 24 hours reduces infectious complications no differently from 3-5 days.
- Loop colostomy (caption): a properly conducted loop colostomy has its spur supported well above the skin.
- Linear stapler can be used for definitive fecal diversion (caption).
- Hartmann's procedure (caption): end colostomy and a rectal pouch; restoring bowel continuity later needs a full laparotomy and extensive dissection.
- **Colostomy closure**
  1. Timing — traditionally wait 6-8 weeks so tissue inflammation subsides. Some now argue for closing as early as 2 weeks so the patient does not carry a bag long; it can be done if the patient is stable with no sepsis. The author still follows the traditional 6-8 weeks.
  2. BE or water-soluble contrast before closure? Formerly routine; now probably not needed. If done, contrast to the distal side is enough; the author thinks that since you are doing it anyway, adding a little proximal contrast costs little extra time.

## Debridement with or without Repair
- Intraperitoneal rectum: close if it can be closed; manage with the same considerations as colon injury.
- Extraperitoneal rectum: if reachable transanally, close it — heals faster. If too deep to suture transanally, good proximal diversion with presacral drainage is enough; no need for extensive mobilization of the extraperitoneal rectum just to close the hole.

## Distal Washout
- Washing the distal limb of the ostomy with NSS to flush feces out of the anus. Now agreed it should not be done: it does not lower infection risk, and it spreads feces, making infection harder to control.

## Presacral Drainage
- No longer routine: drains only posterior and some lateral injuries; does not work for anterior injuries. Experts concluded presacral drainage does not reduce complications from peritoneal sepsis.
- If used, choose patients with posterior extraperitoneal rectal injury, inaccessible wound, or high infection risk — e.g. war weapons, high-energy blunt trauma, associated pelvic fracture, or severe perineal injury with feces spread everywhere.
- **Technique**
  1. Lithotomy position
  2. Curved incision behind the anus; dissect in front of the coccyx, then use the index finger to bluntly pass through the endopelvic fascia or Waldeyer's fascia into the presacral space, up to the rectal injury
  3. Place 2-3 penrose drains in the presacral space; author prefers penrose — more comfortable for the anus than redivac drain
  4. Leave 4-5 days; if fluid is not turbid and no pus, gradually shorten until out
- Formerly the coccyx was excised for easier drainage; this is no longer done.
- Per Demetrios, in theory it is not necessary.

## Anal Injury
- Injury here may include sphincter injury.
- Principle: repair the external sphincter as completely as possible.
- Rectum retracted up or perineum badly shattered → re-approximate the anus; suture rectum and anal canal to levator muscle and perirectal skin.
- Do not forget proximal colostomy and presacral drainage.
- Definitive repair: wait 6 months to 1 year.
