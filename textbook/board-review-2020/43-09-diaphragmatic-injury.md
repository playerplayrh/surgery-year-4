# Diaphragmatic Injury
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 322-337 (PDF pages 2456-2471) · Trauma Edition 2 (chapter dated 23 พฤศจิกายน 2558)

- Usually seen with penetrating injury to the thoracoabdominal area, meaning:
  1. Anterior: from nipple to costal margin
  2. Flank: from anterior to posterior axillary line
  3. Posterior: from tip of scapula to the rib margin at the back
- In blunt trauma the diaphragm tears at the left dome in up to 90%.

## Anatomy of the Diaphragm
- Musculotendinous, dome-shaped structure: central tendon in the middle, muscle laterally fanning out to attach around the chest wall ("shaped like a parachute").
- On the right the large liver sits under the diaphragm; on the left only the spleen, smaller than the liver. With blunt abdominal trauma intra-abdominal pressure rises quickly by about +150-200 cmH2O, so most tears are on the left.
- The IVC passes through the right side of the central tendon.
- **Phrenic nerve**: from C3-5. On the left it runs down the posterolateral surface of the pericardium; on the right beside the IVC. At the diaphragm it spreads in a curvilinear fashion; its terminal branches end about 2-3 cm from the chest wall margin. So an incision in the diaphragm kept within 2 cm of the margin is guaranteed to spare the phrenic nerve.
- During expiration the lung collapses: the right diaphragm can rise to the 4th ICS anteriorly, 6th laterally, 8th posteriorly; the left diaphragm rises anteriorly to the 5th ICS. So penetrating injury below the 4th ICS on the right or the 5th ICS on the left may hit the diaphragm.

## Phases of Diaphragmatic Injury
- Normal supine quiet breathing: intra-abdominal pressure +2 to +10 cm H2O; intrathoracic pressure about -5 to -10 cm H2O.
- With a diaphragm tear, abdominal organs move into the chest, where pressure is negative.
- **3 phases**
  1. **Acute phase**: early; herniation may or may not be present; usually diagnosed during exploratory laparotomy done for another reason.
  2. **Latent phase**: may last weeks, months or years; usually an occult injury that was missed; positive abdominal pressure gradually widens the tear until herniation causes symptoms.
  3. **Chronic and obstructive phase**: organs herniated into the chest compress the lung, and the herniated organ (usually stomach) becomes obstructed.

## Clinical Presentation
- Diaphragmatic injury is hard to diagnose because attention goes to the associated injuries.
- Associated injury in 70-80%: on the right usually liver; on the left usually stomach, next spleen. Beware: descending aorta injury found in 5%.
- **Always think of it when** (นึกถึงเสมอ):
  1. Shot or stabbed in the left thoracoabdomen
  2. Blunt trauma with significant deceleration, e.g. fall from height or steering wheel hitting the chest hard
  3. NG tube inserted but aspiration is blocked/stuck (ดูดติดๆ)
  4. During ICD insertion, the finger sweeping the pleural cavity feels the stomach
  5. Dyspnoea; bowel sounds heard over the lung
  6. Flat scaphoid abdomen

## Investigation
- **Chest x-ray**: poor for diaphragm injury (40% look normal). Suggestive findings:
  1. Left diaphragm higher than right (normally right higher than left), with an irregular diaphragm outline
  2. Atelectasis of the left lower lung or left pleural effusion
  3. Air-fluid level or gas bubble above the diaphragm line
  4. Mediastinal shift to the right
  - If the film leaves you anxious and unable to sleep, put down an NG tube; if the NG coils in the chest, relax — go straight to laparotomy.
  5. A high diaphragm outline must be differentiated from eventration, paralysis, collection and paraesophageal hiatal hernia:

| Differential diagnosis | Features |
|---|---|
| Eventration of diaphragm | No dependent viscera sign; hemidiaphragm appears intact; no associated injuries; typically elderly females without recent trauma |
| Diaphragm paralysis | Paradoxical motion at fluoroscopy (sniff test); no recent trauma |
| Sub-pulmonic or loculated pleural effusion | No abnormally positioned air-filled bowel; crus intact |
| Paraesophageal hernia | Tear rare at esophageal hiatus |

- **FAST**: poor for hollow viscus and diaphragm.
- **CT scan**: same limits as FAST for hollow viscus and diaphragm; CT is mainly for associated injuries. CT may show the **collar sign** — stomach constricted by the diaphragm into a bottle-neck shape.
- **DPL**: now rarely done — poor for diaphragmatic injury, except when the patient already has an ICD and DPL fluid/enteric content comes out through the ICD.
- **Laparoscopy**: very high sensitivity; best diagnostic modality for occult injury. Can also treat if the hole is not too large. Caution: insufflation may cause tension pneumothorax — if unsure, insert ICD first.
- **Thoracoscopy**: suits cases that seem complicated.

## Management of Diaphragmatic Injury
- **Expectant conservative treatment**: should not be used — the diaphragm moves all the time and cannot heal.
- **Laparoscopic or thoracoscopic repair**: may be used for diaphragm tears, with stapler or laparoscopic suturing.
- **Acute phase**: laparotomy so that associated abdominal injuries can be dealt with too. Remember (ต้องจำ):
  1. Before surgery insert an NG tube and aspirate as much gastric content as possible, to help prevent aspiration.
  2. Midline incision is usual; small exception — right thoracotomy is recommended for a right diaphragm tear with only the liver herniated into the chest.
  3. Explore thoroughly and treat associated injuries, then pull the stomach down — or slip a tube up beside the tear into the chest to raise intrathoracic pressure, which helps push the stomach down.
  4. **Suturing the diaphragm** (วิธีเย็บ diaphragm): nonabsorbable suture no. 1-0. The author follows his teacher's method — 2 layers: first horizontal mattress, 1 cm from the wound edge and stitches 1 cm apart; upper layer continuous. Silk or prolene both fine. The repair must be tension-free to prevent recurrent diaphragmatic hernia.
  5. Massive destruction with large loss of diaphragm — 2 ways to close:
     - 5.1 Prosthetic mesh, 2 layers sewn overlapping, covering the pleural and peritoneal surfaces.
     - 5.2 Relocation: detach the diaphragm from its chest wall attachment and suture it back to a rib higher than the tear.
  6. If you do not want an ICD: before tying the last stitch, have the anaesthetist squeeze the bag hard to drive air out of the pleural space, then tie. The author, being lazy about being called at night, puts an ICD in every case.
- **Latent or chronic phase**: usually after 1 week; abdominal associated injuries are unlikely by then, and herniated organs have many adhesions — so in this phase opening the chest is recommended; surgery is easier than via the abdomen.
- **Contamination of GI content**: rice or vegetables are not a big deal, but if feces enter the chest, management is debated; 2 approaches:
  1. Widen the diaphragmatic defect a little, or irrigate through the ICD until clear — the author prefers this.
  2. Some, very afraid of empyema, add an anterolateral thoracotomy to wash out thoroughly, then leave 2 ICDs — not wrong either.

## Summary Management of Diaphragmatic Injury
- ATLS.
- Symptoms, physical exam, chest film; if suspicious, especially a left diaphragm higher than the right → insert NG tube; NG coiled in the chest → laparotomy.
- Still unsure → CT, water-soluble contrast swallow, or laparoscopy; if confirmed → open and repair the diaphragm.

Figure: Diagnosis of diaphragm injury in thoracoabdominal trauma, as steps —
1. Blunt or penetrating → resuscitation.
2. Insert NG (if no contraindications) → CXR.
3. CXR abnormal and diagnostic → surgical intervention.
4. CXR abnormal but nondiagnostic, or CXR normal → further imaging: helical CT, MRI, contrast study, laparoscopy (penetrating injury) → if diagnostic → surgical intervention.
