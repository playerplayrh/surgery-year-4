# Neurosurgery 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 963-984 (PDF pages 2061-2082)

- Author's note: the neurosurgery a surgery resident should know well is trauma, already summarized in edition 2 (2559). This year's chapter covers the disease group likely to appear in MCQs, so readers are not lost in the exam room.

## Neuroanatomy
- **Arterial supply to the brain**
  1. Anterior circulation — from the carotid; large branch = middle cerebral artery, smaller branch = anterior cerebral artery
  2. Posterior circulation — from the vertebral arteries; at the level of the pons they form the basilar artery
  3. Circle of Willis — joins the anterior and posterior circulations
- **Venous return** — brain venous blood returns to the heart via the internal jugular veins; tying both internal jugular veins gives severe brain swelling, and the patient hardly survives.

## Visual Field Defect
- The pattern of visual loss tells where along the optic pathway the lesion is.
- Figure: Lesion site and field defect —
  - Retinal lesion → paracentral scotoma
  - Optic nerve → monocular field loss (one eye)
  - Optic chiasm → bitemporal hemianopia
  - Optic tract → homonymous hemianopia
  - Temporal lesion (optic radiation, lower fibres) → superior homonymous quadrantanopia
  - Parietal lesion (optic radiation, upper fibres) → inferior homonymous quadrantanopia
  - Optic radiation / striate cortex → homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing

## Sensory Dermatome

## Increased Intracranial Pressure
- Normal intracranial volume is made up of (figure "Volume of intracranial contents"): glia 700-900 ml, neurones 500-700 ml, blood 100-150 ml, CSF 100-150 ml, ECF 100-150 ml.
- Raised ICP → **Cushing triad**: hypertension, bradycardia, respiratory irregularity. In children, the fontanelle bulges.
- **Monro-Kellie-Doctrine hypothesis** (printed "Docline"): the brain sits in a non-expandable skull and brain tissue is non-compressible. When a space-occupying lesion raises pressure, early on the brain compensates by reducing venous blood and CSF volume, so ICP stays normal. Once the mass is too large to compensate, ICP rises steadily and herniation begins.
- On the volume-pressure curve: past the point of decompensation, even a small rise in mass volume makes ICP shoot up, until herniation occurs. (Figure caption: intracranial contents first compensate for a new mass such as subdural or epidural hematoma; once the mass reaches a critical threshold, ICP rises rapidly, which can reduce or stop cerebral blood flow.)

## Trigeminal Neuralgia
- Incidence 4/100,000. Main symptom: severe pain along trigeminal nerve divisions, usually V2 and V3. Common triggers: touch and temperature; in severe cases even a breeze, brushing teeth or chewing causes pain.
- Figure: Branches — V1 ophthalmic (forehead, eyes, part of nose); V2 maxillary (cheek, upper lip); V3 mandibular (lower lip, teeth, chin). Pain site depends on the branch involved.
- Most common mechanism: thought to be pulsatile vascular compression of CN5 just before it enters the brainstem (pons).
- Send MRI to look for posterior fossa tumor and multiple sclerosis.
- **Treatment**
  1. Oral carbamazepine — relieves pain very well; next are baclofen and gabapentin. Anticonvulsants such as dilantin were used in the past but no longer.
  2. Surgery for patients failing drugs: **microvascular decompression** — dissect the vessel off the nerve and place teflon or polyvinyl sponge as a barrier between them.
  3. Others: percutaneous trigeminal rhizotomy with RFA of the trigeminal ganglion, or glycerol injection into the pain fibres; works in some patients.

## Hydrocephalus
- Accumulation of CSF from obstructed flow or poor reabsorption of CSF → ventricular dilatation.
- **Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)**
  1. Normal total CSF 150 cc; about 25 cc is in the ventricles.
  2. 80% is made by the choroid plexus; the rest from the brain interstitium.
  3. CSF flow:
     - 3.1 Lateral ventricle → foramen of Monro → 3rd ventricle
     - 3.2 → aqueduct of Sylvius → 4th ventricle
     - 3.3 → foramen of Magendie (midline) and foramina of Luschka (lateral) → over the surface of the brain and spinal cord
     - 3.4 Reabsorbed via the arachnoid villi at the superior sagittal sinus
- **Causes of hydrocephalus**
  1. **Obstruction**
     - 1.1 Non-communicating hydrocephalus — obstruction within the ventricles, so CSF cannot leave them; commonest cause: aqueduct stenosis
     - 1.2 Communicating hydrocephalus — obstruction outside the ventricles, usually in the subarachnoid space, from arachnoid granuloma, scarring, or CSF blockage by blood clot or tumor
  2. **Oversecretion** — e.g. choroid plexus tumor
- **Clinical manifestation**
  1. Children — bulging fontanelle, enlarging head, vomiting
  2. Adults
     - 2.1 Acute — features of raised ICP: headache, nausea, vomiting, blurred vision, drowsiness = high pressure hydrocephalus
     - 2.2 Chronic — usually elderly > 60 years; brain atrophy enlarges the ventricles, pressure not high. Triad: dementia, gait ataxia, urinary incontinence, with no headache = **normal pressure hydrocephalus**
- **Investigation**
  1. CT — shows enlarged ventricles; used to plan shunt placement
  2. Ventriculography — to define the site of duct obstruction clearly
- **Treatment**
  1. Most used shunt: extracranial shunt = **ventriculoperitoneal shunt**; advantage: blockage or malfunction is easier to fix than an internal shunt, which needs reopening the skull.
  2. If caused by tumor → resect the tumor.

## Intracerebral Hemorrhage
- Commonest cause: hypertension.
- Commonest bleeding site: basal ganglia (50%) — basal ganglia include putamen, globus pallidus, internal capsule.
- (Other sites shown on CT figure: thalamic, pontine, cerebellar hemorrhage.)

## Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)
- Commonest cause is trauma, but this book covers spontaneous rupture of aneurysm and AVM.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Most classic: "the worst headache of my life" (as printed, a joke about residents' pay)
  2. Followed by stiff neck (nuchal rigidity), photophobia; heavy bleeding can cause coma
- **Investigation** — when SAH is suspected, investigate (สนใจหน่อย):
  1. CT brain — first investigation, sensitivity 95%; bright signal in the interhemispheric fissure and CSF cisterns, blood spreading to dependent portions.
  2. If CT is negative but SAH strongly suspected and no signs of mass effect → lumbar puncture; SAH gives bloody CSF with RBC > 100,000/mL. In summary: CT and LP both negative → SAH ruled out.
  3. After SAH is diagnosed → 4-vessel angiography.
- **Hunt-Hess clinical grading scale** — groups SAH to guide treatment and predict outcome (exam favourite):

| Grade | Clinical presentation |
|---|---|
| 0 | Asymptomatic unruptured aneurysm |
| 1 | Alert, few symptoms; may have mild headache or slight neck stiffness |
| 2 | Alert but severe headache and obvious neck stiffness |
| 3 | Drowsy with mild focal neurological deficit, but still able to talk with the doctor |
| 4 | Stupor with clear focal neurodeficit, e.g. weakness |
| 5 | Coma with decerebrate or decorticate posture |

- Grade 0-2: good outcome 70%; grade 3-4: good outcome 15%; grade 5: good outcome 0%.
- **Treatment** — every type needs intensive medical Rx in ICU.
  1. Grade 4, 5 — intubate and stabilize the patient first.
  2. Then, in every grade, occlude the bleeding point.
  3. SAH, especially from ruptured aneurysm, is followed by cerebral vasospasm → ischemic stroke; calcium channel blockers help control BP and maintain optimal perfusion.

## Intracranial Aneurysm
- Focal dilatation of the vessel wall → thin wall at risk of rupture; so an aneurysm found should be treated to prevent rupture.
- Figure: Aneurysm site distribution — anterior communicating artery 40%, middle cerebral artery 34%, internal carotid/posterior communicating junction 20%, basilar tip 4%.
- 85% from the anterior circulation (carotid) — number one is middle cerebral aneurysm (as printed); the other 15% from the posterior circulation (vertebrobasilar).
- Age at rupture: usually > 40 years.
- **Risk of rupture**
  1. Unruptured aneurysm: rupture risk 2-3% per year.
  2. After rupture: 1/3 die at home, 1/3 die in hospital; of the 1/3 who survive, only 1/3 are normal, the other 2/3 need PT or a wheelchair.
  3. After the first rupture, chance of a second rupture is about 50% within 6 weeks.
- **Treatment** — treat SAH by grade; treat a ruptured aneurysm quickly. Current standard of care for ruptured intracranial aneurysm = **early aneurysm occlusion**, because rebleeding risk is 25-35% within 2 weeks.
  1. Craniotomy + clipping — still the standard Rx.
  2. Interventional Rx with coil embolization — now very popular; consider it first, and if it fails, go on to clip.

## Arteriovenous Malformation (AVM)
- Irregular connection between artery and vein: arterial blood enters the vein without passing a capillary bed, so the vein carries red arterial blood with fast flow, high volume and pressure; eventually the wall thins and ruptures, followed by seizures.
- 10% of AVMs have an associated aneurysm, rupture risk 2-4% per year.
- Patients < 30 years (aneurysm patients > 50 years).
- **Symptoms**
  1. Most common initial symptom: intracranial hemorrhage, 50%
  2. Sudden headache from bleeding, and loss of consciousness
- CT: enhanced abnormal vessels.
- **Treatment**
  1. Ruptured AVM: usually delayed Rx — wait 3-4 wk for the brain to recover from acute injury, because bleeding and vasospasm effects of ruptured AVM are much less than ruptured aneurysm; wait until stable.
  2. Definitive Rx — 3 options:
     - 2.1 Microsurgical resection — best results
     - 2.2 Coil embolization — mostly pre-op Rx to shrink the AVM before surgery
     - 2.3 Stereotactic radiosurgery — suits AVM < 3 cm

## Brain Abscess
- The brain normally resists infection via the blood-brain barrier, but organisms can reach it by hematogenous spread — most often in patients with lung infection, next in heart disease (cyanotic heart disease, as printed "โรคหัวใจพิการ"); or directly from trauma to brain tissue.
- From otitis media (หูน้ำหนวก): abscess usually in the temporal lobe and cerebellum.
- Organisms: commonly strep and staph; after trauma usually staph; in HIV the abscess organism is toxoplasmosis ("treponema gondii", as printed).
- **Symptoms**: fever, headache, mass effect, stiff neck, seizure.
- MRI is most accurate for diagnosis and treatment planning: well demarcated ring-enhancing wall with surrounding edema, usually at the gray-white interface.
- **Treatment**
  1. IV antibiotic at least 6-8 wk; some cases up to 10 wk.
     - Indication for IV antibiotic alone:
       - 1.1 Small size > 1.5 cm (as printed); some texts set the cut-off at < 3 cm
       - 1.2 Multiple small abscesses
       - 1.3 Critical location
       - 1.4 High surgical risk
  2. Surgical treatment — not routine; only with indications.
     - Indication for surgical treatment:
       - 2.1 Well encapsulated abscess > 1.5-3 cm
       - 2.2 Failed medical Rx — follow-up CT shows no decrease in size within 1 wk
       - 2.3 Near the ventricle — rupture into the ventricle is disastrous
       - 2.4 Mass effect
       - 2.5 Uncertain diagnosis — CT sometimes cannot tell it from tumor
- Two surgical methods:
  1. Stereotactic needle aspiration — for abscess > 1.5 cm
  2. Resection — for well encapsulated brain abscess
- Problem after brain abscess: seizures.

## Pyogenic Vertebral Osteomyelitis
- Bacterial infection of the vertebral body, mostly by hematogenous spread from a distant site; less often by direct extension from nearby infection, e.g. psoas abscess or perinephric abscess.
- Mostly Staph aureus and enterobacter.
- Patients are usually debilitated, e.g. drug addicts, diabetics, renal failure.
- **Symptoms**: fever, back pain; if bone collapses from osteomyelitis → features of cord compression.
- **Film**: lytic lesion in bone; best is MRI.
- **Treatment**
  1. IV antibiotic
  2. Surgery, e.g. debridement, when antibiotics fail; if spinal cord compression develops → decompression
  3. Stabilization and spinal fusion — indicated when stability and deformity problems lead to neurological deficit

## Pediatric Neurosurgery
- Pediatric neurosurgical conditions differ from adult ones in many ways, e.g. congenital malformations: spinal and cranial dysraphism, Chiari malformation and craniosynostosis.

### Spinal Dysraphism
- Abnormal closure of the spinal cord and nerves in utero, with failure of midline fusion of the spinous processes. An open defect = **spina bifida aperta**; common forms: myelomeningocele and meningocele.
- Figure: Spina bifida types — 1 normal spine; 2 spina bifida occulta; 3 meningocele; 4 myelomeningocele.
- Small defect = **spina bifida occulta**; commonly a dermal sinus.

### Spina Bifida Aperta
- Common form: myelomeningocele; thought to be linked with folate deficiency in pregnancy; neural tissue protrudes outside the spinal canal.
- Symptoms: motor, sensory and ANS deficit (bladder and bowel dysfunction); 80% have hydrocephalus.
- Treatment: close the defect surgically within 24-48 h; before surgery keep the baby prone, cover the defect with moist sterile dressing, and give prophylactic antibiotic.

### Congenital Dermal Sinus
- A pit or tract in the midline of the trunk, anywhere from the occiput to the coccyx; commonly in the LS area; a form of spina bifida occulta.
- Besides the sinus, an associated tumor is common: dermoid and epidermal cyst.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Debris or purulent material discharging from the opening
  2. Infection — meningitis or extradural abscess, since the tract opens down to the dura
  3. Neurological deficit, usually from a dermoid cyst compressing the cord
- MRI is the diagnostic study of choice — shows the sinus and tract clearly.
- Surgery is the main treatment:
  1. IV antibiotic; never probe or inject dye into the sinus
  2. Resect and remove the whole tract (จำ)

### Cranial Dysraphism
- Abnormal closure of the cranial vault or cranial base → meningocele and encephalocele (brain tissue protrudes too).
- Surgery: remove the sac and close the defect.

### Arnold Chiari Malformation
- Cerebellar tonsils descend below the level of the foramen magnum; 3 types: type 1, 2, 3.
- **Chiari malformation 1** — cerebellar tonsil descent > 5 mm; may be asymptomatic or cause neck pain, headache from hydrocephalus, weakness; usually adults. Treatment: posterior fossa decompression at the foramen magnum with re-establishing CSF flow by removing the rim of the foramen magnum and the posterior arch of C1; may also need a ventriculo-peritoneal shunt.
- **Chiari malformation 2** — brainstem descends together with the cerebellar tonsils; often associated with myelomeningocele; hydrocephalus is very common; patients are children. Shunt indications as for hydrocephalus surgery, i.e. weakness, lower cranial nerve paresis, respiratory distress.

### Craniosynostosis
- Figure (cranial sutures): metopic, coronal, sagittal, lambdoid sutures; anterior and posterior fontanelles.
- Premature fusion of cranial sutures → abnormal skull shape; about 50-60% sagittal suture, 30-35% coronal suture. That part of the skull is restricted while the rest grows normally → abnormal shapes such as scaphocephaly, brachycephaly.
- **Scaphocephaly** — premature fusion of the sagittal "sinus" (as printed); long, narrow head.
- **Plagiocephaly** or flat head syndrome — flat and also skewed head; "plagio" is Greek for oblique/slanted. Flat heads may come from the baby lying on the same spot for a long time, or from early suture fusion.
- **Brachycephaly** — flat head only (not skewed).
- **Treatment**: cut out the fused suture and fit a skull-remodeling device (wide suturectomy and placement in a cranial remodeling helmet); can be done from age 4-6 months.

## Brain Tumors
- Two groups by histology/cell of origin:
  1. **Glia cell or astrocyte cell**
     - 1.1 Glioma, 50%
     - 1.2 Oligodendroglioma and ependymoma
  2. **Non-glia cell**
     - 2.1 Meningioma — from arachnoid cells
     - 2.2 Schwannoma — from Schwann cells
     - 2.3 Medulloblastoma — from primitive neuroectodermal remnant
     - 2.4 Pituitary tumor
     - 2.5 Pineal tumor
     - 2.6 Hemangioblastoma — from blood vessels
     - 2.7 Metastatic tumor
- Most intracranial tumors are metastases, more than primaries. Commonest source in men: lung; in women: breast; then melanoma. Cancer that rarely goes to brain: ovary.
- Most common malignant tumor in children: medulloblastoma; most common tumor in adults: glioma or astrocytoma.
- In HIV patients the commonest is primary intracranial lymphoma.
- Common site of brain tumor in children: posterior fossa (infratentorial).
- **Signs and symptoms**: headache, typically worst in the morning, nausea, vomiting, signs of increased intracranial pressure, forgetfulness, hemiparesis.
- Figure: Headache patterns to tell apart from tumor headache — cluster: pain around the eyes, patient may wake often in the middle of the night; tension: once or a few times a week, or continuous for several days; migraine: severe intense throbbing, pulsating pain, mostly on one side of the head.
- Beware posterior fossa tumors (brainstem and cerebellum): space is small, so even a small mass can herniate, compress the brainstem, stop breathing and kill quickly.
- Main treatment of brain tumor: surgical resection; if inoperable or incompletely removed → RT.

## Meningiomas
- Overall most common benign tumor = meningioma (15-20%); women more than men 2:1; very rare in children. CT: high density homogeneous contrast-enhanced tumor; slow growing; 5-year survival about 90%.
- Named by location (figure: parasagittal, olfactory groove, suprasellar, clivus, foramen magnum, cerebellar [label partly cut]).
- Example: olfactory groove meningioma — CN1 compression: anosmia, personality change, seizure.
- Sphenoid wing → visual problems from optic nerve compression.
- Foramen magnum → neck pain with arm and leg weakness.
- **Treatment**: surgical resection.

## Vestibular Schwannoma
- (Heading printed "Vesibular".) Tumor of CN8 at the CP angle.
- Symptoms: hearing loss, ataxia, vertigo.
- **Treatment**: surgical resection.

## Pineocytoma
- Tumor of the pineal gland; < 1% of primary brain tumors; age 10-20 years; most common type of tumor (in this region) is germinoma.
- The pineal gland lies behind the 3rd ventricle and secretes melatonin; helps the hypothalamus and pituitary gland function.
- Symptoms: hydrocephalus, increased intracranial pressure, Parinaud syndrome from tumor compressing the tectal plate → upward gaze palsy.
- **Imaging**: MRI shows a midline tumor behind the 3rd ventricle.
- **Treatment**: surgical removal of the mass.
- Slow growing; 5-year survival about 90%.
- The author's view: residents only need the idea; this much is enough to not be lost in the exam room.

## Spinal Tumors
- Two main types:
  1. **Extradural (epidural) tumor** — mostly metastatic; in men from lung and prostate, in women from breast.
  2. **Interdural (subdural) tumor** (as printed)
     - 2.1 Extramedullary tumor — outside the spinal cord; usually benign, e.g. meningioma, neurofibroma, neurilemmoma; in children the commonest is astrocytoma.
     - 2.2 Intramedullary tumor — "outside" the spinal cord (as printed; heading says intramedullary); mostly glial tumors, e.g. ependymoma, astrocytoma, glioblastoma.
- Investigation of choice: MRI; myelogram only when MRI is not conclusive.
- **Treatment**
  1. Surgical resection is the main treatment; some cases also need decompression and spine stabilization.
  2. Incomplete resection → add RT.
  3. Steroid for cord swelling.

## Spinal Tumors (second heading as printed; content on benign tumors)
- Most are benign tumors, slow growing; but a previously painless mass that becomes painful → beware cancer.
- **Treatment**: surgical resection, for diagnosis and to rule out malignancy.

## Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors
- Several types, e.g. schwannoma, neurofibroma.
- Figure: Schwannoma sits eccentrically, pushing nerve fibres aside; neurofibroma lies within the nerve with fibres running through it.

### Schwannoma
- Also called neurilemmoma.
- Most common peripheral nerve tumor; origin = Schwann cells, which form myelin in peripheral nerves.
- Patient age 30 years.
- **Symptoms**: tender mass; pressing it gives a shooting, electric pain (เสียวแปร๊บ).
- **Treatment**: excision (tumor shelled out from nerve fascicles, per figure).

### Neurofibroma
- Arises within the nerve; benign tumor with malignant transformation about 10%.
- **Symptoms**: tender mass, but no shooting pain unlike schwannoma.

### Malignant Nerve Sheath Tumor
- Mass painful all the time without touching; motor and sensory deficit along the nerve distribution.
