# Hand 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 986-995 (PDF pages 2084-2093) · separate chapter by the same author that falls inside the Neurosurgery PDF range (not in the batch list)

- This book's hand section covers only what a general surgery resident must know: hand infection, especially tenosynovitis and deep space infection, and common hand diseases chosen because they have been examined — compression neuropathy (carpal tunnel syndrome) and benign tumor (carpal ganglion). Hand injury is already in Trauma 2018, so it is not repeated.
- Finger and hand anatomy is inserted under each disease; know only what is needed, no need to go deep.

## Hand Infection
- Usually follows an injury, often minor, e.g. picking at the nail, nail biting, or a splinter/thorn prick.
- Organisms: bacteria 90%, Staph aureus 80%, next strep.
- Pus → swab culture. Film if a retained foreign body is suspected, or infection may have spread into bone (osteomyelitis).
- Besides proper antibiotic, with or without I & D: elevate the arm, keep the hand in functional position, and immobilize, e.g. on a volar slab ("volar slap" as printed).

## Paronychia
- Infection of the nail fold at the side of the nail; acute or chronic.
- Symptoms: pain, swelling, redness at the nail fold; very tender on pressure.
- **Acute paronychia** — bacterial infection. Treatment:
  1. Superficial infection, no pus yet → oral antibiotic.
  2. Pus present, usually after 24-48 h → I & D with partial nail extraction (figure: remove the affected portion of nail, small incision if needed, pack with petrolatum gauze).
- **Chronic paronychia** — fungal, commonest candida; long history, recurrent. Patients usually work in moisture, e.g. market vendors, laundry workers, dishwashers. Main symptom: itch; chronic inflamed nail fold, thickened rough nail. Treatment: antifungal, oral and topical; often the whole nail must be removed.
- **Herpetic whitlow** — herpes simplex virus entering a wound directly; seen in health workers in contact with oral and airway secretions of patients, and in HIV patients. Pain, fever, body aches; small clear vesicles in clusters with inflamed lymph nodes. Tzanck smear shows multinucleated giant cells. Treatment: symptomatic plus oral acyclovir; usually heals within 7-10 days, at most 1 month. Do not drain — drainage may cause secondary bacterial infection.

## Eponychia
- Infection of the nail fold at the nail base (eponychial fold).
- Treatment as acute paronychia: I & D by incising both sides of the eponychial fold, cut away the nail plate at the nail base, then slip a small piece of gauze under the raised flap so pus drains better. If nail removal is needed, usually remove the whole nail (total nail extraction).

## Felon
- The distal pulp space is a closed space: front = subcutaneous fascia, back = periosteum of the distal phalanx, with strong fibrous septa anchoring distal phalanx bone to skin, dividing it into small spaces so the fingertip grips firmly (figure: digital pulp space and fascia septa).
- Felon = closed-space infection of the digital pulp space, usually from penetrating trauma to the fingertip.
- Throbbing pain, swollen fingertip, very painful on pressure; pus close to the skin may cause skin necrosis.
- Treatment: antibiotic plus I & D. Two incision options: longitudinal (mid volar) over the pus or the most tender point — but never let the incision cross the DIP flexor crease (figure: volar longitudinal incision starts 3 to 5 mm from the DIP joint).
  - A longitudinal fingertip incision leaves a scar on the fingertip → pain on touch or pressure.
  - The other is a side incision (unilateral longitudinal incision) to avoid a painful scar, but risks injury to neurovascular bundles.
  - Generally mid volar is preferred over lateral. Then break all digital septa and place a small piece of gauze loosely in the wound.

## Acute Bacterial Tenosynovitis
- Bacterial infection of the flexor tendon synovial sheath, a closed space: infection raises pressure, and synovial fluid feeds organisms → rapid, severe tendon inflammation; the tendon may die, or if not, shortens → finger contracture. An infection a general surgeon must know.
- Entry: direct puncture wound, or spread from fingertip pulp infection.
- Symptoms: fever, painful, swollen, red finger. Exam shows the classic **Kanavel signs** of flexor tendon sheath infection — 4 signs:
  1. Fusiform swelling
  2. Flexion deformity
  3. Severe pain on finger extension
  4. Pain along the flexor tendon
- Low immunity or prior antibiotics → Kanavel signs may be absent.
- Differential diagnosis: septic joint, gout, rheumatoid arthritis, etc.
- Pus can track into the midpalmar space and the radial and ulnar bursae.
- **Treatment** — must drain:
  1. Start antibiotic, watch 12-24 h; not better → open; incision depends on extent of infection.
  2. Tendon and soft tissue still viable, sheath fluid only turbid or purulent → transverse incision at the finger base near the distal palmar crease and at the DIP joint, leave a catheter in the tendon sheath and irrigate with NSS continuously until pus clears (usually ~48 h), then remove; dress the wound and keep the finger moving.
  3. Severe, with dead tendon or soft tissue → open longitudinally along the side of the finger to the web, debride dead tissue, wash repeatedly until healed, then correct finger contracture later; some need amputation of the finger.

## Web Space Infection
- Infection of the web, i.e. the fatty finger webs; swelling spreads the fingers apart. Inflammation spreads through small gaps from one side — usually palmar — to the dorsum, so both palm and dorsum are inflamed and swollen = **collar button abscess**.
- Symptoms: pain on spreading the fingers, and obvious dorsal hand swelling.
- **Treatment**: I & D through both dorsal and palmar incisions, vertical or transverse, keeping a skin bridge ≥ 1 cm between the 2 incisions; pass a penrose drain through to keep the tract from closing. Do not use a single incision across the web space — it causes contracture and the fingers cannot spread.

## Deep Space Infection
- Deep spaces:
  1. Thenar space — between thumb and index finger
  2. Hypothenar space — at the base of the little finger
  3. Midpalmar space — medial to the thenar space; flexor tendons of middle, ring and little fingers lie above it
  4. Radial bursa — sheath of flexor pollicis longus in the hand and wrist
  5. Ulnar bursa — synovial sheath around the flexor tendons of index, middle and ring fingers (as printed); about 80% connect with the radial bursa
  6. Digital sheaths — fibrous sheath over the flexor tendon at the proximal and distal phalanx, and a synovial sheath along the whole finger (if confused, review hand anatomy)
- Deep space infection usually follows:
  1. Web space infection between index-middle, middle-ring and ring-little fingers, spreading from the web space into the lumbrical canal of the hand and on into the midpalmar space
  2. Acute tenosynovitis — a closed-space infection spreading into the deep spaces: thumb into the radial bursa, little finger into the ulnar bursa; web space infection between thumb and index spreads into the thenar space
  3. Penetrating injury into the palm
- **Symptoms**: throbbing pain, marked hand swelling, especially dorsal. Thenar space infection → obvious swelling at the base of thumb and index finger; midpalmar infection → most swelling at the base of the middle finger.
- Drain pus through the web space, or by a transverse incision about 1 cm below the MCP flexion crease.
- Figure: Incisions for hand deep infections — midpalmar space (palmar incision); thenar space (alternate incision along the thenar area); ulnar bursa (along the hypothenar/little finger side and at the ulnar wrist); radial bursa (radial wrist); Parona's space (wrist, ulnar to the palmaris tendon).

## Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS)
- **Anatomy of carpal tunnel** (figure): median nerve and flexor tendons pass through the carpal tunnel, roofed by the transverse carpal ligament over the carpal bones.
- CTS = symptoms from median nerve compression in the carpal tunnel, the canal on the volar side of the wrist roofed by the transverse carpal ligament (also called flexor retinaculum). Causes may be tendon inflammation in the tunnel, or a mass or anything compressing the median nerve.
- **Symptoms**: pain and numbness in the median nerve distribution.
- **Signs** — tests of median nerve compression:
  1. **Phalen's test** — arms raised to shoulder level (or elbows resting on a table), wrists flexed about 1 min; the affected side gets tingling in the thumb, index, middle fingers and radial side of the ring finger on the palmar side. (Figure: raise arms to shoulder level, dorsum of hands together in midline, hold wrists in forced flexion 30-60 s; positive = tingling and numbness over median nerve distribution.)
  2. **Tinel's sign** — tapping at the wrist gives tingling along the median nerve distribution.
- **Treatment**
  1. **Non-operative treatment** — always first: rest the wrist, night splint with the wrist in 20 degrees extension, NSAIDs ("NSADs" as printed). Some inject local steroid to reduce tendon inflammation, but side effects are high, so injection is not recommended.
  2. **Indications for surgery** (printed "forgery") — failed non-operative treatment, usually after about 3-4 months, or pain so bad the patient cannot work. Do **carpal tunnel release**: cut the transverse carpal ligament to relieve median nerve compression; open or endoscopic.

## Carpal Ganglion
- Tumor-like lesion: joint fluid leaks out and forms a cyst, seen as a lump at the wrist. Cause unclear; a well-accepted hypothesis by Angelides: repeated stress on joint, ligament and tendon sheath increases mucin-producing cells, which secrete mucin fluid that gradually dissects out from the joint to form a cyst.
- **Location**: dorsal wrist (60-70%) more than volar; mostly asymptomatic. Diagnosis from history and physical exam, plus transillumination test.
- **Treatment**: first reassure that it is not cancer; if asymptomatic and not bothersome → observation; often resolves spontaneously (30-50%). Squeezing the cyst to burst it, or needle aspiration with or without injection, has high recurrence.
  - Surgery gives the best results with the least recurrence; open or endoscopic approach, under brachial block with tourniquet, dissecting down to the joint capsule (figure: ganglion cyst with its stalk to the joint).
