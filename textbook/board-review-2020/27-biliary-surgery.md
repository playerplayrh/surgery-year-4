## Anatomy and Anomalies
- Biliary system anomalies are found in about 33% overall (detail: Shackelford 2019 p. 1249-1260).

## Gallbladder (GB)
- Sits on the right lobe of the liver, segments 4B and 5; 4 parts:
  1. Fundus
  2. Body
  3. Infundibulum or Hartmann's pouch
  4. Neck
- Between GB and the cystic duct junction lie mucosal folds, old name Valves of Heister — they have no valvular function.
- Common cystic duct anomaly: the cystic duct lies parallel to the CBD and joins it close to the duodenum → easy to injure at operation.
- **Artery supply**: cystic A., a branch of the Rt. hepatic A.; the cystic vein drains into the Rt. branch of the portal vein.
- GB wall thickness ≤ 4 mm; > 4 mm suggests an edematous or inflamed GB.
- **Histology**: read under GB cancer — important for treatment.
- **Hydrops GB**: cystic duct obstruction by stone or tumor; without inflammation, bile in the GB is absorbed but the mucosa keeps secreting glycoprotein and mucus → GB distended with mucus content.

## Common Bile Duct (CBD)
- Length 8-11 cm, width 6 mm.
- 3 parts:
  1. Suprapancreatic
  2. Intrapancreatic
  3. Intraduodenal
- **Width**
  1. On US: should not exceed 6 mm
  2. On CT: should not exceed 8 mm
  3. After previous cholecystectomy: may be up to 1 cm
  4. After CBD obstruction:
     - 4.1 Extrahepatic bile duct dilates in about 2 days
     - 4.2 IHD dilates in 7 days
- CBD has no peristalsis — no smooth muscle like the GB.
- CBD blood supply runs along the length of the duct as axial branches at 3 and 9 o'clock.

## Common Hepatic Duct (CHD)
- Length 1-4 cm, width 4 mm; divides into Rt. and Lt. HD.
- Rt. HD divides into:
  1. Anterior sectoral duct — drains segments 5 and 8
  2. Posterior sectoral duct — drains segments 6 and 7
- Lt. HD divides into medial and lateral.

## Ampulla of Vater
- Opens about 10 cm from the pylorus (printed "pyrolus"); surrounded by circular muscle acting as a valve = sphincter of Oddi.
- **4 parts**
  1. Starts at the junction of CBD and PD, 2 patterns:
     - 1.1 98%: CBD joins PD as a common channel
     - 1.2 2%: separate openings into the duodenum
  2. Surrounded by the sphincter of Oddi
  3. Passes through the duodenal wall — in about 75% CBD and PD join outside the duodenal wall
  4. Ends at the major papilla, covered by duodenal mucosa
- Length of the ampulla within the duodenal wall about 1-2 cm.
- Morphine contracts the sphincter; glucagon relaxes it.

## Sphincter of Oddi
- Made up of:
  1. **Biliary sphincter** or sphincter choledochus
  2. **Pancreatic sphincter** or sphincter pancreaticus
  3. **Ampullary sphincter** or sphincter papillae or sphincter of Oddi — thick coat of circular smooth muscle around the distal CBD at the ampulla of Vater

## Calot's Triangle
- **Boundary**: left = CHD, superior = cystic A., inferior = cystic duct (as printed).

## Duct of Luschka
- Drains bile from the liver directly into the GB.
- Injury to it at cholecystectomy → bile leak (see bile duct injury).

## GB Physiology
- Know only a little:
- Absorbs H2O, Na+, Cl- → concentrated bile.
- Secretes H+ and mucus at the neck and infundibulum.
- **CCK**: most potent GB contraction and relaxes Oddi.
- **Somatostatin and cutting the anterior branch of the Lt. vagus** → GB atony → may later form GS.

## Bile Secretion & Enterohepatic Circulation
- Liver secretes 500-1000 cc bile/day.
- 95% of bile is reabsorbed at the terminal ileum; the liver makes new bile only 5%.
- 5% leaves in feces; a small part leaves in urine as urobilinogen.
- **Bilirubin**: from RBC breakdown; reaches the liver as water-insoluble unconjugated bilirubin bound to protein, especially albumin; the liver conjugates it → water-soluble conjugated bilirubin.

## Investigations in Biliary Diseases
- Many; only those already examined are covered.
- **Oral cholecystography** and **intravenous cholangiography**: no longer work now; US is better and easier.
- **Radionuclide scan or HIDA scan**: IV iminodiacetic acid labelled with Tc, taken up by the liver and excreted in bile → scan shows liver, bile duct, GB. Usable with bilirubin up to 10 mg%; if bilirubin up to 20 mg%, DISIDA scan is better.
  - Normal: liver seen at 5 min, bile duct 15 min, GB 45 min, small bowel within 60 min.
  - GB not seen within 4 h → cystic duct obstruction → HIDA is the **most sensitive test** for acute cholecystitis. But US is far more available, cheaper, and does not need a 4 h wait; if US fits acute cholecystitis, HIDA is not needed to confirm. HIDA still has a role in suspected GB motor dyskinesia.
- **US**: initial study for obstructive jaundice; distal CBD may be unclear due to bowel gas shadow.
- **Endoscopic US (EUS)**: linear US transducer on the tip of an EGD → identifies stones in the lower part (blind spot of transabdominal US); also has a biopsy channel for FNA of tumor or lymph node.
- **MRCP**: accuracy for bile duct and PD equal to ERCP.
  - Advantages
    1. Non-invasive → use before ERCP
    2. No radiation exposure
    3. No contrast
    4. Independent of liver function
    5. Shows proximal bile duct above the obstruction
    6. MRCP with MRI also shows liver abnormalities
  - Disadvantages
    1. Small distal-most CBD not clearly seen
    2. No biopsy → no tissue diagnosis like ERCP; cannot tell stone from cancer (จำ)
    3. Unsuitable for claustrophobia
    4. Cannot be used with clips or pacemaker
- **ERCP**: current role is cases needing both diagnosis and treatment.
  - Advantages
    1. Biopsy of the ampulla
    2. Treatment, e.g. 2.1 stent; 2.2 life-saving procedure such as in toxic cholangitis
  - Disadvantages: opposite of MRCP's advantages; commonest complication post-ERCP pancreatitis (3.5%), then bleeding, perforation, cholangitis.
- **CT-cholangiography (CT-C)**: helical CT, one shot, clear images; hepatitis limits IV contrast use.
- **Choledochoscopy**: scope into the bile duct; find stones to remove.
  - Routes
    1. Per oral — baby scope through the ampulla
    2. Transcystic or trans-CBD during LC
    3. Via PTC — PTBD placed about 2 weeks earlier so the tract matures, then dilate the tract to 16-18 F and pass the scope along it
- **PTC**: small needle through the skin into an intrahepatic duct, inject contrast to see the duct above the obstruction.

## Gallstone (GS) Disease
- **Causative factors** — multifactorial:
  1. **Cholesterol supersaturation**
     - Bile has 3 main components: 1.1 bile salt 80%; 1.2 phospholipid 15%, mostly lecithin; 1.3 cholesterol 5% — water-insoluble; in bile it binds bile salt and phospholipid as **micelles**.
     - When cholesterol exceeds bile salt and lecithin, the balance of the 3 (shown as a triangle by Small) is lost → supersaturated cholesterol precipitates as stone = **Small triangle**.
     - Figure: Small triangle — axes % cholesterol, % bile acids, % phosphatidylcholines; micellar phase only at low cholesterol; higher cholesterol → solid crystals (cholesterol monohydrate) or vesicles and liquid crystals (multilamellar vesicles).
  2. Increased mucin and bilirubin
  3. GB hypomotility or stasis — common in the elderly
  - Note (หมายเหตุ): after OLT GS cannot form — no GB (as printed).
- **Type of GS**
  1. Mixed stone 75%
  2. Pure cholesterol stone 10%
  3. Pigmented stone 15%
- **Cholesterol GS**: common in the West; yellow-white, green-black mixed (oily), smooth, single; mixed stone has > 70% cholesterol mixed with bile pigment and calcium.
- **Pigmented stone**: common in Asian women; from bilirubin precipitation, main component calcium bilirubinate; < 20% cholesterol.
  1. **Black stone**: small, hard, multiple; related to chronic hemolysis and cirrhosis; in the GB; not related to infection and stasis — hemolysis raises bile load which precipitates → black stone is **metabolic in origin**.
  2. **Brown stone**: brown, soft, round; related to infection and stasis; in Asia usually from infection, in the USA from stricture or stone → brown stone often needs biliary drainage.
     - Brown stone is fairly soft, musky, mud-like; contains bacterial colonies, especially E. coli, which raise unconjugated bilirubin until it precipitates as calcium bilirubinate — remember (จำไว้): it is not conjugated bilirubin that precipitates.
- Most GS cause no symptoms; in the elderly symptoms are vague, e.g. bloating, without pain despite stones.
- **Typical biliary symptom**
  1. Severe epigastric or RUQ pain lasting 1-5 h; may refer to the scapula
  2. Pain often at night, especially ½-1 h after a heavy meal
  3. Often with nausea and vomiting
  4. Pain < 1 h or > 24 h → unlikely biliary
- Symptomatic patients should be investigated for GS:
  1. **Plain abdomen**: 10% radiopaque; in Thailand x-ray shows 40% because stones contain more calcium than in Westerners
  2. **US**: works best, sensitivity 95%, specificity 90%
- **Symptomatic GS**: complications 7%/year; complication rate from stones 42-72%; 5-30%/year need surgery; once diagnosed and no contraindication, remove the GB.
- **Asymptomatic or silent GS**
  1. Chance of symptoms 1-3%/year — 10% in 5 years and 20% in 20 years — very low
  2. Does not raise death rate; 80% stay symptom-free until death
  3. Even DM or cirrhosis with silent GS: leave alone; do LC only when symptomatic
- **Indications for prophylactic cholecystectomy in asymptomatic GS** — LC or OC even without symptoms when:
  1. Size > 2.5 cm (some use 3 cm; commonly 2 cm)
  2. Incomplete calcified or porcelain GB (cancer risk 5-6%); complete calcification leaves no mucosa to develop cancer → may observe
  3. With GB polyp
  4. Splenectomy in hemolytic anemia — if GS present, remove the GB as well; diseases in this group:
     - 4.1 Hereditary spherocytosis — GS in 85%
     - 4.2 Sickle cell anemia — GS in 17-70%; 50% of GS become symptomatic in 3-5 years; GS pain cannot be told apart from sickle cell crisis
     - 4.3 Thalassemia — GS in 12-23%
  5. Bariatric surgery — stones common from rapid weight loss (controversial)
  6. Long-term TPN → bile stasis and more sludge
  7. Transplant patients on immunosuppressive drugs — cholecystitis is severe; studies: in heart and lung transplant, LC for silent GS helps, but in kidney and pancreas transplant it does not (as printed, "odd")
  8. No access to medical care
  9. Concurrent abdominal operation for another reason — remove the GB as well, especially:
     - 9.1 Gastric cancer — later cholecystectomy would be difficult
     - 9.2 Hostile abdomen — reopening would be difficult
     - 9.3 Intestinal carcinoid tumor — may need long-term somatostatin analog
  10. Population at high risk of GB cancer (Schwartz)
  11. Pediatric GS
  12. Long common channel of bile duct and PD
  13. Pregnancy — Rush's view: operate, since complications are severe; others advise observe first with diet manipulation
  14. Odd recommendations from Cameron 9th p. 406 (6 items; not in the 12th edition):
     - 14.1 Life expectancy > 20 years (as printed, a joke that no one knows when they will die)
     - 14.2 Radioopaque calculi
     - 14.3 Nonfunctioning GB
     - 14.4 Women < 60 years
     - 14.5 Patients with blood disease needing splenectomy — remove the GB as well even without GS (the author does not really agree)
     - 14.6 Stone size < 3 mm with patent cystic duct
- Caution (ข้อควรระวัง): asymptomatic GS in spinal cord injury — low pain response, so cholecystitis is diagnosed late; but the lifetime chance of needing cholecystectomy is < 6.5% → prophylactic cholecystectomy is not needed.
- DM, cirrhosis with asymptomatic GS: prophylactic cholecystectomy is still not recommended.
- Elective LC: 3 RCTs of 1,437 patients — prophylactic antibiotic not needed, except age > 60, acute cholecystitis, CBD stone, biliary pancreatitis, jaundice, cholangitis, DM, American Society Anes class 3 → give antibiotic. OC → prophylactic antibiotic (reduces SSI).
- **Do not do incidental cholecystectomy in:**
  1. Operation for acute conditions, e.g. PU perforation
  2. Needing an extended or extra incision
  3. High-risk patients who need to finish quickly
  4. Heavily contaminated cases, e.g. colostomy
  5. Terminal cases
- **Nonoperative treatment** — know a little; 2 methods, poor results → rarely used for GB stones now:
  1. **Oral dissolution therapy**: Ursodiol (chenodeoxycholic acid as printed) — lowers cholesterol.
     - Indication: 1.1 small stone < 1 cm; 1.2 pure cholesterol stone; 1.3 functioning GB
     - Take 6-12 months; recurrence after stopping up to 50%.
  2. **Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL)**: breaks stones hoping they pass — did not work.

## Acute Calculous Cholecystitis
- Calculous 95%; acalculous 5%.
- **Cause**: cystic duct obstruction → bile stasis, infection.
- **Symptoms**: RUQ pain, biliary colic, nausea, vomiting, fever, RUQ tenderness, Murphy's sign +ve; diagnosis not hard — no need to memorize Tokyo guideline criteria. Murphy's sign: press RUQ while the patient breathes in → patient stops breathing from pain; press gently or the patient may slap you (as printed).
- **Observations** (ข้อสังเกต)
  1. GB palpable in no more than 1 in 3
  2. Jaundice in acute (as printed "acalculous") cholecystitis is uncommon, ≤ 10%; if jaundiced, think of:
     - 2.1 CBD stone & cholangitis
     - 2.2 Severe sepsis
     - 2.3 Mirizzi syndrome (covered later)
- **Differential diagnosis**: cholangitis, liver abscess, pancreatitis, peptic ulcer, etc.
- **Investigation**
  1. CBC: high WBC & N
  2. LFT: normal or slightly raised
  3. **US**: most useful diagnostic study for acute cholecystitis, sensitivity about 96%; findings:
     - Thick-wall GB > 4 mm
     - Intramural edema
     - Distended GB, thick wall, pericholecystic fluid
     - Linear shadows in the fatty tissue around GB
     - Sonographic Murphy's sign +ve
     - GS or related debris
  4. **CT scan**: for complications such as empyema, perforation, emphysematous cholecystitis, or when US is unclear
  5. **HIDA scan**: the most sensitive test, +ve 98%; rarely used — slow, expensive, limited availability; use when US is equivocal
  6. **MRCP**: better than US or CT; shows cystic duct obstruction and pericholecystic fluid, but expensive → choose when CBD stone is suspected, e.g. high bilirubin or dilated CBD on US
- In practice: signs & symptoms plus upper abdominal US; if they fit acute cholecystitis, treat without confirming by HIDA or CT.
- **Treatment**
  1. Start conservative: NPO, IV, ATB, analgesic such as pethidine or pentazocine. NPO stops GB stimulation; restore the patient; treat comorbidities such as DM.
     - 80% improve. Debate continues on timing of cholecystectomy; Schwartz cites 72 hr — early LC within 72 h if possible: little morbidity, short stay, early return to work.
     - If late > 72 hr or patient not fit: ATB to buy time until better, then elective LC about 2 months later; if symptoms recur meanwhile or during ATB, do LC then.
     - **Poor-condition patient** (ผู้ป่วยแย่ลง) — e.g. ICU patient or acute MI, very high perioperative risk — 2 options:
       - 1.1 **Percutaneous cholecystostomy**: first-line intervention, CT- or US-guided, decompresses and drains infected bile; about 90% improve. Leave the tube 4 weeks for the track to mature, then contrast study: cystic duct patent → remove tube; cystic duct blocked → leave tube in; then LC when fit, usually within 6-8 weeks.
         - Centres with skilled endoscopists may do endoscopic GB drainage: EST then a tube into the GB to drain infected bile via the ampulla (transpapillary drainage), or EUS-guided puncture through stomach or duodenum into the GB with a covered stent as internal drainage.
       - 1.2 If unavoidable, cholecystectomy anyway, e.g. gangrene or perforation of the GB, or conditions like emphysematous cholecystitis — even if the patient is very ill, the surgeon has to operate.
  2. The 72 h belief has faded: RCTs (5 trials of 451 patients) compared early LC (< 1 week after onset of symptoms) with delayed LC (> 6 weeks): even with symptoms up to 7 days, LC is safe, only slightly longer than delayed LC; no difference in operative morbidity (bile duct injury, bile leakage), mortality or conversion rate. But the patient must be fit for anesthesia and, very importantly, the surgeon must be expert — at least 15 LC per year.
     - Advantages of early LC: short stay, early return to work, same mortality; with a skilled surgeon, bleeding and operative time are similar; delayed LC sometimes takes longer due to adhesions.
     - Cameron 9th ed: regardless of days since onset, if fit for OR and anesthetists are not too busy, operate within 24-48 h, more so in elderly, cirrhosis, DM, immunocompromised — high risk of progressing to gangrene or perforation.
     - Summary (สรุป): if the patient can tolerate anesthesia and presents within ... days (as printed, number missing), early cholecystectomy is better than delayed; see Tokyo guideline 2018.
  3. Patient within 72 h but too sick for anesthesia/LC, e.g. myocardial infarction: percutaneous cholecystostomy under local → decompresses the obstructed GB; > 90% improve; then wait until fit before considering surgery. How long to wait is unclear; one follow-up of unfit patients over 2 years found most had no further inflammation.

## Tokyo Guideline 2018
- Japanese HBP surgeons first produced diagnosis and treatment guidelines for acute cholecystitis and acute cholangitis in 2007; the revised treatment guideline familiar to us is **Tokyo guideline 2013 (TG 13)**, grading severity in 3 grades:
  1. Grade 1 (mild): no organ failure, no grade 2 or 3 features → early LC
  2. Grade 2 (moderate): fever, abdominal pain > 3 days, white count > 18,000, tender RUQ mass → delayed LC, except abscess, bile peritonitis or emphysematous cholecystitis → emergency surgery
  3. Grade 3 (severe): shock, organ dysfunction (liver, kidney, lung/heart, brain, hemato) → anesthesia would kill them (as printed); GB drainage only, e.g. percutaneous cholecystostomy; when patient and doctor are ready, delayed LC
- TG 13 was well received — backed by Japanese and international research, practical; the author suggests Thai residents follow TG for both acute cholecystitis and acute cholangitis — new and middle-generation examiners in the Board tend to like it.
- Latest edition is TG 2018; summarised for an idea, useful at HBP conferences.
- **Diagnostic criteria in TG 18** — unchanged from TG 13:

## Summary of Management TG 18 (สรุป)
- **Grade 1**: if fit for anesthesia, TG 18 uses the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) — heart disease, DM, stroke — with the anesthetists' American Society physical status; if no contraindication → early LC.
- **Grade 2**: assess fitness with CCI and ASA physical status (details too many — see tables); if no contraindication and, newly added, the surgeon is experienced → early LC; a surgeon who spends the day on stocks and LINE (as printed) should treat medically first or just do GB drainage.
- **Grade 3**: changed from TG 13 which forbade early LC — now allowed in an advanced center with very skilled surgeons once organ failure has been corrected; for patients looking very poor, just do early/urgent biliary drainage, then delayed LC if they survive.
- **TG 18 management bundles for acute cholecystitis**:
  1. Suspected acute cholecystitis → assess every 6-12 h, US abdomen; CT and HIDA only when needed, especially if US is not certain
  2. Assess severity grading within 24 h together with surgical risk
  3. Immediately initiate antibiotics + supportive care
  4. **Grade 1** (mild): LC within 7 days (within 72 h is better); if treated conservatively, review in 24 h — if worse, 2 options: LC if onset ≤ 7 days, or biliary drainage (cholecystostomy)
  5. **Grade 2** (moderate): experienced surgeon and adequate performance status → urgency/early LC; otherwise urgency/early biliary drainage then delayed LC
  6. **Grade 3** (severe): high surgical risk → urgent/early biliary drainage; early LC acceptable if the patient tolerates it and is in an advanced center
  7. Grades 2, 3: culture blood or bile, or both

## Acute Acalculous Cholecystitis
- About 5%; usually inpatients, not OPD cases.
- Cause unclear, but the 1° predisposing factor most believed is **gallbladder stasis**.
- Observation (ข้อสังเกต): seen in ICU patients with many comorbidities, e.g.:
  1. Hypovolumic shock reducing GB mucosal blood flow — post-trauma, burn, sepsis, multiple organ failure
  2. Ileus and prolonged fasting
  3. Poor nutrition
  4. Multiple blood transfusion
  5. On PEEP
  6. On narcotics
- These ICU patients are intubated on ventilators and cannot complain of abdominal pain → diagnosis is always late → mortality up to 50% from GB gangrene, empyema and perforation.
- Suspect with fever, unexplained sepsis, rising white count, RUQ tenderness (frowning).
- **Diagnosis** is mainly clinical.
- **Investigation** helps confirm:
  1. **US**: bedside; GB wall > 4 mm without stones or sludge; gangrenous GB → air in GB; pericholecystic fluid; CBD usually normal
  2. **HIDA**: nonvisualized GB — most sensitive and specific, but not very practical; use when US gives no answer
  3. **Contrast-enhanced CT or contrast-enhanced MRI**: higher sensitivity and specificity than US — poor contrast enhancement of the GB wall, irregularity or GB wall rupture, gas in the GB wall seen more clearly
- **Treatment**: if the clinical picture fits, the patient is very ill and US shows a thick GB wall → treat at once:
  1. **Cholecystectomy**: if stable enough, LC
  2. **Cholecystostomy**: for high-risk patients unfit for anesthesia, e.g. recent MI; now usually percutaneous, US-guided; about 90% improve; if they survive, cholecystectomy later (no hurry) — so it is wrong that every case of acute acalculous cholecystitis must have cholecystectomy at once.

## Acute Emphysematous Cholecystitis
- GB infection by gas-forming bacteria, usually mixed anaerobe Clostridium perfingen (as printed) and aerobe E. coli.
- Typical patient: frail man, low immunity, diabetic, drowsy, severe sepsis, looking very ill; inflammation spreads fast — GB may perforate or necrose quickly.
- **Classic**: air in the GB wall; plain film shows air in 15%, CT more sensitive; once plain film shows air in the GB wall, no need for US or CT — resuscitate until stable enough for anesthesia, then rush to OR.
- **Treatment**: IV antibiotic + urgent cholecystectomy; cholecystostomy is unsuitable because the GB is usually rotten/necrotic.

## Gallstone Pancreatitis
- 3-4 hypotheses (details not needed); the believed one is the common channel hypothesis: a GS passes from CBD and lodges at the ampulla with the sphincter of Oddi → inflamed, swollen orifice → pancreatic duct obstruction with increased intraductal pressure → pancreatitis; lipase and amylase are usually high.
- Fortunately most stones are small and pass into the bowel → pancreatitis subsides within 2-3 days, by which time amylase should be normal.
- **Typical presentation**: nausea, vomiting, midabdominal pain, high serum lipase, US shows GS or CBD stone, with a soft, swollen pancreas.
- Usually symptoms subside though amylase may still be slightly high; if no contraindication to surgery, do LC + IOC in the same admission — no need to wait 4-6 wk, because without LC the risk of repeat pancreatitis is 30-65% (25% recurrence in 2 weeks, 50% in 3 months) and SAP (severe) about 5% within 30 days.
- **Treatment**: initially as acute pancreatitis (see Pancreas chapter); then decide when to do LC by severity — mild, moderately severe or severe — based on pancreatic morphology on CECT plus organ failure (modified Marshall scoring), local complications (APFC, ANC, PP, WOPN) and systemic complications such as heart and lung (details: revised Atlanta 2013 in Pancreas chapter).
  1. **Mild GS pancreatitis**: abdominal pain, raised amylase or lipase, LFT transiently raised for no more than [number missing as printed] hours; pain soon eases or goes and labs usually normalize, as the GS or sludge passes into the duodenum. No SIRS, no organ failure, no local complications; Ranson < 3, APACHE II < 8; CECT usually not needed — if done shows only interstitial or edematous pancreas; but US is needed to confirm GS. No risk of infection in this grade.
     - Treatment: supportive — NPO, IV, analgesic, antiemetic; urgent ERCP + EST not needed. When pain-free and afebrile, low-fat diet. In mild AP, then judge good risk or poor risk:
     - **Good risk**: once inflammation subsides, operate in the same admission — delayed LC carries recurrent GS pancreatitis about 17% while waiting. Do LC + intraoperative biliary imaging, either IOC or laparoscopic US; preoperative MRCP or ERCP not needed. If IOC shows CBD stone, remove it — 3 options:
       1. Laparoscopy in progress → lap. transcystic duct or lap. CBD exploration
       2. Open surgery → explore CBD, remove stone, place T-tube
       3. Not confident/skilled → refer for scope removal of stone later
     - **Poor risk**: frail, shaky hands, weak voice (as printed) → endoscopic sphincterotomy (ES) to buy time; operate later when the patient is stronger.
  2. **Moderately severe GS pancreatitis**: abdominal pain > 48-72 h not resolving; may have fever, raised white count; pancreatic and liver enzymes stay high; transient SIRS; organ failure < 48 h. Send CECT: interstitial or edematous pancreas, APFC, and a few develop ANC, usually sterile necrosis. Bilirubin > 4 → MRCP to exclude obstructing stone. Low risk of infection in this grade.
     - Treatment: early fluid RLS to keep urine output 0.5-1 cc/kg/hr; organ failure is usually transient; APFC resolves fast. Biliary obstruction → EST; otherwise most moderately severe cases should have LC + IOC before discharge.
  3. **Severe GS pancreatitis**: SIRS persisting 1-2 weeks, organ failure > 48 h, Ranson > 3, APACHE II > 8, CT severity index > 2 — APNC, ANC necrosis > 30%, WON; cholangitis → MRCP. Moderate to high risk of infection in this grade.
     - Treatment: aggressive treatment in ICU, NPO, full IV; antibiotic only with cholecystitis or cholangitis. If improving, treat as moderately severe; if worsening and looking very poor, urgent scope for ES to relieve the obstruction first — once stone or sludge passes the Oddi, the AP improves. Cholecystectomy after SAP: wait at least 2-3 months, with CT to confirm inflammation has truly subsided, then LC + IOC.
- **Role of urgent ERCP + ES**: not routine — about 97% of stones at the ampulla pass by themselves; use only in high risk.
  - Indication
    1. Severe acute GS pancreatitis + obstructive jaundice or cholangitis
    2. Severe case within 72 h (จำ) not responding to aggressive resuscitation with signs of SIRS
    3. Very high risk

## Acute Cholecystitis in Pregnancy
- Second commonest surgical disease in pregnancy after appendicitis.
- GS chance in pregnancy 1-6%; biliary sludge in 31% during pregnancy, about 30% disappears after delivery; pregnancy doubles GB volume and bile becomes cholesterol-supersaturated → GS forms easily.
- Symptomatic GS in pregnancy without LC → 70% recurrence; so LC in the 2nd trimester, the lowest-risk period.
- 1st trimester: beware abortion and teratogenic effect.
- 3rd trimester: beware damage to the uterus and premature labor.
- **Imaging**: MRI, MRCP or US.
- **Treatment**: NPO + IV ATB + analgesic, then by trimester:
  1. 1st trimester: try to delay → LC +/- ERCP in the 2nd trimester
  2. 3rd trimester: try to delay until after delivery
  3. Most respond to conservative treatment; if worse and cannot be left, operate regardless of gestational age — LC best if possible; if too ill, 2-staged approach: US-guided percutaneous cholecystostomy first to buy time, then surgery at a suitable time
- **LC technique in pregnancy**
  1. Open technique for the camera port
  2. Beware pneumoperitoneum — the uterus already compresses the IVC; high pressure compresses it further → venous return to the Rt. heart falls further
  3. Shield the fetus during IOC
  4. Pneumatic compression device to prevent DVT from venous stasis

## Gallstone Pancreatitis in Pregnancy
- Presents as in non-pregnant patients; most stones pass into the small bowel; if stable, LC in the 2nd trimester or after delivery.
- 1st trimester: delay to the 2nd trimester.
- 3rd trimester: wait until after delivery; if cannot wait, OC rather than lap. — in the 3rd trimester the large uterus is in the way; save the mother first, the baby can go into an incubator as preterm (as printed).
- Stone impacted at the ampulla → endoscopic treatment: balloon dilation or ES to remove the stone, then LC later; shield the fetus for any x-ray.

## Mirizzi Syndrome
- Uncommon complication of GS, first reported in 1948.
- GS impacted at Hartmann's pouch or cystic duct → inflammation; the stone compresses the CHD → partial or complete bile duct obstruction, and pressure necrosis → cholecystobiliary fistula.
- Incidence 0.4-1.4%; age 45 up, fairly elderly; associated with GB cancer 25%.
- Symptoms: RUQ pain, jaundice, nausea, vomiting; fever in 25%; some present with recurrent episodes of jaundice and cholangitis.
- **US**: best screening method — GS, distended GB, bile duct dilation above the obstruction while the CBD below the stone is normal size → suspect Mirizzi.
- **MRC**: helps diagnosis, shows biliary anatomy and classifies the Mirizzi type.
- **McSherry classification** (coarse):
  1. **Type 1**: GS impacted at Hartmann's pouch compresses the CHD → obstructive jaundice
  2. **Type 2**: stone erodes the CHD → fistula between GB and CHD
- **Csendes classification** — 5 types, splitting type 2 by fistula size:
  1. **Type 1**: as McSherry
  2. **Type 2**: cholecysto-choledochal fistula involving < 1/3 of CBD circumference
  3. **Type 3**: fistula > 1/3 but < 2/3 of CBD circumference
  4. **Type 4**: fistula with complete destruction of the CBD wall (whole circumference)
  5. **Type 5**: GB-CBD fistula plus a fistula between GB and bowel (cholecystoduodenostomy); 5a = no gallstone ileus, 5b = with gallstone ileus
- Figure: Mirizzi stages — chronic cholecystitis (no type); external compression of CHD = McSherry I / Csendes I; cholecystocholedochal fistula = McSherry II / Csendes II, III, IV.
- Preoperative diagnosis is fairly hard — known before surgery in no more than 62%; these cases are sticky and difficult; inexperienced surgeons beware — bile duct injury about 17%.
- **Treatment**: although LC has been reported in Mirizzi type 1, overall the operation is usually open, because Calot's anatomy is badly distorted by inflammation.
- **Intraoperative diagnosis**: > 50% of Mirizzi syndrome is diagnosed at operation; suspicious findings:
  1. Shrunken GB with distorted anatomy
  2. Dilated GB with thick walls and a large stone or multiple stones
  3. Impacted GS at GB neck or infundibulum
  4. An obliterated Calot's triangle
  5. Dense fibrotic mass at Calot's triangle
  6. Dense adhesions at subhepatic space
- **Operative approach**
  1. **Type 1**: top-down or fundus-downward cholecystectomy; if inflammation is severe and Calot's structures cannot be identified, play safe — partial cholecystectomy (Strasberg: subtotal cholecystectomy): remove the inflamed anterior part, leave a little GB on the liver bed, and suture the cystic duct opening from inside the GB.
  2. **Types 2, 3, 4**: the GB usually allows only partial cholecystectomy; the problem is the fistula — decide by its size:
     - 2.1 Small or medium (no mm cut-off given; roughly fits a T-tube): partial cholecystectomy, then use the remaining GB to close the opening with a T-tube as stent; or, if the whole GB can be removed, put the T-tube into the fistula — both allow T-tube cholangiogram. Another way: close the fistula with the GB remnant left after partial cholecystectomy, then bypass.
     - 2.2 Large or circumferential destruction of the CHD (type 4): may need bypass, e.g. Roux en Y hepaticojejunostomy.
     - 2.3 Patient unfit for surgery, e.g. very high risk: biliary stent first, then remove stone with basket or ESWL.
     - 2.4 Do not forget F/S at the fistula — rule out GB cancer invading the CBD.
  3. **Type 5**: 5a — manage the GB, usually subtotal cholecystectomy, then close the bowel opening; 5b — treat as gallstone ileus.

## Gallstone Ileus
- Mechanical intestinal obstruction: GS with GB inflammation and long-standing adhesion to nearby organs → stone erodes into the duodenum → fistula, stone passes into the GI tract (biliary-enteric fistula). GS ≥ 2.5 cm usually lodges at the terminal ileum at the IC valve.
- Usually women about 70 years, obese; history of biliary colic 1-2 days before, then partial or complete small bowel obstruction, with no previous abdominal operation and no hernia; history of GS-related symptoms in about 30-50%.
- **Plain film**: **Rigler triad** (20-50%):
  1. Small bowel dilatation with air-fluid levels
  2. Radio-opaque lesion in the RLQ — about 20%
  3. Most important: air in GB or biliary tree (aerobilia) — 40%
- **CT**: sensitivity up to 93%; besides diagnosing the cause of SBO, shows where the stone lodges; about 12.5% have more than one stone in the bowel; also shows the hepatobiliary system.
- **Aerobilia** on plain film → think of:
  1. GS ileus
  2. Mirizzi syndrome type 2, 3, 4
  3. Previous endoscopic sphincterotomy or biliary-enteric bypass
- **Treatment**: once diagnosed, operate — GS > 2.5 cm cannot pass the IC valve.
  1. Midline incision; run the whole small bowel to see how many stones are lodged.
  2. Do not open the bowel at the impaction site (bowel wall is usually bruised); milk the stone back proximally to an unaffected segment, longitudinal enterotomy about 30 cm above the obstruction, remove the stone, close the bowel transversely.
  3. If the bowel is gangrenous, resect.
  4. GB and fistula: usually not done at the 1st operation — patients are elderly, obese, with many comorbidities, and it is an emergency → two-staged approach preferred.
- Controversy: whether the 2nd stage is needed — only about 10% develop acute cholecystitis needing interval cholecystectomy, 70% of fistulas close spontaneously, cancer risk is not significantly increased, and most patients are elderly and frail, often dying before recurrence.
- **Greenfield**: lifetime recurrent GS ileus only 5-10%; recurrences usually within 30 days of the first episode, believed to be missed stones left in the bowel at the initial operation, not stones from the GB → one-stage surgery alone is not wrong.
- The author still advises: in younger or fit patients, interval cholecystectomy and fistula closure — until better data appear.
- **Bouveret syndrome**: GS lodges at the pylorus or 1st part of duodenum (Bouveret, 1896) → non-bilious vomiting without abdominal distension = gallstone ileus presenting as duodenal obstruction; film: RUQ opacity, distended stomach, very little bowel gas.
- Treatment: try endoscopic retrieval first; if it fails, laparotomy — push the stone back into the stomach, open the stomach and remove it; that is all for the first step. Later prepare the patient for cholecystectomy and fistula closure.
- In truth this is not ileus — a misnomer; better called GS-related small bowel obstruction (SBO).

## Biliary Dyskinesia
- Functional motility disorder of the GB → delayed emptying without GS or sludge.
- Another disease in this group: sphincter of Oddi dysfunction (SOD) → obstruction of biliary or pancreatic secretion.
- **Symptoms**: 15% have typical biliary colic or RUQ pain 30-60 min after meals, fatty food intolerance, but no GS. Before blaming this disease, exclude infectious causes such as viral hepatitis, CMV, typhoid, and IBD, primary sclerosing cholangitis, SLE, pancreatitis, gastritis and sphincter of Oddi dysfunction.
- Diagnosis now uses Rome IV criteria — too detailed, not worth knowing.
- US usually normal; CT not helpful; LFT, amylase, EGD all normal.
- **Diagnosis**: after excluding other diseases, confirm with **cholecystokinin (CCK)-Tc HIDA scan**: HIDA scan first; once isotope fills the GB, IV CCK, wait about 20 min, then calculate GB ejection fraction. GBEF < 38% is abnormal; GBEF < 10% — the lower the value, the better LC works.
- **Treatment**: select patients well → LC; 85-94% become pain-free after GB removal — "No GB... No Pain"; those still in pain → think of post-cholecystectomy syndrome.

## Post Cholecystectomy Syndrome
- Nothing is 100% in medicine; even typical biliary colic should go after GB removal, but persistent or new pain may be from:
  1. Misdiagnosis, e.g. pain from PU, GERD, chronic pancreatitis, or correct diagnosis but 2 coexisting diseases — this cause up to 70%
  2. Retained stone in CBD
  3. Long cystic duct stump — (as printed, from the author's memory) stump > 1 cm may hold a stone causing pain
  4. Papillary dysfunction or stenosis
  5. Underlying biliary malignancy
  6. Bile duct injury, stricture
- Frank G. Moody believed post-cholecystectomy syndrome occurs in up to 20% — possibly in the past; today the author thinks ≤ 10% (Moody wrote the section in Schwartz 11e 2019; RIP).
- Approach: history, examination, new labs, US as baseline, then investigate by suspected cause — PU or GERD → EGD; retained CBD stone → CT, MRCP, EUS, etc.

## Common Bile Duct Stones
- **Type of CBD stone**
  1. Primary CBD stone: forms in the CBD; much less common than 2°; seen in Asia
  2. Secondary CBD stone: most CBD stones come from the GB; more in USA than Asia
- **Primary CBD stone**: high bilirubin, low cholesterol; associated with bile duct stricture, stasis and chronic bile duct infection.
- **Criteria for diagnosing 1° CBD stone (Saharia)**
  1. Previous cholecystectomy with or without CBD exploration
  2. No biliary symptoms for at least 2 years after surgery
  3. Brown, soft, mud-like, easily crumbled stone
  4. No long cystic duct or bile duct stricture from the previous operation
- **Secondary CBD stone**: passed from the GB — cholesterol or black pigmented stone — into the CBD.
  1. 8-18% of symptomatic GS have a stone in the CBD
  2. 6% of cholecystectomy patients have CBD stone (จำ)
  3. 25% of CBD stones are in the elderly > 60 years with symptomatic GS
- Peak age > 60 years; found about equally in acute and chronic cholecystitis.
- Bile duct stones may be silent — floating stones in the CBD, or small stones that pass into the bowel.
- Because CBD stone complications (obstructive jaundice, cholangitis, acute pancreatitis) can be fatal, even asymptomatic CBD stones should be treated if there is no contraindication to surgery or procedure — not like silent GS.
- Presentation ranges from none to abdominal pain, jaundice, cholangitis, obstructive jaundice with pale stools — beware:
  1. **CBD stone**: may cause intermittent jaundice
  2. **Underlying malignancy**, especially periampullary: jaundice usually progressive
- Investigation — 2 phases:
  1. Preoperative: non-invasive — US, MRC; invasive — endoscopic US, ERC
  2. Intraoperative: IOC and laparoscopic US
- Principles of choosing investigation:
  1. Do it before surgery — do not hunt for stones in the OR
  2. Preoperatively, choose non-invasive first

## Algorithm for Management of Patients with Symptomatic CBD Stones
- Estimates the chance of CBD stone to guide choice of investigation and treatment; many consensus guidelines exist; the popular one is from The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE).
- Routine MRCP, CT-C or ERCP for all symptomatic GS to look for CBD stone is overkill and costly → selective use. Over ten years ago, criteria of Liu, Consorti and Kawashima (Sabiston 17th p. 1618) were used; although dropped from Sabiston 18th and 19th, Liu's guideline is the starting point of current guidelines — summary:
  1. Clinically clear, e.g. cholangitis, US CBD ≥ 5 mm and at least 2 of 4 abnormal LFT (total bilirubin ≥ 1.5, alkaline phosphatase ≥ 150, AST ≥ 100, ALT ≥ 100) → CBD stone chance up to 93% → no MRCP; send for therapeutic ERCP
  2. Cholecystitis or pancreatitis only, US CBD ≥ 5 mm, at least 2 of 4 abnormal LFT → CBD stone 32% → MRCP first
  3. Cholecystitis or pancreatitis, US CBD < 5 mm, at least 2 of 4 abnormal LFT → CBD stone only 4% → LC + IOC
  4. Biliary colic only, US CBD < 5 mm, LFT abnormal 2 of 4 (as printed; figure shows T Bili < 1.5, Alk phos < 150, AST < 100, ALT < 100) → CBD stone only 1% → LC alone is enough
- Figure: algorithm as steps —
  1. Choledocholithiasis + CBD ≥ 5 mm + ≥ 2 abnormal LFT → therapeutic ERCP → LC with IOC (risk of CBD stones 93%).
  2. Cholecystitis/pancreatitis/resolving choledocholithiasis + CBD ≥ 5 mm + ≥ 2 abnormal LFT → MRC → CBD stones → therapeutic ERCP → LC with IOC; no CBD stones → LC with IOC (risk 32%).
  3. Cholecystitis/pancreatitis/resolving choledocholithiasis + CBD < 5 mm + ≥ 2 abnormal LFT → LC with IOC (risk 4%).
  4. Biliary colic + CBD < 5 mm + T Bili < 1.5, Alk phos < 150, AST < 100, ALT < 100 → LC (risk 1%).
- CBD stone in Schwartz 11e: as printed, the author says it is poorly written and not reliable.
- Likewise Shackelford 2019 p.1287 — the guideline below looks too old, no longer current (as printed).
- Figure: Shackelford algorithm for symptomatic cholelithiasis ± choledocholithiasis, as steps —
  1. Debilitated or elderly patient, or inexperienced laparoscopist → ERC/ES → follow.
  2. Jaundice, cholangitis, dilated CBD, elevated LFTs, severe pancreatitis → ERC/ES → laparoscopic cholecystectomy IOC/LUS.
  3. Otherwise → laparoscopic cholecystectomy IOC/LUS → stones → transcystic extraction → stones remain → postoperative ERC/ES, or CBDE/T-tube → stones → remove via T-tube; retained stones after LC → ERC/ES.
  4. Laparoscopy contraindicated → open cholecystectomy IOC → stones → open CBDE T-tube (stones → remove via T-tube; no stones → remove T-tube) or drainage procedure.
- Most popular: **American Society of GI Endoscopy (ASGE) 2010** guideline for CBD stones, often cited by bile-duct enthusiasts; 3 groups by likelihood of CBD stone:

| Predictor strength | Predictors |
|---|---|
| Very strong | CBD stone on trans-abdominal US; clinical ascending cholangitis; bilirubin > 4 mg/dl |
| Strong | Dilated CBD on US (> 6 mm); bilirubin > 1.8-4 mg/dl |
| Moderate | Abnormal liver biochemical test other than bilirubin; age > 55; clinical gallstone pancreatitis |

  1. **High risk** (stone chance > 50%): preoperative ERCP + ES
  2. **Intermediate risk** (10-50%): MRCP / EUS first; if CBD stone found, remove it, then LC — what most surgeons do; alternatively LC + intraoperative laparoscopic IOC or lap US as an option
  3. **Low risk** (< 10%): LC + IOC (no further evaluation)
- April 2019 (as printed, a joke about "lavender scent") the ASGE revised the CBD stone guideline — new, the author likes it:

| 2019 ASGE risk | Criteria | Management |
|---|---|---|
| High | CBD stone visualized on imaging, or cholangitis, or total bilirubin > 4 mg/dL and dilated CBD | Go to ERCP |
| Intermediate | Abnormal LFTs, or age > 55, or dilated CBD (> 6 mm with GB in situ) | EUS, MRCP, laparoscopic IOC, or intraoperative US |
| Low | None of above (symptomatic cholelithiasis without any of these risk factors) | Cholecystectomy with/without IOC or intraoperative US |

- In moderate/intermediate risk, GS pancreatitis was removed — most stones pass spontaneously. In high risk, TB 1.8-4 + CBD dilated (or TB > 4 alone) changed to TB > 4 + CBD dilated. Treat as per the table.
- Schwartz 2019 p.1407 (broad): GS with US CBD > 8 mm, biliary pain, jaundice → think CBD stone; if in question → MRCP.
- **Management of CBD stone** depends on:
  1. When the CBD stone is found — before surgery, during LC, or after surgery
  2. CBD size; number, size and position of stones
  3. Patient low risk or high risk
  4. Surgeon's experience
  5. Hospital capability, e.g. availability of ERCP
- **GS and CBD stone found before surgery** — 4 options:
  1. ERC first, clear CBD stone by endoscopic sphincterotomy, then elective LC when ready. Prophylactic LC reduces mortality; some wait only 24-48 h to let the ERC air subside, then LC. Without LC: biliary events 20% within 6 weeks, 47% in 2 years, and about 5% cholangitis with death. Even after preop ERC+ES, do IOC at LC. This route is the most favoured, but needs 2 admissions (2 stage).
  2. LC + IOC with lap. stone removal — needs a skilled surgeon.
  3. LC + IOC, if it fails convert to open CBD exploration.
  4. Open cholecystectomy + IOC + open CBD exploration — last choice; in Thailand lap. CBD exploration instruments are scarce and it is difficult and needs skill, so open is not wrong.
  5. Previous gastrectomy with B2 or Roux en Y: in the past ERC+ES was not done; Shackelford 2013 advises trying ERC+ES first, and if it fails remove stones by lap.
- **CBD stone found during LC**
  1. Remove by lap. transcystic or lap. CBD exploration (LCBDE)
  2. Open and explore CBD if lap. is not available or experience is limited
  3. Postop. ES to remove the stone
  4. Stone ≤ 4 mm and IOC shows contrast passing into the duodenum → try IV glucagon then irrigate CBD with NSS; without distal obstruction the stone should pass
  5. Intraoperative per oral sphincterotomy by endoscope in the OR during LC — fairly hard: patient must be turned from supine to left lateral; whoever can do it, go ahead
- **CBD stone found after LC** — check whether a T-tube is in place:
  1. T-tube present → wait 4-6 wk for the tract to mature, then T-tube cholangiogram and Dormia basket stone removal under fluoroscopy
  2. No T-tube → ERCP + ES
  3. Still not removed → percutaneous transhepatic approach +/- ESWL to fragment the stone
  4. Open re-operation — last resort: adhesions, difficult, very tiring

## Endoscopic CBD Stone Clearance
- Skilled hands: duct clearance rate 90-96%.
- Failures usually with stone impaction, history of gastrectomy, Roux-en-Y anatomy, periampullary diverticula, Mirizzi syndrome.
- Complications: pancreatitis 5-10%, duodenal perforation and bleeding.

## CBD Exploration (CBDE)
- Though less done now, residents should know open CBD exploration technique and answer confidently; laparoscopic CBDE is advanced lap for expert surgeons — know it only as an idea for exams.
- **Principles of open CBDE** (also apply to lap.)
  1. Avoid CBDE in a duct < 5 mm — do not do CBDE if CBD < 3 mm (as printed) — high bile duct stricture risk; stones in a small CBD should be removed by ES, or by lap. transcystic duct approach
  2. CBD stones < 3 mm usually pass into the bowel spontaneously
  3. IOC before opening the CBD every time — confirms position and number of stones and is a roadmap of ductal anatomy
  4. Open the CBD longitudinally on its anterior side, incision about 1-2 cm; not transverse or on the side — cuts the CBD blood supply → stricture. Open as close to the duodenum as possible, in case bypass is needed. After opening, irrigate with NSS downward, pass a balloon catheter through the sphincter into the duodenum and pull back gently; same upward. After clearing stones, complete examination with choledochoscope or IOC — most experts advise both
  5. Remember: avoid forceful manipulation — never answer stone forceps or Bake dilator in orals; the examiner will grill you, as they are rigid and injure the CBD and sphincter; use Fogarty biliary catheter instead
  6. NSS flushing with a red rubber or Robinson catheter: be careful flushing upward — may push the stone up into the liver where removal is hard
- **Open CBDE technique** (วิธีทำ)
  1. Pre-explore IOC
  2. Complete Kocherization, then feel with your expert hand whether it is stone or tumor, especially in the intrapancreatic part
  3. If the CBD is not clear, do not open blindly — aspirate with a needle first; bile confirms it
  4. Open the CBD above the duodenum with stay sutures
  5. Flush CBD with NSS by red rubber catheter — stones usually come out; if not, biliary balloon catheter
  6. Choledochoscope up and down to make sure all stones are out
  7. Repeat IOC
  8. The author prefers a T-tube > 14 F, for:
     - 8.1 Sphincter of Oddi spasm or edema → without T-tube, bile leak for sure
     - 8.2 Route for treating retained stones later
     - 8.3 Place the T-tube with a straight, shortest tract, perpendicular to the CBD
  9. Close the CBD with nonabsorbable (the author uses chromic 4-0, as printed), interrupted in a watertight fashion
  10. T-tube to a bag for gravity drainage
- **Cautions** (ข้อควรระมัดระวัง)
  1. T-tube may not be needed routinely if the sphincter of Oddi mechanism is normal, prior EST, a stent is already in place lower down, or remaining stones can be retrieved endoscopically
  2. Again: never answer stone forceps or Bake — you will surely fail (as printed)

## Laparoscopic CBD Stone Clearance
- 2 methods: transcystic CBDE, and CBDE by opening the CBD directly.
- **Transcystic CBDE**.
- **Trans CBD exploration**: open the CBD below the cystic duct junction.

**Laparoscopic Transcystic CBDE Versus Laparoscopic Choledochotomy**

| | Transcystic | Choledochotomy |
|---|---|---|
| Stone number | < 8 | Any |
| Stone size | < 9 mm | Any |
| Stone location | Distal to insertion of cystic duct | Entire duct |
| Bile duct size | Any | > 6 mm |
| Drain | Cystic duct tube (optional) | T-tube (not routine) |
| Advantages | 1. No T-tube 2. Short hospital stay | T-tube for PO. access |
| Contraindications | 1. Friable cystic duct 2. IHD stone or proximal stone 3. Multiple large stones | 1. Small diameter duct 2. Inability to secure laparoscopically |

- Lap. CBD exploration: routine T-tube was once advised for assessing the biliary system and removing retained stones, but it has drawbacks — bile leak and late stricture; comparisons of T-tube vs none show no difference in complication rates, morbidity & mortality → use selectively, not routinely.
- After stone removal, completion IOC or choledochoscopy again.
- Operator should have experience as first surgeon in > 25 LC; someone who only holds or wipes the camera should not attempt it — the patient's CBD will surely be wrecked; open is advised instead (as printed).
- Laparoscopic CBD stone clearance rate is as good as endoscopic clearance.

## Indication for Explore CBD
- Residents should know this, especially for orals — senior examiners ask the chance of finding stones for each indication:
  1. Palpable stone — 98%
  2. IOC +ve — 95%
  3. Jaundice & cholangitis — 50%
- **Relative indications**
  1. Biliary pancreatitis
  2. CBD dilation
  3. Abnormal LFT

## Intraoperative Cholangiogram (IOC)
- Routine IOC finds unsuspected CBD stone in only 4%, and stones < 3 mm are not clinically significant as they pass → routine IOC to find CBD stones is not justified. Routine IOC to prevent bile duct injury is also unsupported by RCTs → for that purpose use IOC in complicated cases such as pancreatitis, cholecystitis, jaundice, or unclear anatomy only. Conclusion: IOC is not standard of care.
- **Indication for IOC**
  1. Preoperative abnormal LFT, e.g. AST, ALT, ALP, bilirubin
  2. Unclear anatomy during LC
  3. Suspicion of intraoperative injury to the biliary tract
  4. Dilated CBD on preoperative imaging
  5. GS pancreatitis without endoscopic clearance of CBD
  6. Jaundice
  7. Large CBD size with small CBD stone
  8. Multiple small GS with large cystic duct
  9. Unsuccessful preoperative ERCP
- **Open IOC technique**
  1. Transgallbladder — needle into GB, aspirate bile, inject contrast; not with cystic duct obstruction
  2. Transcystic duct
  3. Transcommon bile duct — needle no. 25, aspirate bile from CBD, then inject contrast
- **Contrast**
  1. Urograffin 20 cc in 50 cc water, or concentration < 30%, so contrast does not hide stones; check for stones and number; then contrast:water 1:1 (stronger) to show bile duct anatomy
  2. Inject twice:
     - 2.1 First: about 5-10 cc; with a large CBD, 10 cc — aimed at the distal CBD
     - 2.2 Second: the rest; to see proximal ducts or IHD clearly, put the head down
- **Tips** (ข้อแนะนำ)
  1. Position: supine head-down, tilted to the right
  2. Large film: upper edge at the nipple line, lower edge at the navel
  3. Beware air bubbles when injecting
  4. During filming keep injecting slowly; ask the anesthetist to pause the ventilator so the patient is still
  5. No narcotic or morphine during injection — causes sphincter of Oddi spasm
- **Acceptable IOC**
  1. No filling defect; whole bile duct clear; IHD seen in every segment
  2. Contrast flows freely into the duodenum, no bubbles

## Indication for Biliary Enteric Drainage
1. 3rd operation
2. 1° CBD stone
3. Unable to clear all stones, e.g. in IHD, or stricture of the distal CBD or sphincter
5. CBD > 2 cm (numbering skips 4 as printed)
6. Now the key indication for bypass: stones cannot be removed endoscopically
- **Operative options** by CBD size:
  1. CBD < 1.5 cm → transduodenal sphincteroplasty
  2. CBD > 1.5 cm → choledochoduodenostomy or choledochojejunostomy
- Some say CBD stones > 5 → retained CBD stone after surgery > 80% → consider bypass at once; the author thinks the data cited are too old.

## Retained or Recurrent CBD Stone
- **Retained CBD stone**: CBD stone found within 6 months after LC (some use 2 years — no point arguing, treatment is the same).
  - Causes
    1. IHD stones dropping down
    2. Poor IOC technique
    3. IOC misread — thought to be air bubble or blood clot
- **Recurrent CBD stone**: found > 6 months after LC.
- **Spontaneous passage**: possible for stones < 3 mm, or in patients with a biliary stent left at the ampulla, especially > 6 months — the stone rubs against the stent and breaks into small pieces that pass, since 1° CBD stones are soft and crumble easily.
- **Treatment of retained CBD stone**: choose non-reoperative methods first; key question — is a T-tube still in place?
  1. **T-tube present**: nothing needed in the first 6 wk — 10-20% of stones break and pass, but most do not; then remove:
     - 1.1 Extraction through the T-tube — Dormia basket over guide wire or via choledochoscope; success 88-97%.
       - Failure causes: 1. impacted CBD stone; 2. stone in an unreachable spot, e.g. cystic duct; 3. T-tube tract < 16F even after dilation, or tract tortuous; 4. large stone that cannot be broken
     - 1.2 Endoscopic sphincterotomy (ES): second line if T-tube route fails; in centres with expert ERCP, no need to wait 6 weeks — go straight to ERCP.
  2. **No T-tube**: endoscopic sphincterotomy (ES) or balloon dilation of the sphincter first; expert endoscopists clear stones in 90-98%.
     - ERCP shows CBD stones → EST and clear all stones; very large stones → fragment first with ESWL, laser or EHL.
  3. Open re-operation — last resort.
- **Tips** (ข้อแนะนำ)
  1. Leaving the T-tube in for a long time hoping the stone breaks and passes rarely works in practice — the patient carries the tube, it is a nuisance, and bile is lost daily. Summary: if the stone is still there at 6 wk, find a way to remove it.
  2. CBD stone < 1 cm or young patient → may try balloon sphincteroplasty (balloon dilation of the papilla) first.
  3. Percutaneous transhepatic stone removal suits stones in the IHD better.
- **Treatment of recurrent CBD stones**: as for retained stone without T-tube — endoscopic removal.

## Difficult Bile Duct Stones
- Stones that are tough to deal with; the treating doctor should be experienced; new, unskilled hands should avoid them and refer to a senior who enjoys a challenge (as printed).
  1. **Size**: > 1.5 cm — hard to extract, baskets break; or > 3 stones — also difficult
  2. **Location** — difficult types:
     - 2.1 Impacted stone at the ampulla
     - 2.2 Stone above a biliary stricture
     - 2.3 All IHD stones
     - 2.4 Retained or recurrent bile duct stone
  3. **Anatomical abnormalities**
     - 3.1 Congenital, e.g. biliary stricture, Calori disease (as printed) or choledochal cyst
     - 3.2 Acquired, e.g. previous liver or GB surgery with dense adhesions, or previous gastrectomy with B2 — scope access to the stone is hard
  4. **Pregnancy**: large uterus makes everything hard (as printed, "don't think dirty"); x-ray IOC is also troublesome

## Impacted CBD Stones
- Firmly stuck stones that cannot be pulled out; 5 approaches:
  1. ERCP + ES
  2. Laparoscopic CBD exploration
  3. Open CBD exploration
  4. Transduodenal sphincterotomy
  5. Biliary enteric bypass
- ES or lap. CBDE may need ESWL, EHL or laser to fragment the stone; IV glucagon relaxes the papilla so small stones may pass.
- Fragmenting: the probe tip must touch only the stone — never the CBD; remember, handle the bile duct gently.

## Acute Cholangitis
- Medical-student-level knowledge; for exams, expect toxic or suppurative cholangitis — about 15% of acute cholangitis is severe form; common in elderly women.
- **Pathophysiology** — 2 causes:
  1. Bacteria in bile (bactibilia)
  2. Raised intraductal pressure
- **Basic**: bile is normally sterile; bacteria may enter the CBD but the body clears them by 5 mechanisms:
  1. Bile always flows down into the bowel
  2. Sphincter of Oddi closes the lower end against bacterial reflux
  3. Tight hepatocyte junctions — bacteria cannot pass quickly (as printed, likened to driving on a bumpy road)
  4. Kupffer cells in hepatic sinusoids catch and eat bacteria as a snack (as printed)
  5. IgA and bile salt in bile are bactericidal
- Bacteria in bile deconjugate bilirubin and hydrolyse phospholipid → sludge or stones.
- **Risk factors of bactibilia**
  1. Age > 70 years
  2. Clinical jaundice, especially CBD obstruction
  3. CBD stone
  4. Acute cholecystitis
  5. Surgery within 1 month after resolution of acute cholecystitis
  6. Previous biliary tract surgery, e.g. biliary enteric bypass
  7. Fever or chills within 1 week after surgery
  8. Emergency biliary surgery
  9. Current GI infection
- **Bacteria**
  1. Aerobe: 1st E. coli, 2nd Klebsiella
  2. Anaerobe: uncommon; formerly B. fragilis thought 1st, but Schwartz now says Cl. Perfingen (as printed) is more common
- **Biliary pressure**
  1. Normal 7-14 cmH2O
  2. Pressure > 15 → bacteria and endotoxin in bile are pushed into the circulation → sepsis
  3. Pressure > 30 → toxic cholangitis for sure
  4. Higher pressure → less antibiotic enters bile; pressure > 25 → antibiotic cannot enter the bile duct at all — the key reason for urgent biliary decompression
- **Clinical presentation**
  1. **Charcot's triad** (Jean Martin Charcot 1877): fairly low sensitivity; most (60-70%) have 2/3 signs
     - 1.1 Fever 95%
     - 1.2 RUQ pain 90%
     - 1.3 Jaundice 80%
  2. **Reynold's and Dargan pentad** — suppurative cholangitis: Charcot's triad plus 2 more:
     - 2.1 Hypotension
     - 2.2 Alteration of conscious(ness)
- **Etiology**: now 1st is biliary tract manipulation, e.g. CBD stone removal or stent obstruction; then bile duct obstruction from CBD stone and cancer.
- **Diagnosis**
  1. Mainly clinical; imaging confirms — US usually the initial study; MRCP helps a lot, sensitivity and specificity equal to ERCP, but MRCP diagnoses only, cannot treat
  2. Gold standard: ERCP (one best) and PTC, as they also treat:
     - 2.1 ERCP for stones or lower lesions, e.g. periampullary lesion
     - 2.2 PTC for hilar lesion — place PTBD

## Tokyo Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Cholangitis
- Reported in Hepatobiliary Pancreato Surg 25: 31-40; latest update 2018.
- Helps diagnosis and treatment planning for both acute cholecystitis and acute cholangitis.

| TG18 / TG13 diagnostic criteria for acute cholangitis | |
|---|---|
| A. Systemic inflammation | A-1 Fever and/or shaking chills; A-2 Laboratory evidence of inflammatory response |
| B. Cholestasis | B-1 Jaundice; B-2 Laboratory: abnormal liver function tests |
| C. Imaging | C-1 Biliary dilatation; C-2 Evidence of the etiology on imaging (stricture, stone, stent etc) |
| Suspected diagnosis | One item in A + one item in either B or C |
| Definite diagnosis | One item in A, one in B and one in C |

- A-1 fever = temperature > 38 °C.
- A-2 = WBC > 10,000 or < 4,000, or C-reactive protein > 1 mg/dl.
- B-2 = total bilirubin ≥ 2 mg/dl and AST, ALT, ALP > 1.5 × upper limit.
- C = imaging (US, CT, MRI/MRCP, EUS) showing bile duct dilatation, or stone or cancer.
- After diagnosis, grade severity — 3 grades:
- **Grade III** (severe acute cholangitis): dysfunction in at least 1 of 6 organs/systems:
  1. Hypotension requiring dopamine ≥ 5 µg/kg per min, or any dose of norepinephrine
  2. Disturbance of consciousness
  3. PaO2/FiO2 ratio < 300
  4. Oliguria, serum creatinine > 2.0 mg/dl
  5. PT-INR > 1.5
  6. Platelet count < 100,000/mm³
  - Grade III → resuscitate and urgent biliary decompression as soon as possible.
- **Grade II** (moderate acute cholangitis): at least 2 of 5:
  1. WBC > 12,000 or < 4,000
  2. Fever ≥ 39 °C
  3. Age ≥ 75 years
  4. Total bilirubin ≥ 5 mg/dl
  5. Albumin < lower limit of normal × 0.7
  - Grade II → IV antibiotics and early biliary decompression within 24 h.
- **Grade I** (mild acute cholangitis): no grade II or III criteria; antibiotics only; biliary decompression only if worsening.
- TG 18 treatment guideline follows TG 13.
- Figure: TG 18 acute cholangitis management, as steps —
  1. Grade I (mild) → antibiotics and general supportive care (blood culture) → finish course of antibiotics, or biliary drainage if needed → treatment for etiology if still needed.
  2. Grade II (moderate) → antibiotics and general supportive care → early drainage → treatment for etiology if still needed.
  3. Grade III (severe) → urgent biliary drainage, organ support, antibiotics → treatment for etiology if still needed.
  4. Treatment for etiology = endoscopic treatment, percutaneous treatment, or surgery.
- **Summary of treatment per TG 18** (สรุปการรักษา)
  1. **Grade 1, mild acute cholangitis** — main treatment:
     - 1.1 Resuscitation & close monitoring
     - 1.2 IV antibiotics & correct coagulopathy
     - 85% of acute cholangitis improve within 12 hr; within 2-3 days the patient looks better and WBC falls; if controlled, continue IV antibiotics 1-2 wk, then definitive surgery later, usually in the same admission.
  2. 15% worsen or fail to improve within 12-24 hr — TG 18 grade II needing early biliary drainage, or grade III: fever > 39 °C, persistent abdominal pain, jaundice, hypotension, mental confusion (Reynolds pentad) → acute toxic cholangitis; patient near death → urgent biliary drainage — just drain the infected bile (pus) in the bile duct:
     - 2.1 1st line: urgent or emergency EST + drainage (จำ) — once infected bile gushes out, dramatic improvement; then stone extraction (takes extra time; if in a hurry, leave a biliary stent — helps a lot)
     - 2.2 If 2.1 fails → PTC and PTBD — beware bleeding & bile leak
     - 2.3 If 2.2 also fails → OR for surgical drainage: T-tube ≥ 16F only to drain infected bile — just drain; the patient is too ill for anything fancy; definitive treatment later if they survive
- **Endoscopic biliary drainage in toxic cholangitis**
  1. **Nasobiliary catheter**: can see content, culture, irrigate pus → less blockage, contrast injection possible; downsides — costly, uncomfortable, often pulled out
  2. **Plastic stent**: cheaper, easier, less uncomfortable; downsides — blocks more easily, content not visible, cannot be irrigated; needs a scope to remove when no longer needed
  3. **ES** in acute toxic cholangitis: better avoided — high bleeding risk, swollen mucosa, anatomy hard to see; use ES only when no stent available or a stent cannot pass because the stone blocks the orifice
- **Role of surgical treatment in elective setting**: endoscopic biliary drainage alone → 14% recurrent cholangitis within 6-12 wk → elective LC + IOC once inflammation subsides, about 6-12 wk.
- Observation (ข้อสังเกต): with a stent across CBD stones, stones may break and pass spontaneously.

## Intrahepatic Duct Stone
- Stones above the hepatic confluence; like primary CBD stones — brown, soft, friable, usually infected, especially E. coli and Clostridium.
- Common in Taiwan, Japan, Korea, Singapore; in Thailand 1.5% of bile duct stones.
- Also called hepatolithiasis or recurrent pyogenic cholangitis.
- Asian man with recurrent RUQ pain, age 30-40 → beware this disease.
- **Cause**: truly unknown; liver flukes were once blamed, but oddly in Isan, where flukes are common, these stones are rare.
- IHD stones → recurrent cholangitis → stricture and poststricture dilation → liver atrophy, cirrhosis, finally portal HT and cholangiocarcinoma.
- Cholangio CA incidence 2-10% → follow up with CA19-9 periodically.
- Asymptomatic in 40% — incidental on US or CT.
- Symptomatic in 60%: commonest pain 89%, transient jaundice or obstructive jaundice with cholangitis 10-30%; some present seriously, e.g. sepsis or bile duct cancer.
- **Investigation**
  1. US: initial study; IHD dilation, stones if lucky
  2. CT: better than US for seeing stones; shows distribution of stones in the liver, liver status — abscess, atrophy
  3. MRI / MRCP: non-invasive; location of IHD stones, IHD dilation and liver parenchyma
  4. CT cholangiogram: only when MRCP has problems or is unsuitable
  5. Do not forget tumor marker, especially CA19-9
  6. Invasive studies: ERCP and PTC
- **Treatment** (การรักษา)
  1. Asymptomatic, incidental: famous experts advise follow-up with US every 6 months — only about 10% develop cholangitis in 10 years. Once cholangitis occurs, the stones are a problem → treat: antibiotic first, then biliary decompression, usually PTBD, rather than rushing to surgery
  2. IHD stones are usually 1° stones, multiple, with bile duct stricture → besides removing stones, always consider a drainage procedure
  3. **Indications for surgery in IHD stone**
     - 3.1 Intrahepatic duct stricture
     - 3.2 Atrophy of affected segment or lobe
     - 3.3 Presence of liver abscess or cholangiocarcinoma
- **Principles of surgery**
  1. Complete stone removal
  2. Correct bile duct stricture with adequate bile drainage
  3. Prevent stone recurrence
  4. Future access to the IHD for later treatment, e.g. burying a subcutaneous jejunal limb
  6. Remove non-functioning liver segment — atrophic segment or with cholangiocarcinoma → hepatic resection (numbering skips 5 as printed)
- **Surgical treatment**
  1. IHD stone only in the Rt. or Lt. hepatic duct (1st order branch):
     - 1.1 No stricture or only mild:
       - 1.1.1 Low risk, fit for anesthesia → LC + LCBD-E or open + explore CBD, as for CBD stone
       - 1.1.2 High risk, too frail → PTC or ERCP stone removal only
     - 1.2 Significant hilar stricture, or recurrence after surgery as in 1.1.1 (re-operation likely to recur again) → hepaticojejunostomy with diameter about 2.5 cm, plus subcutaneous hepaticocutaneous jejunostomy loop — bury the end of the Roux limb subcutaneously in the abdominal wall for future scope access to remove stones; mark the site of the buried limb. Some object to the subcutaneous loop (infection, parastomal hernia, long-term fistula) and advise a large T-tube for future stone extraction (the author is not sure long-term T-tube does not work, as printed).
  2. IHD stone in 2nd or 3rd order branches:
     - 2.1 Stones only in the Lt. lobe → Lt. lateral segmentectomy or Lt. hepatectomy
     - 2.2 Left-sided stones with left liver atrophy → Lt. hepatectomy as well
     - 2.3 Bilateral stones — difficult:
       - 2.3.1 Lt. > Rt. with left atrophy → Lt. hepatectomy; right IHD stones → balloon dilation of the Rt. duct, then scope and basket removal
       - 2.3.2 Rt. > Lt. → before Rt. hepatectomy, be sure the remaining liver is enough
       - 2.3.3 High risk with high surgical mortality → PTC, dilate the IHD, pass a scope through the tract to remove stones, or lithotripsy
- **Summary of indications for hepatectomy in IHD stone**
  1. One lobe involvement, especially Lt. lobe
  2. Severe stricture — bypass alone surely fails
  3. Liver atrophy already
  4. Liver abscess or cholangiocarcinoma
- Steigman operation and Sitaram operation for recurrent IHD stones — residents need not know.
- **Endoscopic treatment**: high IHD stones are hard to extract transpapillary; via PTBD it is feasible — wait about 2 weeks, dilate the PTBD tract to 18 Fr, pass a choledoschope, and use balloon or fragment stones; but recurrence is fairly high since the stricture is not corrected → endoscopic treatment mostly for patients unfit for surgery.

## Cystic Disorders of the Bile Duct (Choledochal Cyst)
- West: choledochal cyst; Japan: congenital biliary dilatation. Common in young females, F:M = 8:1; 60% found before age 10.
- Cholangiocarcinoma risk about 2.5-15% (some texts up to 30%).
- Cause unknown; in about 90% of children believed due to **anomalous pancreatobiliary duct junction (APBDJ)**.
- APBDJ: CBD and main PD join > 1.5 cm from the ampulla → pancreatic secretion refluxes into the CBD → inflammatory change and raised CBD pressure →
  CBD dilation. Long-term complications: cholangitis, bile duct stone, liver cirrhosis, liver abscess, pancreatitis and malignant transformation (3-39%). So once diagnosed: with cholangitis → surgical resection as soon as feasible; asymptomatic → elective, about 3-6 months.
- **Triad**: RUQ pain, jaundice and RUQ abdominal mass — all 3 in only 20%.
- **Imaging**: in children US is preferred — MRCP is hard (cramped tube, crying, motion artifact, poor films); in adults MRCP is the first choice imaging technique.
- **Todani classification** — modified from Alonso-Lej, from 3 types to 5:

| Type | Imaging description | Incidence (%) |
|---|---|---|
| I | Dilatation of extrahepatic bile duct only — IA cystic, IB saccular, IC fusiform | 80-90 |
| II | Diverticulum | 2 |
| III | Choledochocoele involving intraduodenal portion of CBD | 4-5 |
| IV | Intra and extrahepatic duct dilatation — IVa intra and extrahepatic cysts; IVb multiple extrahepatic cysts | 10 |
| V | Caroli's disease | Rare |

- **Type 1**: fusiform dilation, 85-90% — most common type. Subdivision into 1A cystic, 1B saccular, 1C fusiform by shape is, in the author's view, pointless for exams — treatment does not depend on shape; whatever shape, excise it all.
  - **Treatment**
    1. Surgery: with cholangitis, operate as soon as possible; asymptomatic → elective when ready. Best operation: cyst excision — upper margin up to the hepatic duct confluence, lower margin down to the narrow part below the cyst; send F/S margins; reconstruct with Roux-en-Y 45 cm retrocolic hepaticojejunostomy.
    2. Cyst densely stuck from inflammation to PV or hepatic A. → **Lilly and Okada procedure**: open the cyst, dissect out the mucosa, leave only the posteromedial outer wall, then Roux-en-Y bypass — like the AAA technique (as printed).
- **Type 2**: 1-2%; true diverticulum, saccular, from the side of the CBD wall.
  - Treatment: open or laparoscopic cyst excision, then close the CBD defect by suture, put a T-tube through the opening, or bypass — as appropriate.
- **Type 3**: 1-2% (as printed; table says 4-5); local dilation of the CBD at the intramural part of the duodenum = choledochoceles; EGD shows the distal CBD protruding into the duodenal lumen; MRCP shows cystic dilatation of the distal CBD.
  - Treatment: formerly laparotomy, open the duodenum and excise the cyst, carefully identifying the ampulla of Vater; often needed PD. Now for Todani type 3 the treatment of choice is endoscopic (Blumgart, Sabiston, Schwartz): sphincterotomy ± biliary stent. Type 3 rarely involves APBDJ and cancer is very rare → Liu believes no long-term follow-up is needed after EST (the author thinks no follow-up is going too far).
- **Type 4**: about 10%; multiple cysts.
  - Type 4A: both extrahepatic and intrahepatic cysts
  - Type 4B: multiple cysts, all extrahepatic
  - Treatment: 4B as type 1; 4A — the problem is the intrahepatic cyst; if on one side of the liver, hepatectomy of that side.
- **Type 5**: multiple cysts only in the liver; 1%; called Calori (as printed) disease. Sporadic phenotype usually in one lobe; fibrous phenotype usually both lobes and associated with polycystic kidney disease 60-80%; association with cholangiocarcinoma about 7%.
  - Treatment
    1. One lobe → nonanatomical resection
    2. Both lobes → OLT
- **Summary — choledochal cyst** (สรุป)
  1. Presenting with cholangitis: admit, IV antibiotics until infection subsides, then operate in the same admission (จำ) — no need to wait 6 weeks, as cholangitis tends to recur; waiting 6 wk wastes time
  2. Treatment must be excision — never bypass the cyst without removing it
  3. Bypass has many long-term complications:
     - 3.1 Anastomotic stricture
     - 3.2 Biliary calculi
     - 3.3 Cholangitis
     - 3.4 Biliary tract malignancy

## Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC)
- More common in men, M:F = 2:1; mean age 35 years; can occur in children.
- Slow progressive fibrosing inflammatory process of intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts; cause unknown (จำ) — blamed on autoimmune as usual.
- **PSC is associated with**
  1. IBD 70-80%, especially UC
  2. Fibrotic diseases, e.g. retroperitoneal or mediastinal fibrosis, Riedel struma
  3. Autoimmune hepatitis
  4. Minor ones: porphyria cutanea tarda, liver fluke, celiac disease, pancreatitis, ankylosing spondylitis, colon adenocarcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma
- Remember (จำไว้): not associated with PJ syndrome.
- **Bile duct involvement**
  1. Extrahepatic bile duct only 10%
  2. Intrahepatic bile duct only 15%
  3. Both extra and intrahepatic 75%
- **Clinical presentations**: fatigue, weight loss, itching, RUQ pain and obstructive jaundice; diagnosis usually takes 1-2 years.
- **Diagnosis**: clinical presentation, biochemical abnormalities, histologic features and cholangiography.
- **Imaging studies**: now start with MRCP — gold standard for diagnosis; ERCP is an option; shows multiple dilations or multilevel strictures or beading of IHD and EHD; MRCP also monitors progress periodically.
- PSC usually runs down progressively → long-term cirrhosis, portal hypertension, liver failure; lifetime risk of cholangiocarcinoma 10-18%.
- **Liver biopsy**: not needed in every case, but most patients get needle liver biopsy to exclude other cholestatic liver disease and to stage — early: only duct swelling; hard liver = end stage.
- **Treatment** — principal goals: delay progression to cirrhosis and malignant transformation:
  1. No effective medical treatment at present
  2. Obstructive jaundice: try endoscopic balloon dilation; if endoscopic dilatation fails, dilate via PTC or surgery — especially EHD stricture → resection + Roux-en-Y with a long-term indwelling stent, helps prolong survival
  3. OLT, especially with intrahepatic bile duct stricture or established biliary cirrhosis
  4. PTBD is palliative treatment only
  5. Do not forget yearly screening colonoscopy — strong association with IBD
- Formerly the best treatment was believed to be liver transplant — patients followed 10-12 years died of liver failure, and the new liver was thought not to get PSC.
- Now the transplanted liver is known to develop PSC again → OLT, though the most effective treatment (5-year survival rates 75-85%), is not a cure as once believed.
- Figure: PSC management algorithm, as steps —
  1. Symptoms? No → no treatment. Yes → step 2.
  2. Liver failure? Yes → liver transplantation. No → step 3.
  3. Dominant stricture? No → medical palliation. Yes → CCA suspected? Yes → resection; No → endoscopic dilation → success → medical palliation; failure → resection.

## Bile Duct Injury
- Formerly OC 0.1-0.2%, LC 0.4-0.6%; now LC 0.1-0.3% — about the same as OC.
- Injury may be cutting, ligation or clipping; most often during dissection at Calot's triangle with excessive cephalad retraction of the GB, so the CBD is mistaken for the cystic duct, or dissection strays too far from the liver bed.
- **Risk factors of bile duct injury**
  1. Advanced age, male sex, obesity
  2. Severe inflammation or infection
  3. Aberrant anatomy
  4. Intraoperative hemorrhage
  5. Surgeon experience
- **Prevention of bile duct injury during LC**
  1. Meticulous dissection; before cutting any structure obtain the **critical view of safety**: strip peritoneum, clear fat and fibrous tissue in the hepatocystic triangle (texts still say Calot triangle — not wrong, but with Calot beware cystic artery variations; more than one artery can mislead). Then dissect the inferior 1/3 and medial aspect of the GB between liver bed and GB-cystic duct junction (จำ), where only the cystic duct and cystic artery enter the GB (infundibular technique). Bonus tip: retract the GB laterally, avoid pulling upward.
  2. Dissect the GB off the liver staying close to the GB.
  3. If Calot's structures cannot be cleared or seen clearly, e.g. 3.1 uncontrollable bleeding, 3.2 GB too inflamed to handle, 3.3 equipment not ready — do not push on stubbornly; convert to open.
  4. If unsure, think of IOC before cutting anything; routine IOC does not reduce injury risk, but do IOC when bile duct anomalies or a cut duct are suspected.
  5. Cystic duct or vessel not clearly seen → no blind clip.
  6. Alternatively find **Rouviere's sulcus** on the lower surface of the right lobe, where the right hepatic duct lies: dissect above the line of this sulcus, never below → CBD is spared.
- **Tokyo guideline 2018** — safe steps from Delphi consensus 2017 for LC in acute cholecystitis, 6 steps:
  - Step 1: very distended GB → aspirate bile with a needle
  - Step 2: retract the GB enough to open Calot's triangle
  - Step 3: start dissection at the GB neck above Rouviere's sulcus
  - Step 4: dissect along the GB surface throughout
  - Step 5: dissect the lower GB bed to reveal the critical view of safety
  - Step 6: clear the critical view of safety area clearly
- Difficult LC → TG 18 advises subtotal cholecystectomy as a bail-out procedure: open the GB, remove as much GB wall as possible, then close the cystic duct opening from inside or close the remaining stump.
- **Diagnosis of bile duct injury** — 3 time points:
  1. Intraoperative recognition — 25-30%
  2. Early postoperative recognition — most are found here
  3. Late recognition
- **Intraoperative recognition**: bile flowing, or bile pooling under the liver during LC → suction and check whether the cystic duct clip has slipped; if just cystic duct leakage from a slipped clip, re-clip and confirm with IOC. If not cystic duct leakage — contrast leaks on IOC — open, or if unsure convert to open, and IOC to locate the injury: 43% at the CHD, 23% at the hilum.
  - **Treatment** depends on severity:
    1. Lateral injury ≤ 1/4 or 30% of circumference → approach: 1° repair with absorbable suture, e.g. chromic or vicryl 4/0 or 5/0, plus drain. Formerly a T-tube was placed at a separate site with its limb as a stent, left 2-3 months, with cholangiogram before removal. Some newer surgeons think a T-tube is unnecessary; the author still places one, as does Shackelford 2019 p.1346.
    2. CBD injury > 30% of circumference, or caustic injury (usually from cautery to stop bleeding hitting the bile duct) → approach:
       - 2.1 Retrocolic Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy, Y limb 40-60 cm
       - 2.2 By an experienced surgeon, or one doing HBP daily with good hands and luck (as printed) — success rate 83-100%
       - **Principles of Roux-en-Y biliary-enteric anastomosis**
         1. Anastomose to the proximal duct, where tissue inflammation is less
         2. Mucosa to mucosa with absorbable suture, one layer
         3. Tension free
         4. Biliary stent not necessary
    3. **Complete transection of CBD** → approach:
       - 3.1 Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy is best
       - 3.2 Remember the surgeon must be skilled, with many flight hours
    4. **Isolated hepatic duct injury**
       - 4.1 < 3 mm or draining only one liver segment → ligate
       - 4.2 > 3 mm, usually draining ≥ 2 lobe (as printed) → Roux-en-Y
- **If the surgeon has little experience** (and is junior, and unlucky — as printed): whatever is done, even a good bowel anastomosis, may still stricture or leak. Oral exam scenario — examiner asks: you cut the bile duct, bile flows, IOC shows contrast leakage — what do you do? Answer: (as printed, joke about being a mouse or rabbit) just stop bleeding, place a JP drain under the liver — there will surely be a bile collection — then refer to a senior (one who likes a challenge) for biliary reconstruction.
- Residents should not boast in orals that they will do the Roux-en-Y themselves: success of surgeons not doing HBP regularly is only 20%, most end in stricture; HBP surgeons have success up to 90%.
- Remember: complications from bile duct injury — leak or stricture — make patients suffer for life, some die of liver failure. Juniors should start with hernia, appendix, cyst, gaining skill before major operations (as printed, ends with a joke).
- **Early postoperative recognition**: usually diagnosed day 7-10 P.O. Uncomplicated LC: within 24 h the patient eats, is well, afebrile, pain-free → home. If after surgery the patient looks unwell — low fever, RUQ pain, distension, fatigue, slightly abnormal LFT especially transaminases; jaundice rising slowly; some have sepsis from cholangitis or bile peritonitis.
- Any of these → suspect bile duct injury with collection (no routine drain after LC) → investigate.
  - **First step**: IV antibiotics, then US — usually a collection under the liver → percutaneous drainage under US or CT guidance, turning uncontrolled leakage into controlled leakage.
  - **Next step**: find where the bile leaks from and the bile duct proximal to the injury — MRCP (non-invasive); then decide whether ERCP + stent drain is needed to lower biliary pressure. Cameron favours adding CTA or MRA to rule out right hepatic artery injury, found in 10-30%.
- **Classification**: bile duct injury — Strasberg A, B, C, D, E1 to E5 (summary below); bile duct stricture — Bismuth.
- Figure: Strasberg classification — A cystic duct stump / GB bed leak; B occluded aberrant right duct; C transected aberrant right duct (leak); D lateral injury of main duct; E1 transection > 2 cm below confluence; E2 < 2 cm below confluence; E3 at the confluence (confluence preserved); E4 confluence destroyed (right and left ducts separated); E5 involving an aberrant right duct plus the main duct.
  1. **Strasberg A**: cystic duct stump leak or small accessory duct leak at the GB bed — subvesical duct of Luschka (accessory cholecystohepatic duct, about 10%).
     - Treatment: type A usually resolves with ERCP + stent and percutaneous drainage of the bile collection within 7 days.
  2. **Strasberg B**: occlusion of an aberrant right hepatic duct; mostly asymptomatic, a few have mild LFT abnormality.
     - Treatment: observation; if no infection or liver atrophy problems, no treatment needed. Rarely needs surgery, but with stricture + cholangitis treat as type C. So for types A and B, answering "operate" is usually wrong.
  3. **Strasberg C**: transection of an aberrant Rt. hepatic duct → bile leak.
     - Treatment: after draining the leaked bile, check duct size: ≤ 3 mm → ligate; > 3 mm → bilioenteric bypass, or resect that liver segment.
  4. **Strasberg D**: partial tear of the main bile duct.
     - Treatment: try ERCP + EST + biliary stent first if the bile leak is well drained and there is no sepsis — success 70-80%. If it does not close, wait until no infection or intra-abdominal inflammation, generally about 4-6 weeks, no more than 6 weeks, then operate:
       - 4.1 No tissue loss → repair; T-tube or not is debated — the author places a T-tube in every case, as taught
       - 4.2 Tissue loss → hepaticojejunostomy
  5. **Strasberg E**: main duct transected; usually ends with surgery — Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy with a stent; some advise a subcutaneous access limb when re-stricture is feared.
- **Role of ERCP**: diagnosis plus intervention — stent lowers biliary pressure and promotes leak closure. Beware: ERCP may look normal — usually an aberrant Rt. hepatic duct injury with normal CBD and CHD.
- If PTC is done, leave a PTBD — at surgery the proximal duct is easier to find.
- **Possible exception** (อาจจะมีข้อยกเว้น): postoperative bile duct injury needing early return — within 24-48 hr after LC, CBD injury with uncontrollable biliary fistula, or jaundice from complete CBD obstruction usually from a clip across the CBD; inflammation is still minimal → if stable, go back and remove the clip.
- **Late recognition**: presents with obstructive jaundice from bile duct stricture and cholangitis; whoever gets it has a hard time — the basic rule is a good primary operation (more later).

## Bile Duct Stricture
- Occurs at 2 times: early, right after surgery, e.g. clip or cut (covered above); or later, recurrent cholangitis months or years after surgery.
- By cause:
  1. Benign cause — 90% of bile duct strictures: after LC with bile duct injury, or after biliary-enteric anastomosis with anastomotic stricture; then benign diseases such as bile duct stone, Mirizzi syndrome, chronic pancreatitis, post RT, PSC, etc.
  2. Malignant stricture: bile duct or pancreatic cancer
- This section covers benign postoperative stricture; the key factor is destruction of the bile duct's specific blood supply.
- To understand fully, residents must know the CBD blood supply well:
- **Arterial system**
  1. 60% of CBD blood supply comes from below — retroduodenal, retropancreatic and gastroduodenal artery
  2. 39% from hepatic and cystic artery above
  3. 1% from the common hepatic artery
  - **Axial arteries**: a pair running along the CBD at 3 and 9 o'clock, giving 6-8 small branches about 0.3 cm that pierce the CBD into the epicholedochal plexus in the submucosa, supplying the hilar duct at the bifurcation, together with the venous plexus covering the bile duct.
  - The arterial and venous plexus covering the bile duct = vasobiliary sheath.
- **Venous system**: veins run with the axial arteries, not connecting to the PV until they end as branches at the portal triad.
- **Symptoms**: on-off jaundice after surgery for months or years; about 1/3 have cholangitis; thought to be from gradual fibrosis constricting the duct.
- **Investigation** — to locate the stricture and see anatomy proximal to it:
  1. LFT: obstructive jaundice pattern
  2. MRCP first
  3. ERCP for distal lesion
  4. PTC for proximal lesion
- **Principles of treatment**
  1. Non-operative first, e.g. dilation and stent — suits minor stricture
  2. Major stricture: improve general condition and control sepsis first, then drain bile by ERCP or PTC with serial dilation of the stricture (try non-operative first); if it fails, operate
  3. Choice of operation requires knowing the Bismuth classification
  4. Long-term patency: surgery is better than balloon dilation or stent
- **Non-operative treatment**: endoscopic treatment has advanced greatly → for bile duct stricture try non-operative first: dilation via endoscope or via PTC with balloon dilation of the stricture. Re-stricture after dilation is common → re-evaluate with cholangiogram every 3-6 months; may need repeated dilation; 5-year patency after dilation about 50%.
  - Intraluminal stent: suits malignant stricture with short life expectancy — stents block easily; stents in benign stricture do not work, needing change every 3-6 months.
- **Surgical treatment**: better long-term results than non-operative; before surgery know the Bismuth type.
- **Bismuth classification**: biliary stricture in 5 types by the relation of the stricture to the confluence of the hepatic ducts; types 1 and 2 are easy, types 3, 4, 5 are complex injuries.
  - **Type 1**: stricture > 2 cm from the bifurcation — fairly easy: Roux-en-Y jejunum to the side of the CBD.
  - **Type 2**: stricture < 2 cm from the bifurcation; most common. Harder — incise the hilar plate to reach the Lt. duct, then retrocolic Roux-en-Y jejunum 70 cm side to side hepaticojejunostomy with chromic 5-0 = **Hepp-Couinaud approach**; leave a stent 2-3 months, then remove.
  - **Type 3**: 2nd most common; stricture right at the hilum; hepatic ducts still normal, not involved.
  - **Type 4**: severe stricture at the hilum, but Rt. and Lt. ducts not fully involved.
    - Approach for types 3 and 4: dig into the Rt. and Lt. hepatic ducts and try to anastomose both; if impossible, dig at segment 3 for a peripheral bypass to one good side.
  - **Type 5**: very rare; stricture involves a Rt. branch deeper in — find a bypass by digging into the liver, or resect that side of the liver.
- **Tips for biliary reconstruction**
  1. Do not forget a stent if:
     - 1.1 No good fibrosis-free bile duct for anastomosis — common at reoperation
     - 1.2 Mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis not possible
     - 1.3 Duct < 0.6 mm (as printed)
     - The author leaves stents > 6 weeks; may flush with NSS every 2-3 days to prevent blockage; contrast x-ray before removal.
  2. (no item 2 as printed)
  3. If re-stricture looks likely, place the end of the Roux limb subcutaneously in the abdominal wall as a route for periodic dilation (see intrahepatic duct stone).

## Bile Duct Stricture Secondary to Chronic Pancreatitis
- 5-10%; inflammation and parenchymal fibrosis of the pancreas constrict the intrapancreatic CBD.
- Investigation: MRCP or ERCP.
- **Treatment**
  1. Start with ERCP + stent
  2. Stent fails or obstruction damages the liver → surgery; choledochoduodenostomy preferred over Roux-en Y choledochojejunostomy

## Vasculobiliary Injury
- Bile duct injury with hepatic artery injury: besides bleeding, the bile duct loses blood supply → leaks and strictures easily.
- Most injured artery: right hepatic artery — it lies right behind the common hepatic duct; injury is usually occlusion.
- **Stewart-Way classification** of bile duct injury with right hepatic artery injury:
  - Class I: CBD mistaken for cystic duct, but error recognized before CBD is divided
  - Class II: damage to CHD from clips or cautery placed on the duct; often where visibility is limited by inflammation or bleeding
  - Class III: most common (60%); CBD mistaken for cystic duct; common duct transected and a variable portion including the cystic-common duct junction excised
  - Class IV: damage to right hepatic duct, mistaken for cystic duct or injured during dissection
- **Treatment**: depends on the patient; if diagnosed at the start, repair the artery together with the bile duct injury to reduce liver ischemia.

## Benign Gallbladder Tumors
- 5% on US, incidental.
- Mostly asymptomatic — found incidentally in about 5% of upper abdominal US.
- Most common (about 60%): cholesterol polyp or cholesterosis — macrophages take up cholesterol, GB mucosa red and thick with yellow spots like strawberry skin; usually multiple, pedunculated; US hyperechoic compared with liver; usually < 1 cm.
- Worrying: polyps > 1 cm — usually adenoma, about 4%; whether adenoma relates to GB cancer is unproven, but > 1 cm → beware cancer.
- **Symptoms**: 64% none; 23% abdominal symptoms like GS; 13% LFT abnormalities.
- **US**: filling defect that does not move with patient position and no acoustic shadow.
- **EUS**: for suspected GB tumor — thick wall or nodule on the GB wall; widely used 4-5 years ago, but CT now gives clear images → less need for EUS.
- **Treatment** — per Zakko 2012 up-to-date management of GB polyp (widely cited), by polyp size and symptoms:
  1. Polyp < 0.5 cm: usually benign cholesterolosis, asymptomatic; no treatment; repeat US at 6 and 12 months; if unchanged, stop follow-up
  2. Polyp 0.5-1 cm: may be cholesterol polyp, adenoma or carcinoma; check high risk features — age > 50, solitary lesion, sessile polyp, fast-growing polyp, US polyp isoechoic to liver, or polyp with GS; with high risk features (chance of neoplastic polyp) → LC
  3. Polyp > 1 cm → LC; but > 1.8 cm strongly suggests cancer → CT and EUS before surgery (see approach to GB cancer)
- **Indications for surgery**
  1. Symptoms referable to GB polyp, after investigations exclude other causes — patients usually have had EGD
  2. Polyp with risk or suspicious malignancy:
     - 2.1 Size > 1 cm — most important (จำ)
     - 2.2 Number < 3, especially solitary polyp — high cancer chance
     - 2.3 Sessile lesion
     - 2.4 Sonographic evidence of mucosal invasion
     - 2.5 Age > 50 years
     - 2.6 Concomitant GS
     - 2.7 Rapid increase in size during follow up
- Remember (จำไว้): single polyp > 1 cm in an older person → must treat.
- **Treatment**: prefer open > LC; choose by size (author follows Blumgart):
  1. Size < 1.8 cm → may try LC first; if dissection during LC looks difficult, open OC
  2. Size ≥ 1.8 cm → open in all
  3. Sessile polyp > 1 cm → open — higher cancer risk than pedunculated polyp
  4. Shackelford formerly advised open for all; latest edition: lap is fine if the operator is gentle
- Figure: polypoid lesion of the gallbladder (PLG), as steps —
  1. Symptomatic (upper GI and biliary tract otherwise normal on investigation) → consider cholecystectomy.
  2. Asymptomatic, large PLG (> 10 mm) → consider cholecystectomy.
  3. Asymptomatic, small PLG (< 10 mm) → high-risk features (age > 50, solitary lesion, polyp growth, gallstones)? Yes → consider cholecystectomy; No → serial ultrasound every 6 months.

## Gallbladder Carcinoma
- Rare tumor, very poor prognosis — overall 5 yr. about 5% only.
- 85-90% asymptomatic; by the time it is known it is advanced or spread.
- Chemo and RT do not work well; for cure or long survival, adequate resection is needed — how much to remove is still debated.
- **Incidence**
  1. Elderly; F:M = 3-6:1
  2. 1-2% of elderly LC patients have foci of CA. GB
  3. 70-90% of CA. GB have GS; GS > 3 cm raises cancer chance about 10 times. But on average only 0.5% of GS patients of all ages undergoing LC have CA. GB — low, so LC in GS patients to prevent cancer seems overkill
  4. 90% adenocarcinoma
- **Spreading**: spreads in all directions; Blumgart believes it prefers the liver > lymph nodes; first node is the cystic duct LN or Calot node.
- **Risk factors**
  1. Female gender
  2. Geographic area — northern India, Eastern Europe, South America, Asia
  3. Ethnic — Native USA, Hispanic, East Asia
  4. High carbohydrate diet
  5. Hormonal factors — parity, late onset menarche
  6. GS > 3 cm — risk ×10
  7. Calcified GB wall (porcelain GB) — from chronic inflammation, scar formation and calcification; formerly cancer chance thought 30-60%, now only 7-10%; Schwartz 2019: discuss risk/benefit with the patient before deciding on cholecystectomy
  8. Choledochal cyst
  9. Chronic infection — typhoid and H. pylori
  10. GB polyp > 1 cm
  11. Anomalous pancreaticobiliary duct junction
  12. Tumor suppressor gene P53, K-ras mutation
  13. Miscellaneous — obesity, estrogen, IBD, FAP
- Remember (จำไว้): liver fluke, e.g. Clonorchis sinensis, does not raise risk — more related to cholangio CA.
- **Applied anatomy of GB**
  1. GB lies under the liver at segments 4B and 5 — wedge liver resection removes these segments
  2. Cancer site: fundus 60%, body 30%, neck 10%
  3. Arterial supply: cystic A., a branch of the Rt. hepatic A.
  4. Venous drainage directly into liver segments 4, 5
- **Applied histology**
  1. Inner mucosa & thin lamina propria; no submucosa
  2. Single muscular layer
  3. Subserosa — the surgical plane of cholecystectomy
  4. Serosa — visceral peritoneum covers only the anterior surface of the GB
- **GB histology differs from the gut**
  1. No submucosa or muscularis mucosa — from mucosa straight to muscle
  2. Single muscle layer, unlike gut with 2 layers OLIC
  3. Peritoneum as serosa covers only the front; the back has only thin fibrous tissue, sometimes called cystic plate
- **Location**: fundus 60%, body 30%, neck 10%.
- **Pathology** — 3 forms:
  1. Infiltrative type: wall thickening; spreads along the subserosal plane → cholecystectomy may cut into tumor
  2. Nodular type: invades adjacent pericholecystic structures, into the liver fairly early; tumor margin of invasion clearly seen
  3. Papillary type: tumor protrudes into the GB lumen with very little wall invasion; good prognosis
- **Symptoms** (อาการ)
  1. Early case: few symptoms; some present as cholecystitis
  2. Late case: obstructive jaundice, especially mid-CBD obstruction, weight loss, ascites, hepatosplenomegaly; with these about 95% are unresectable (as printed: better to book the funeral pavilion early)
- **Investigation**
  1. **Tumor marker**: of little use; CEA or CA 19-9 may be used for follow up, but not very useful
  2. **Imaging studies**
     - 2.1 **US**: done in almost all — 1st imaging study for biliary symptoms; sensitivity < 80%. US signs of CA. GB (→ send ceCT):
       1. Discontinuous thickening > 12 mm
       2. Wall thickening > 12 mm
       3. Mural calcification
       4. Fixed or protruding mass into the lumen
       5. Loss of interface between liver and GB
       6. GB polyp > 1 cm, especially sessile
     - 2.2 **Contrast enhanced CT (ceCT) chest, abdomen, pelvis**: if the radiologist is good at reading films, sensitivity up to 90%. CT signs of CA. GB:
       1. Enhancing mass with ill defined border in the GB lumen
       2. Eccentric GB
       3. GB thickening
       4. Fungating mass
       5. Liver invasion
       6. Lymphadenopathy
       - Venous phase helps distinguish CA. GB from chronic cholecystitis.
- Diagnostic imaging now: US, CT or MRI with IV contrast; some centres add MRCP to look for invasion of hepatoduodenal ligament structures and nodal involvement.
- Blumgart also advises 2 extra luxury investigations:
  1. Duplex US for the hepatic vein — the author thinks this is overkill since ceCT venous phase should show it
  2. PET-CT — rarely seen used; in 100 GB cancer cases with preoperative PET, treatment changed in only 5% → routine PET not worthwhile
- **Laparoscopy and LUS**: same idea as CA. pancreas — avoid unnecessary operation. Blumgart favours routine use — reduces unnecessary operations by 20-48% (the author agrees): if liver lesions or peritoneal seeding are found, biopsy and palliative care.
- Two things not very necessary for CA. GB now:
  1. **EUS**: formerly for staging; CT is now very good, so no need to swallow a scope
  2. **Preoperative biopsy**: keep for unresectable cases, or aspirate bile for cytology; if resectable, go straight on — no preop. biopsy
- **Staging and Management**
  1. Formerly Nevin staging — did not work; now AJCC/TNM 2017:

| Category | Definition |
|---|---|
| T1a | Tumor invades lamina propria of gallbladder wall |
| T1b | Tumor invades muscular layer of gallbladder wall |
| T2 | Tumor invades perimuscular connective tissue but not across serosa |
| T3 | Tumor invades across serosa of gallbladder wall and/or invades liver and/or one adjacent structure or organ |
| T4 | Tumor invades main portal vein or hepatic artery or two or more extrahepatic structures or organs |
| N0 | No lymph node involvement |
| N1 | Lymph node involvement within hepatoduodenal ligament |
| N2 | Lymph node involvement beyond hepatoduodenal ligament |
| M0 | No distant metastases |

| Stage II | T2 N0 M0 |
| Stage IIIA | T3 N0 M0 |
| Stage IIIB | T1-3 N1 M0 |
| Stage IVA | T4 N0-1 M0 |
| Stage IVB | T1-4 N2 M0; T1-4 N0-2 M1 |

  2. **Treatment**: extent of resection depends mainly on T — residents must remember this
  3. N +ve with near nodes, N1 (hepatoduodenal group), that can be cleared → still favour surgery; except distant N2 nodes, e.g. celiac, SMA → no surgery
  4. Encasement of major vessels or M +ve, e.g. peritoneal met, +ve malignant ascites → palliative care only

## Preoperative Suspicious CA. GB
- Symptoms and imaging point clearly to cancer → work up and stage:
  1. **Unresectable**, e.g. distant metastasis → treat the problem, e.g. jaundice or pain, non-operatively, e.g. stent
  2. Looks resectable → open, F/S of GB and LN (if any); if F/S cancer → resect. Remember: if cancer is suspected before surgery, never do LC — open only.

## Incidental Intraoperative Diagnosis
- During LC the GB looks suspicious — hard, difficult to grasp, enlarged LN, or a liver mass not seen on preoperative US.
- **Approach** — 3 options:
  1. **Convert to open** and radical resection, or (reported by expert centres) radical resection by lap or robot — most do not recommend this: no staging was done before surgery, and importantly no consent was obtained from patient and relatives that LC might include hepatectomy as a bonus
  2. **Terminated procedure** — start afresh: stage properly, then curative resection another day (as printed, "today is not a lucky day"). Youman Fong (world-class) says staged resection outcome equals 1° resection — the author uses this method.
  3. **Terminated procedure** without biopsy — risk of peritoneal dissemination; close and refer to someone (a senior) to do it.

## Postoperative Diagnosis
- Usually the pathology after LC reports cancer in the GB specimen. Answer constructively: discuss with the pathologist to review and assess tumor location, depth, margin, perineural or lymphovascular invasion, and Calot node if present.
- Review operative details: was the GB torn, any bile spillage — if so, treat as T2 CA.
- Repeat CT chest / abdomen / pelvis before definitive resection.
- On opening, first rule out distant lymph node metastasis — check celiac axis and aorto-caval groove; if present, send frozen section; if positive, the case ends early.
- **General rule**: T1a — LC usually enough; T1b and above → reopen.

## T1 Staging
- Split into T1a and T1b; formerly both treated by cholecystectomy, but T1b now seems more aggressive.
- **T1a**: tumor confined to mucosa or lamina propria, not reaching muscle; node positive very rarely (≤ 1.1%).
  - **Treatment**
    1. Cholecystectomy is adequate — subserosal plane in T1a does not cut into tumor
    2. Check cystic duct margin:
       - 2.1 +ve margin → open, resect cystic duct stump, CHD, CBD with F/S until free; may need bile duct resection with biliary enteric reconstruction
       - 2.2 –ve margin → enough; follow up
    3. No node dissection
    4. For reoperation, wide excision of every port site with > 1 cm margin was formerly advised; no longer necessary
    5. No staging workup needed (NCCN)
- **T1b**: tumor invades muscle but not serosa; node positive up to 20%; about 11% of T1b die of GB cancer.
  - **Treatment**: for tumor confined to muscle, still debated. Approach — 2 options:
    1. As T1a — favoured by Schwartz (most people disagree)
    2. Reopen for radical surgery as for T2 — Blumgart 2012 advises this and the author agrees

## T2 Staging
- Tumor through muscle but not through subserosa; cholecystectomy surely enters the tumor plane. Before the second operation NCCN advises complete staging in T2: CT chest, abdomen and pelvis.
- For a free margin at the second operation, liver must be removed en bloc as a package; question is how much — 10 years ago a liver bed margin of about 2 cm was accepted.
- Follow Blumgart and Cameron 12e (current practice): radical en bloc resection of segments 4B and 5 with portal LND, which Shackelford 2019 calls radical or extended cholecystectomy with portal lymphadenectomy (N1 group).
- Bile duct resection or not: depends on cystic duct margin, as for T1.

## T3 Staging
- Means:
  1. Tumor through serosa, may invade the liver or involve one adjacent organ, e.g. duodenum or colon
  2. Any T with N +ve (จำ)
- **Treatment**: still debated; trend among big names is aggressive — up to extended right hepatectomy or trisegmentectomy rather than the T2 resection (the author agrees with aggressive resection). Before reoperation some advise staging laparoscopy; NCCN says no need — just open and look.

## T4 Staging
- Tumor invades main PV or hepatic A., or +ve N2 group, or M1 disease.
- **Treatment**: palliative care, especially jaundice and pain; or chemo. or clinical trial. The author would advise T4 patients to study the Dharma, make merit, cleanse the mind in their last days, rather than suffer with toxic drugs that do little for this disease (as printed, personal view).

## Hepatectomy for CA. GB
- Anatomical resection guided by IOUS preferred over non-anatomical resection.
- Tumor at the infundibulum within < 1 cm of the portal pedicle → liver resection may need extended Rt. hepatectomy.

## Lymphadenectomy for CA. GB
- First know the basic lymphatic drainage.
- Lymphatic drainage of GB: to cystic and pericholedochal lymph nodes, then posterior pancreaticoduodenal, periportal, common hepatic artery nodes within the hepatoduodenal ligament = N1 group; beyond that celiac, aortocaval, SMA nodes = N2 group.
- Blumgart: remove only the hepatoduodenal ligament group (N1). MSKCC advises removing at least 6 nodes. If more distant nodes, e.g. celiac or retroperitoneal, are +ve → too far; stop, close, and go for prayers and chemo (as printed).
- The author prefers to save his own coronary blood flow — avoid risky things — and advises believing Blumgart.

## Port Site Recurrences
- **Definition**
  1. Local circumscribed tumor growth at ≥ 1 trochar sites
  2. Not confined to the extraction port — any port
  3. Mean time to detection 6 months after LC
- 10-18% in incidental CA. GB after LC; after OC abdominal wall recurrence only 6%.
- **Experimental evidence** — why more with LC than open:
  1. Carbon dioxide insufflations
  2. Peritoneal injury
  3. Bile spillage
- Older data: at re-exploration excise every previous port site (skin and fascia). Current data: port excision does not improve overall or disease free survival → NCCN and Cameron 12e: not needed, no improved outcome; Shackelford: surgeon preference.

## Chemotherapy
- Not effective for CA. GB; slightly better agents: gemcitabine, oxaliplatin, cisplatin.

## Radiotherapy
- May help locoregional control, especially +ve margin or LN metastasis; routine use still limited.

## Palliation
- Advanced disease usually lives ≤ 4 months; treat what the patient suffers from; if jaundiced, PTBD is enough — palliative RT and palliative chemo do not work.

## Chogiocarcinoma (CC) (heading as printed)
- Cancer of bile duct mucosa, inside and outside the liver, excluding GB cancer and periampullary CA.; 3% of GI malignancy.
- 2 main groups:
  1. **Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC)**: 20%, tending to increase; cancer of bile ducts within the liver → liver mass, presenting like HCC
  2. **Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ECC)**: 80%, tending to decrease; 3 levels:
     - 2.1 Hilar CC or upper 1/3: from CHD to extrahepatic hepatic ducts — 60% of ECC
     - 2.2 Middle third CC — 20% of ECC
     - 2.3 Lower third CC: distal CBD to periampullary area — 10%
     - 2.4 Remaining 10%: multifocal or skip lesions at several sites
- Some institutions use 3 groups: intrahepatic, perihilar and distal CC (figure: iCCA, pCCA, dCCA).
- **Incidence**: M:F = 3:1.
- **Etiology**: unknown; in Thailand linked to eating raw freshwater scaled fish carrying Opisthorchis viverini larvae, which live in human bile ducts; fermented fish dishes (ปลาร้า ปลาเจ่า ปลาจ่อม ปลาส้ม) contain much nitrosamine, a promoter accelerating cancer.
- **Risk factors for cholangiocarcinoma (CC)** — Established (accepted association):
  1. Opisthorchis viverini (OV) infection — strongly associated, especially in Isan, Thailand
  2. Primary sclerosing cholangitis
  3. Hepatolithiasis
  4. Anomalous biliary-pancreatic malformation & choledochal cyst
  5. Thorotrast exposure
  6. Liver cirrhosis
  7. Hepatitis C virus infection
- **Probable** — believable but needs more thought:
  1. Clonorchis sinensis infection — no. 1 in Thailand, then nitrosamines in ปลาร้า (as printed)
  2. HIV
  3. Hepatitis B virus infection
  4. Biliary-enteric anastomosis
  5. Environmental or occupational toxin exposure, e.g. digoxin (as printed), polyvinyl chloride
  6. DM
  7. Congenital hepatic fibrosis
  8. Genetic factors — many odd ones; no need to know
- Note (หมายเหตุ): not risk factors for CC — GS, chronic pancreatitis, biliary atresia.
- **Screening & surveillance**: though CC is common in Thailand, no lab or tumor marker is sensitive enough for early diagnosis → prognosis of CC is still poor today.
- **Gross**
  1. ICC: mass, grey-white, hard; resembles metastatic cancer to the liver
  2. ECC: narrowed, hard duct; upper lesions usually scirrhous type; lower ones usually nodular and papillary type
- **Histology**: CC differs from HCC — CC has mucin and makes no bile; but CC mucin cannot be told apart from mucin of metastatic cancer to the liver.
- **Spreading**
  1. Slow-growing; 2 patterns:
     - 1.1 Longitudinal spreading — along the length of the bile duct
     - 1.2 Vertical spreading — sideways, invading adjacent organs
  2. Prefers lymphatic over hematogenous spread; a few to bone, brain or skin. Nodes: hepatoduodenal group first, then celiac group.

## Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma (ICC)
- Cancer of bile duct mucosal cells within the liver; presents with a liver mass or hepatomegaly, often mistaken for HCC.
- Men > women; highest in the world in Isan, especially Khon Kaen.
- In Thailand ICC > HCC; outside Thailand HCC > ICC.
- **Cause**: not certain; in Thailand OV and nitrosamine are major risks.
- **Symptoms**: abdominal discomfort 50-80%, dyspepsia, fatigue, tiredness, anorexia, weight loss, wasting, pallor, low fever, shoulder and back pain; jaundice 20%.
- **Lab**: high alkaline phosphatase, CEA and CA19-9 high in about 2/3 of ICC.
- **Imaging**: usually starts with US — solitary mass, irregular margin, iso- or hypodensity. The best imaging, to do in all, is multiphase contrast-enhanced CT or liver protocol MRI (LPMRI) with IV contrast for diagnosis and treatment planning. CT of ICC: hypodensity in all phases, heterogeneous pattern (central necrosis); some show peripheral venous enhancement in portovenous phase; capsular contraction; dilated proximal IHD with a liver mass → think ICC.
- **Principles of ICC treatment**
  1. Depends on classification
  2. Surgery is the only chance for cure — need free margin; sometimes PVE is needed
  3. Ablative treatment, e.g. RFA, is supplementary
  4. Chemoradiation still on trial — mostly poor results
  5. Distant metastasis → symptomatic treatment
- **Preoperative biopsy**: not needed if clinical and imaging fit cancer — treat as cancer; consider diagnostic laparoscopy before surgery (option).
- **Classification of ICC**
  1. Classification by อ.ทองอวบ, Khon Kaen — 4 types; popular only in Khon Kaen
  2. Classification by the Japanese liver cancer study group — the author and others find it more appealing than Khon Kaen's; 3 classes, easy to remember and practical:
     - 2.1 Mass-forming type
     - 2.2 Periductal infiltrating type
     - 2.3 Intraductal type

## Mass-Forming Type
- Most common, 49%; as the name says, a mass with clear border; surrounding liver looks normal.
- Another ICC vs HCC difference on CT: both enhance in arterial phase, but in portovenous and delayed phase HCC shows venous washout (fades), while ICC shows progressive heterogeneous venous enhancement in portovenous phase (more visible).
- **Treatment** (ICC): surgery is the only hope of cure; ablative treatment such as RFA does not work well for bile duct cancer.
- **Principles of operative treatment**
  1. Anatomical resection as for HCC; ICC has no capsule → wide margin needed
  2. LND — 30% already have LN metastasis:
     - 2.1 Right-liver tumor → remove hepatoduodenal ligament, hepatic artery and retropancreatic nodes
     - 2.2 Left-liver tumor → remove hepatoduodenal ligament, hepatic artery, retropancreatic, paracardia and lesser curvature nodes
  3. Liver transplant does not work well
- **Contraindications for surgery**
  1. Extrahepatic disease
  2. Bilobar or multicentric tumors where R0 is not possible
  3. LN metastases beyond regional nodes — celiac and periaortic nodes
- **Prognosis**: 5 year survival 21%.

## Periductal Infiltrating Type
- 38%; spreads along tissue around the bile duct, quickly invading and obstructing at the hilum → worst prognosis.
- **Symptoms**: like hilar CC — obstructive jaundice; by diagnosis usually far advanced.
- **Treatment**: if resectable, very aggressive hepatopancreatoduodenectomy to clear tumor and LN basin.
- **Prognosis**: 5 year survival 7%.

## Intraductal Growth Type
- 19%; formerly called cystadenocarcinoma.
- Spreads along bile duct mucosa; usually papillary growth.
- Slow growth and spread → very good prognosis — the best.
- **Treatment**
  1. En bloc affected hepatic segment & bile duct resection
  2. LND
- **Prognosis**: 5 year survival 58%.
- **Summary**

| Mass forming | 49 | Anatomical hepatectomy + LND | 21 |
| Periductal infiltration | 38 | Hepato PD | 7 |

Imaging: dilated bile duct with a mass inside, from chronic cholangitis; malignancy chance 40-60% → treat as cancer.

## Extrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma (ECC)
- Most common is perihilar CC; usually advanced by diagnosis.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ)
  1. Malignant jaundice 70%
  2. Non-jaundice 30% — trend increasing
  3. Minor presentations: liver mass 14%, hydrops GB 6%, or acute acalculous cholecystitis 7%
- **Investigation** — many; not all needed: US and CT/MRI with IV contrast, assessing vascular invasion well, is enough to proceed.
  1. **LFT**: alkaline phosphatase high in both jaundice and non-jaundice groups; also check platelet, PT, PTT
  2. **Tumor marker**: no tumor-specific marker for CC yet
     - 2.1 **CEA**: raised in 30%, some reports up to 90% (overkill); also raised in other cancers, e.g. colorectal cancer
     - 2.2 **AFP**: usually normal
     - 2.3 **CA 19-9**: raised in up to 85% of CC; CA19-9 > 100 unit/ml → sensitivity 75%, specificity 85%; but benign obstructive jaundice and many other cancers can raise CA19-9
  3. **US**: initial study — jaundiced patients (yellow eyes, pale stools, itching, anorexia, weight loss) always get US first; with luck it shows a mass (e.g. at the bifurcation)
  4. **CT with IV contrast in arterial and portovenous phase**: good for resectability
  5. **MRI, MRCP**: non-invasive, very suitable for ECC; shows obstructed and non-obstructed bile ducts, tumor margin and extension, invasion, liver atrophy. Limitation: vascular invasion poorly seen → problems assessing resectability; Blumgart advises MRCP with duplex US (NCCN favours MRCP in all)
  6. **ERCP**: tissue biopsy or bile cytology — malignant cells found < 30% → ERCP for diagnosis does not work well; better kept for stent placement as palliative care
  7. **PTC**: little needed, like ERCP — except when PTBD is wanted
  8. **EUS**: shows the bile duct; FNA possible
  9. **PET scan**: when metastatic disease is suspected but CT is unclear (e.g. peritoneal metastasis of perihilar CCA)
- **Tissue diagnosis**: by ERC, PTBD or choledochoscopy is hard; HBP experts advise resection even without tissue diagnosis.
- **In practice**, suspected ECC:
  1. Start with US
  2. Then thin cut spiral CT or MRI / MRCP; if it looks resectable, take to theatre
  3. MSKCC adds duplex US for certainty — full options
- **Staging** — several systems:
  1. **Bismuth-Corlette classification**: old but still most used; good guide for surgery; limitation — lacks vertical or radial extension, nodal disease and distant metastasis
  2. **TNM**: in texts and NCCN, but little used — it is pathological staging, so does not help assess resectability, which is what is needed for preoperative planning
  3. **International cholangiocarcinoma group**: still under evaluation; uses tumor extent, tumor size, PV and HA involvement, liver volume remnant, lymph node status and metastasis; complicated, but gaining ground in America
  4. **Blumgart classification**: like Bismuth but adds vascular involvement; used at MSKCC (Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center), New York:
     - T1: tumor involves the biliary confluence with unilateral extension to secondary biliary radicles; no portal vein involvement or hepatic atrophy
     - T2: tumor involves the biliary confluence with unilateral extension to secondary biliary radicles, with ipsilateral portal vein involvement or ipsilateral hepatic lobar atrophy
     - T3: tumor involves the biliary confluence with bilateral involvement up to secondary biliary radicles; or unilateral extension to secondary radicles with contralateral portal vein involvement; or unilateral involvement up to secondary radicles with contralateral hepatic lobar atrophy; or main/bilateral portal vein involvement
- **Criteria for unresectability**
  1. Patient unfit for surgery — poor heart, (as printed) poor fate, or patient refuses surgery
  2. **Extent of local disease**
     - 2.1 Bilateral hepatic duct involvement beyond 2° biliary radicals
     - 2.2 One-sided liver atrophy with tumor invading the contralateral PV
     - 2.3 One-sided liver atrophy with contralateral hepatic duct involved beyond 2° biliary radicals
  3. **Vascular involvement**
     - 3.1 Bilateral hepatic A. invasion
     - 3.2 Encasement or occlusion of the main PV above the bifurcation
  4. **Extrahepatic spreading** or distant metastasis

- Now **main PV invasion** — mostly Japanese data — is no longer a contraindication: PV resection and reconstruction clearly prolongs survival; even hepatic artery invasion, front-line surgeons still go ahead (the author prefers to wait for standard textbooks on this).
- **Lymph node metastasis**: limited to the hepatoduodenal ligament group → not a contraindication to curative resection; spread beyond it, e.g. celiac, periaortic, pericaval, SMA node +ve → contraindication to oncologic resection. Japanese master Katagawa operated on N2+ve and reported 5-year survival 13%.
- **Surgical management** — 3 topics residents must know to satisfy examiners:
  1. Preoperative biliary drainage
  2. Curative resection
  3. Palliative treatment
- **Preoperative biliary drainage**: routine or not is debated — America does not routinely; Japan advises it in all, especially perihilar cholangiocarcinoma.
- **Indications** agreed by all:
  1. Cholangitis
  2. Operation cannot be scheduled soon — long queue (as printed, queue-jumpers) → PTBD meanwhile
  3. Prior to PVE, or when liver resection leaves an assessed future remnant liver < 30%
  4. Patients planned for neoadjuvant therapy
  5. Symptomatic jaundice — itching, sleepless — or severe jaundice: cholestasis impairs liver regeneration after major hepatectomy
  6. Renal impairment — obstructive jaundice predisposes to renal failure, because:
     - 6.1 Impaired Kupffer cells → portal endotoxin normally destroyed in the liver remains, loads the systemic circulation → kidneys → renal failure
     - 6.2 Urine bilirubin (large molecules) drives osmotic diuresis; if dehydrated → pre-renal failure
- **Biliary drainage**: 2 methods — PTBD and endoscopic biliary drainage; endoscopic divides into endoscopic biliary stent and endoscopic nasobiliary drainage.
- Disadvantages of preoperative biliary drainage, usually PTBD: reports of cutaneous implantation along the PTBD tract; endoscopic stents have stent problems.
- Japanese biliary experts now advise preoperative biliary drainage in all, trying endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) first; PTBD if that fails.
- Especially the famous Nagoya group advises routine preoperative biliary drainage, particularly in perihilar CC needing liver resection; results of the group
  are very good — operative mortality 0% (fans of the Japanese approach). Japanese reasoning: preop. biliary drainage relieves itching, lets the patient sleep and eat, no wasting, better liver function; draining only one lobe is enough; allows x-ray of the bile duct and bile culture. They drain until bilirubin < 2 mg/dL, about 4-6 wk, then operate.
- USA formerly strongly opposed unless indicated, now softer; if USA does preop. biliary drainage, they drain only until bilirubin < 6-7 mg/dL then operate. The author reads American theory but in practice follows Nagoya.

## Surgical Management
- Before surgery: hydration, correct electrolytes & coagulogram, improve nutrition.
- Some need preoperative biliary drainage.
- If liver resection is needed, assess future liver remnant.
- **Operative technique**
  1. **Hilar resection**: free margins proximal and distal; NCCN — control margin with F/S; proximal margin should be > 1 cm. By subtype: sclerosing CC (the majority) — > 10 mm is enough; papillary or nodular CC spreads further longitudinally → proximal margin > 2 cm. Distal margin is rarely a problem — plenty can be cut; best to cut the CBD down to the upper border of the pancreas.
  2. **Lymphadenectomy**: clear nodes around the lesser omentum entirely = skeletonization; strip around PV and hepatic A. cleanly. Behind the PV there are many small vessels — ligate well or they bleed annoyingly. MSKCC: remove no fewer than 7 nodes.
  3. **Caudate lobe resection**: Japanese routinely remove it for types 1 and 2, or margin may not be free; USA only for type 2 — the author agrees with USA.
  4. **Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy**: mucosa to mucosa, size > 1 cm; best to bypass both lobes.
  5. **Stent**: with anastomosis diameter > 1 cm a stent may be unnecessary; the author likes placing one — drains bile and allows postoperative cholangiography.
  6. Looked resectable before opening but found unresectable — mass fills the field → surgical segment 3 bypass.
  7. Recurrence after curative resection → trend is no re-resection; PTBD then other treatment.
- **Operation by Bismuth-Corlette classification** — like most, the author uses this anatomically based, surgery-oriented system; more appealing than others:
  - **Type 1**: tumor below the confluence of hepatic ducts, CHD only.
    - Treatment: bile duct resection ± caudate lobe resection (Japanese favour it in all; not enough for expert Japanese centres, who advise right hepatectomy even in type 1 because the right hepatic duct is short and margin may not be free) + LND.
  - **Type 2**: tumor at the confluence.
    - Treatment: bile duct resection + caudate lobe resection + LND; Japanese experts advise right hepatectomy for type 2 — short right hepatic duct, bile duct resection alone may not give a free margin.
  - **Type 3A**: type 2 + tumor into the 2° Rt. hepatic duct.
    - Treatment: as type 2 + Rt. hepatectomy.
  - **Type 3B**: type 2 + tumor into the 2° Lt. hepatic duct.
    - Treatment: as type 2 + Lt. hepatectomy.
  - **Type 4**: tumor reaches the 2° Rt. and Lt. hepatic ducts.
    - Treatment: palliative care, stent only — except Yuji Nimura and Hideki Nishio's group, who claim resection of type 4 up to 70% and 5-year survival up to 30% after resection; Japanese influence in Thailand is strong.
- **Distal cholangiocarcinoma** that is resectable → pancreaticoduodenectomy.

## Palliative Treatment
- Jaundice is the first priority.
- **Principles of palliative care**
  1. Quality of life before meeting Indra (as printed) (QoL)
  2. Minimal invasion
  3. Few complications
  4. Short stay, cheap
- Unresectable: average survival about 5 months.
- **ERCP + biliary stent**: overall most popular; suits distal CC.
- **PTBD stent**: suits proximal lesions not reachable by ERCP.
  - Advantages: can irrigate; blocked tubes can be changed; can dilate, x-ray, and give chemo through the PTBD tube.
  - Disadvantages: bile loss — water and electrolytes; a bag hangs on the patient — a nuisance.
  - **Contraindications**
    1. Coagulopathy
    2. Massive ascites
    3. Diffuse liver metastasis
    4. Multiple bile duct obstruction
- **Intraluminal brachytherapy**: Iridium 192 through a stent into the bile duct; problems — cholangitis and poor results; not popular now.
- **Photodynamic therapy**: through a metal stent; prolongs life a little.
- **Chemotherapy & radiotherapy**: slightly better than prayers (as printed).
- **Biliary stent**
  1. One or two sides — similar effectiveness
  2. Obstruction right at the bifurcation: draining one side → cholangitis of the undrained lobe in 10-15%
  3. Stent types:
     - 3.1 **Plastic stent**: usually blocks in 3-4 months
     - 3.2 **Metallic stent**: usually blocks in 6-9 months from tumor ingrowth
     - 3.3 **Covered metallic stent**: prevents tumor ingrowth → lasts longer; downside — may block side branches of the bile duct
  4. Patient with short expected survival, e.g. massive liver metastasis or generalized carcinomatosis → plastic stent is enough
  5. Expected survival > 6 months → the expensive metallic stent
  6. **Metallic stent**: expandable, lasts longer than plastic; downsides — expensive, cannot be removed, and no tumoricidal effect
- **Follow up**: ICC or ECC after curative resection — history, examination, US or CT every 3 months for 2 years, then yearly.
- **Liver transplantation**: poor results, like liver cancer — almost all die within 5 years, except the Mayo clinic protocol: locally advanced unresectable cases with positive diagnosis of cancer, by intraluminal brush cytology/biopsy or CA 19/9 ≥ 100 plus imaging looking malignant; mass ≤ 3 cm, node –ve, not extending below the cystic duct, no spread into or outside the liver, no prior RT or chemo, no other cancer within 5 years. Protocol: external RT 4,500 then IV 5-FU, then radium (as printed) brachytherapy 2,000-3,000 cGy with ceperitabide (as printed); then laparotomy — if no metastatic disease, transplant. Liver transplant for cholangiocarcinoma gave 5-year survival 82% — very high (interesting), but do not believe yet; await other centres. Residents should know liver transplant is not yet standard treatment for bile duct cancer.
- **Summary approach to ICC** (สรุปแนวทางการ)
  1. Patient with abdominal pain, abdominal mass, low fever of unknown cause, anorexia, weight loss — importantly from Isan, e.g. living in Khon Kaen → keep ICC in mind
  2. History, examination, LFT, US show a suspicious liver mass → MRI or CT; if it looks malignant → AFP, CEA, CA 19-9
  3. If it looks like ICC, assess resectability:
     - 3.1 **Resectable** → open and resect hoping for cure; if unresectable criteria are found at operation → palliative treatment
     - 3.2 **Unresectable**, e.g. metastasis → palliative care
- **Summary approach to ECC** (สรุปแนวทางการ)
  1. Patient with jaundice, abdominal pain, a face suggesting cancer → LFT and US; if ECC suspected → CT or MRI / MRCP
  2. If it looks like ECC → also CA19-9
  3. ECC has obstructive jaundice, so it must be treated anyway:
     - 3.1 **Resectable** → go ahead; if unresectable at operation → bypass
     - 3.2 **Unresectable** → stent, together with making merit in the last stage of life (as printed)
- Figure: suspected cholangiocarcinoma algorithm, as steps —
  1. Non-invasive imaging (ultrasound; MRCP/MRA; CT).
  2. Duct dilatation/obstruction with cholangitis → decompression (ERCP/PBD) → specialist surgical opinion. Without cholangitis, or no duct dilatation/obstruction → specialist surgical opinion.
  3. Histology & staging → resectable tumour → assess fitness for surgery ± laparoscopy → surgery ± adjuvant/neo-adjuvant therapy (unfit → palliation); non-resectable tumour → palliation.

## Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm of the Bile Duct (IPMN-B)
- Neoplastic intraductal papillary growth of biliary epithelium from peri-biliary gland cells, with fine fibrovascular core, protruding into the lumen of the bile duct — intrahepatic, extrahepatic, GB and pancreatic duct; can turn malignant.
- Common in China, Korea, Japan, Taiwan, associated with hepatolithiasis; in Thailand associated with Opisthorchis viverini.
- H&E: IPMN-B phenotypes like pancreatic IPMN — pancreatobiliary, intestinal, oncocytic and gastric type.
- Patients fairly old, usually > 60, with RUQ pain, weight loss, jaundice; US shows intraductal mass with intrahepatic bile duct dilatation; CA 19-9 raised.
- Next MRCP; cholangioscopy is even better — IPMN-B appears as yellow-brown papillary growth; biopsy possible and tumor extension better defined.
- Surgery hoping for cure is the best treatment — it can turn malignant; if malignant, invasive cancer in IPMN-B > 60%.
- Current debate: margin of resection — tumor may spread along bile duct mucosa further than seen (like pancreatic IPMN) → intraoperative cholangioscopy before resection.
- IPMN-B in the liver → resect as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma mass forming type.
- Extrahepatic IPMN-B → resect as perihilar cholangiocarcinoma.
- Prognosis better, longer survival than cholangiocarcinoma.

## Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy
- **Gas** — ideal properties:
  1. Colorless, inert, non-toxic, easy to obtain, cheap
  2. Non-explosive (จำ)
  3. High solubility in water and blood — dissolves well, so no air embolism
- **Carbon di oxide (CO2)**: most used — non-explosive and highly soluble in blood, so no air emboli.
- **Nitrogen**: dissolves in water/blood as well as CO2, but explodes easily — anyone answering nitrogen instead of CO2 will surely be failed (จำ).
- **Helium, Argon, Neon**: inert, so non-explosive, but poor solubility → air emboli possible.
- **Physiological effects of raised abdominal pressure** — guess what happens to compressed organs:
  1. **CVS**: decreased preload, BP drop; reduced venous return → stasis → DVT
  2. **RS** (respiratory system):
     - 2.1 CO2 absorbed via peritoneum → respiratory acidosis; luckily the body buffers CO2 well in bone
     - 2.2 Hypoxia is rare
     - 2.3 Pressure on the diaphragm → shoulder pain
  3. **Renal**: reduced renal blood flow → less urine
  4. **CNS**: raised intracranial pressure
- **Intraabdominal pressure**: normal people tolerate about 15 mmHg; if insufflation causes BP drop, simple fix — release gas, or allow pneumoperitoneal escape.
- **Key factor** for converting LC to OC: inability to clearly identify anatomical structures.
- **Pregnancy**: do it in the 2nd trimester — in the 1st trimester CO2 may affect the fetus; in the 3rd the large uterus makes it hard. Use open technique for the camera port — no blind puncture.
- **Contraindications for LC** — fewer and fewer; what remains:
  1. Potentially curable or suspicious CA. GB (จำ)
  2. Patient too poor for anesthesia — insufflating CO2 would surely kill
  3. Diffuse peritonitis
  4. Hemodynamically unstable
  5. Refractory coagulopathy
- **GS spilled into the abdomen during LC**: retrieve as many as possible; if not found — rough figure: leaving GS in the abdomen causes problems in about 8%, usually abscess.
  - Advice from Leslie (Canadian journal 1994): irrigate with the scope and retrieve as many as possible; converting to open just to find stones is overkill; if an abscess later forms, percutaneous drainage is fine.
- **Post LC GI perforation**: bowel usually perforates from cautery burn → bowel wall necrosis → perforation.
  - **Symptoms**: usually 2-3 days after LC — sudden abdominal pain, peritonitis, rigid abdomen; film may show free air.
  - Differs from CBD injury with collection: those patients have dull, mild pain, low fever on day 2, abdomen not rigid like bowel perforation; confirm with US.
  - **Treatment**: laparotomy, close the perforation.
- **Post LC shock**
  1. Early, within 24 hr: usually hemorrhagic shock, e.g. slipped cystic A. clip
  2. After 24 hr: usually septic shock, e.g. clipped CBD or bile duct injury with cholangitis
- **Cholecystostomy**: now percutaneous under LA preferred over open — catheter through the Rt. lobe into the GB, then pigtail catheter drainage; LC later when the patient is stable.
- **Transduodenal sphincteroplasty**: cut at 11 o'clock to avoid injuring the PD.
- **Choledochoduodenostomy**: late complication — repeated cholangitis from anastomotic stricture.
  - **Technique to prevent anastomotic stricture**
    1. Reduce tension by wide kocherization of the duodenum
    2. Anastomosis diameter wider than 1.5 cm
    3. Anastomose the CBD as distal as possible
    4. Do not use non-absorbable suture such as silk
