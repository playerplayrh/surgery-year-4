# Head and Neck 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 317-347 (PDF pages 321-351)

## Introduction (book p.317)
- Head and neck disease (other than thyroid/parathyroid) is unfamiliar to most surgical residents. This summary is useful for boards and for treating patients at provincial hospitals.
- Advice from the author: try to help with salivary gland tumors and neck dissection; for cancers of the oral cavity or tongue, diagnose, give basic treatment, then refer big cases.
- This topic is not covered as deeply as GI or thyroid.

## Anatomy of head and neck (p.317-318)
- Figure: sagittal diagram of the head and neck with subsites (nasal cavity, oral cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx, esophagus) and an open-mouth view of the oral cavity.
- Six main parts: 
  1. Nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses: most often tumors in the maxillary sinus.
  2. Oral cavity: lip, gingiva, buccal mucosa, retromolar trigone, floor of mouth (FOM), oral tongue (anterior two thirds); also hard palate.
  3. Pharynx: nasopharynx, oropharynx (base of tongue, soft palate, tonsillar pillar and fossa), hypopharynx.
  4. Larynx: supraglottic (false cords, arytenoids, epiglottis, aryepiglottic fold), glottic (true cord), subglottic.
  5. Thyroid.
  6. Salivary gland.
- Anatomy of larynx (figure labels): epiglottis, hyoid bone, thyroid cartilage, cricoid cartilage, vestibule of larynx, vestibular fold, vocal fold, vocal muscle, infraglottic space, trachea.

## Cystic hygroma (lymphangioma) (p.318-319)
- Congenital lymphangiomatous malformation: a mass of many fluid-filled cysts; present from birth, rarely first seen in adults.
- Associated with polyhydramnios (not oligohydramnios).
- Location: anywhere, but most often the neck, in the posterior triangle.
- US to see the size of the cysts and the borders of the mass.
- Complications: infection is the commonest; then hemorrhage, respiratory distress, dysphagia; no strong report of malignant transformation.
- Treatment: conservative excision of everything is best, but do not cut radically since nerves may be hurt; another option is sclerosant injection.

## Branchial cleft cyst and fistula (p.319)
- From the branchial clefts, which develop into structures along the side of the neck, ear and trachea; when development is complete the cleft/groove should disappear; the problem is when it does not disappear: the child has a cyst or a fistula.
- Ectodermal branchial cleft: 4 types exist, but know 3 (focus on number 2).
- First: develops from the preauricular and submandibular area; opening usually in front of the ear, near the mandible; the tract opens at the external ear canal and runs through the parotid gland, so beware the facial nerve in surgery.
- Second (commonest): on the side of the neck; external opening along the anterior border of the SCM at its middle; internal opening at the tonsillar fossa of the same side; the tract passes through the carotid bifurcation; beware the hypoglossal nerve in surgery. (Figure: typing of branchial cleft cyst with CN X, XI, XII and 2nd branchial anomaly.)
- Third: opening in front of SCM like the second but lower, near the suprasternal notch; internal opening at the piriform sinus; the tract runs behind the external and internal carotid arteries and so reaches/passes through thyroid; some patients present with suppurative thyroiditis.
- Fourth: tract from the pyriform sinus passes to the chest, loops back and opens in front of SCM.
- Infection is common and cancer risk has been reported; so treatment = surgical excision with the whole tract.
- If infected as an abscess: I&D + antibiotic first, then excise the mass later.

## Carotid body tumor (chemodectoma) (p.319-321)
- Benign unifocal non-hereditary paraganglioma.
- A lateral neck mass in people older than 35 years.
- The mass pulsates, may have a bruit, painless; do not do FNA (hypervascular tumor; risk of bleeding).
- Investigation: US or CTA; CTA shows the classic lyre sign (mass at the carotid bifurcation splaying ICA and ECA); CT: homogeneous, hypervascular, well-defined strongly enhancing mass at the carotid bifurcation.
- Because malignancy has been reported, excise every case.
- Treatment:
  1. Excision; if the mass is >3 cm do preoperative angiogram then embolization of the feeding vessels first (main feeding vessels 2; after embolization the mass is dark with less vascularity, easier to operate).
  2. The tumor sticks to the carotid bifurcation wall, so dissect it off in the subadventitial plane.
  3. Beware injury to the superior laryngeal nerve (a branch of vagus) and the hypoglossal nerve.

## Neck dermoid cyst (p.321)
- Mass is in the midline, under the chin, separate from the thyroid, not moving with swallowing.
- Has an epithelial lining filled with skin appendages: hair, hair follicles, sebaceous glands.
- Treatment: excise because of infection risk.

## Submandibular abscess (p.321)
- Infection of the submandibular space; commonest cause is a peridental (dental) abscess; others: cancers in the mouth, fractured jaw bone, foreign body.
- Organisms: oral anaerobes (peptostreptococcus) and aerobic gram-negative organisms.
- Symptoms: usually within 48 hours; high fever, swollen jaw/neck; if untreated may develop Ludwig angina (airway obstruction).
- Treatment: 1. IV broad-spectrum antibiotic; 2. incision and drainage is a must (percutaneous aspiration or percutaneous catheter drainage alone is not enough); 3. consult a dentist to treat the cause.

## Ludwig angina (p.322-323)
- Figure: submandibular space (sublingual space, submaxillary space) above/below mylohyoid; clinical photo; CT.
- Rapid, severe soft tissue infection of the submandibular space.
- Common in diabetics, cirrhotics, and other patients with poor dental hygiene.
- Commonest cause: dental caries (75%), of the 2nd and 3rd lower molars.
- Bacteria are the oral flora: 1. aerobes: strep and hemophilus; 2. anaerobes: strep, peptostrep, fusobacteria.
- Clinical: 1. skin inflamed like cellulitis; firm swelling above the hyoid bone, painful swallowing. 2. Patient may have difficulty breathing and must sit upright because lying down impairs breathing; trismus; on opening the mouth the tongue is pushed up against the palate, so the doctor must press it out.
- Investigation: 1. panoramic film of the mandible: shows dental caries or abscess; 2. soft tissue neck film: prevertebral soft tissue in front of C2 normally not thicker than 2 mm; 3. chest x-ray; 4. contrast-enhanced CT neck = imaging of choice for deep neck infection; 5. bedside needle aspiration of pus for testing.
- Treatment: 1. maintain the airway first: if breathing is borderline do not wait for stridor or cyanosis; tracheostomy is preferred over ET tube since opening the mouth is very difficult. 2. The text recommends high-dose antibiotic, e.g. PGS 12-20 million units per day. 3. Surgical drainage with an incision parallel to the mandible, 1 cm from it, in cases with fluctuation, crepitus, pus on aspiration or air on imaging, after full antibiotics; some drain pus intraorally, which is difficult because the tongue and nose make care hard. 4. Improve nutritional status; control diabetes.

## Pharyngeal abscess (p.323)
- Pharyngeal space = retropharyngeal space (behind the pharynx) and parapharyngeal space (beside the pharynx). (Figure: sagittal view of retropharyngeal abscess.)
- Retropharyngeal abscess: fever, difficult and painful swallowing; the mouth shows red swelling bulging at the posterior pharynx.
- Parapharyngeal abscess: fever, painful swallowing, neck swelling, trismus; the mouth shows the tonsil and uvula displaced toward the midline.
- Treatment, same for both: IV antibiotics + I&D.

## Neck mass (p.324)
- Pediatric age group: think congenital, inflammation, infection.
- Adults who smoke or drink alcohol and present with a persistent neck mass: think of cancer.
- Rule of seven: 7 days = inflammation or infection; 7 months = tumor; 7 years = congenital.

### Clinical evaluation of neck mass (p.324-325)
- History: 1. chief complaint: how long; acute, subacute or chronic; mass, wound, difficulty swallowing, cough with blood, hoarseness, new-onset otalgia; 2. risk factors for cancer; 3. features of cancer: symptoms longer than 2 weeks, history of smoking, drinking alcohol; if painful it hurts all the time; hard mass, enlarged nodes.
- Physical examination: 1. inspection: where is it; skin surface of face and neck and visible mucosa; 2. palpation: features of the mass, inflamed or not; if the mass is hard like cancer, with induration attached to adjacent tissue, or the lesion is deep in the tongue, pharynx, larynx, or the patient has trismus and cannot open the mouth, exam under anesthesia (EUA) is needed.
- Investigation: 1. CBC (+/- HIV if TB node suspected); 2. imaging: choose cheap ones first, e.g. film neck, mandible, chest, skull, which often help staging; 3. expensive: CT to see the extent, bony destruction, invasion of adjacent organs and nodes; MRI suits involvement of soft tissue, parotid and parapharyngeal space and separates a mass from inflammation better than CT (example CT: floor of mouth cancer with mandibular invasion); 4. fiberoptic endoscopy (nasopharyngolaryngoscopy) is part of evaluating the upper aerodigestive tract; bronchoscope and esophagoscope in selected cases.
- Biopsy: be sure it is not a vascular tumor. 1. FNAC: perfect (better than biopsy) for neck mass, lymph node, thyroid and salivary gland; 2. incisional or punch biopsy: for lesions in the oral cavity; 3. excisional biopsy: for small lesions in the mouth or cases where FNA is negative only.
- About 5% of head and neck cancers have a second primary SCC in head and neck, esophagus or lung: for larynx look at the lung; for oral cavity look at the esophagus.
- Approach: if a neck mass is likely infectious (acute onset, systemic symptoms, fever, pain; or with dental caries or oral pus), treat with antibiotics and oral hygiene, full dose, not more than 4 weeks; then review; if not improved, think neoplastic cause and do FNA.
- Approach to head and neck cancer:
  1. Oral cavity cancer: 1.1 early stage = stage 1 and 2, good risk: choose surgery, except cancers named below (nasopharynx and base of tongue) where radiation is chosen; for high-risk patients, give RT even in early cancer; choose either surgery or RT. 1.2 Advanced stage = 3 and 4: multimodality treatment: surgery with radiation plus chemo.
  2. Cancers of the upper aerodigestive tract such as nasopharynx, hypopharynx, larynx, tonsil or oropharynx: now organ preservation with chemoradiation is the main approach; surgery is kept for residual lesion after chemoradiation.
- Surgical margin for oral cavity cancer: if no frozen section reader, cut >=1 cm; if frozen is available, cut by steps of 5 mm.

## Risk factors (p.325)
1. Cigarette and alcohol: the strongest risk, both together; smoking is worse than drinking; if both, risk x35.
2. Chronic irritation from ill-fitting dentures, chewing betel, poor oral hygiene, rough teeth or dentures.
3. Chronic infection, e.g. TB, syphilis; CA oropharynx is associated with young people with high-risk sexual behavior with HPV positive up to 60-80%; EBV with CA nasopharynx.
4. Plummer-Vinson syndrome. 5. Malnutrition. 6. UV exposure (lip cancer). 7. Genetic.

## Precancerous lesions (p.325-326)
- Know leukoplakia and erythroplakia.
- Leukoplakia: white patch, thick, on the oral mucosal surface; unlike a fungal patch it cannot be scraped off; risk of cancer about 5 times normal; biopsy in every case; beware lesions that are ulcerated or red with higher cancer risk. If the lesion is small, excise it; if large, take only a punch or incisional biopsy, and send every specimen for pathology; treatment depends on pathology:
  1. If benign: conservative, explain, avoid risk factors, follow up.
  2. If no improvement within 1 month, or pathology shows carcinoma in situ or dysplasia: dysplasia has 10-30% risk of future cancer; do complete resection with free margin.
- Erythroplakia: red patch in the mouth; risk of SCC 20% (more than leukoplakia); approach like leukoplakia, but some believe the risk is as high as 90% and advise WLE straight away.

## Staging (AJCC 2017, oral cavity) (p.326)
- T (size and depth):
  - T1: <=2 cm in greatest dimension and depth of invasion (DOI) <=5 mm.
  - T2: >2 cm but <=4 cm, or DOI >5 mm but <=10 mm.
  - T3: >4 cm, or any size with DOI >10 mm but <=20 mm.
  - T4a: invasion of adjacent structures, e.g. skin, cortical bone of upper or lower jaw, deep muscle of the tongue, maxillary sinus.
  - T4b: invasion of masticator space, pterygoid, skull base, encases the internal carotid artery.
- N (size and involvement; note extranodal extension, since N1 without ENE can have surgery or RT alone, otherwise combined treatment):
  - N1: single ipsilateral node <=3 cm in greatest dimension.
  - N2a: single ipsilateral node >3 cm but <=6 cm.
  - N2b: multiple ipsilateral nodes, largest <=6 cm.
  - N2c: bilateral or contralateral nodes, largest <=6 cm.
  - N3: any node >6 cm is N3a; N3b: any size but with extranodal extension.

## Sentinel lymph node in head and neck cancer (p.326)
- 4-5 years ago SLN was still a future direction; studies use SLN in cN0 oral cancer with occult metastasis >20% to avoid unnecessary neck dissection, as in breast cancer; per current NCCN, SLN is now in the treatment guideline.
- Summary: for T1-2 N0 lip cancer, still debated. For T1-2 N0 oral cavity cancer with high risk of metastasis (buccal mucosa, anterior tongue, alveolar ridge, retromolar trigone, hard palate), SLN role: 1. SLN negative: do nothing more; 2. SLN positive with a single node and no adverse features: give RT; if criteria not met, do neck dissection; 3. SLN identification unsuccessful: do neck dissection.
- Adverse features: 1. extracapsular nodal spread; 2. positive margin; 3. primary pT3 or pT4 (locally advanced); 4. N2-3; 5. nodal disease at level 5 or 6; 6. perineural invasion; 7. angiolymphatic invasion.

## TNM stage groups, oral cavity (p.327)
- Stage 1: T1 N0. Stage 2: T2 N0. Stage 3: T3 N0, or T1-2 N1. Stage 4: T4 N1-3 M0, any T any N M1. (N1 <3 cm; N2 3-6 cm, multiple, bilateral; N3 >6 cm.)
- Currently T4 is divided into T4a and T4b; T4b is considered inoperable.
- Stage 4a: T4a N0-1 M0, or T1-3 N2 M0. Stage 4b: any T N3 M0, or T4b any N M0. Stage 4c: any T any N M1.
- 1/3 of patients are stage 1, 2: usually single modality treatment; 2/3 are stage 3, 4: multimodality treatment.
- Prognosis: lymph node positivity reduces 5-year survival by about 50%, so cervical lymph node status is a significant prognostic factor.

## Principles of surgery (p.328)
1. En bloc resection of the primary tumor; T4a oral cavity cancer can still be operated.
2. Cut the primary tumor with a margin of about 1.5-2 cm from the gross tumor, with frozen section control (Schwartz: margin 1 cm).
3. Clear margin = distance from the margin to invasive cancer >=5 mm; less than 5 mm the pathologist reads as close margin.
4. Invasion of CN 7, 10, 11, 12 that cannot be preserved, or nerve paralysis already present before surgery: segmental resection and nerve grafting.

## Indications for adjuvant radiation (p.328)
- Give RT within 6 weeks after surgery.
1. T3 or T4 (size >4 cm, i.e. locally advanced): give RT in every case; 2. positive margin; 3. field contamination; 4. poor differentiation; 5. vascular, lymphatic or nerve invasion; 6. N2 or N3; for N1 only with extracapsular extension; 7. post excisional biopsy of a neck node (open biopsy must be followed by RT in all; hence use FNA for neck nodes).

## Indications for adjuvant chemoradiation (p.328)
1. T3 or T4; 2. multiple nodes; 3. positive surgical margin; 4. lymphovascular invasion; 5. extracapsular extension of nodes.
- Note: adding chemo with RT is usually considered in patients with poor prognostic factors, appearing to be better than RT alone.

## Indications for neoadjuvant chemoradiation (p.328)
1. Advanced stage to reduce tumor size until some can be resected; 2. carotid artery involvement; 3. involvement of deep muscles.

## Lip cancer (p.328-329)
- 40% of head and neck cancer (as printed); 90-95% on the lower lip, mostly SCC; upper lip only 2-7%, usually BCC.
- Workup: if bony invasion is suspected, send a panorex film; also ask a dentist to evaluate.
- Treatment: surgery and RT have equally good results for T1 and T2, but surgery is chosen first, especially for the lower lip, which has much volume: margin 1 cm; after resection, a defect not more than 33% can be closed primarily; if the defect is >33% close with a flap.
  1. Surgery: 1.1 T1-2 N0 = size <4 cm: WLE with negative margin (preferred); the alternative is definitive RT; if residual tumor after RT, resect it; SLN role in T1-2 N0 is not needed yet. 1.2 T3-4 N0 = size >4 cm, or N1: WLE + SOHND + postoperative RT. 1.3 If the lesion is deeper than 4 mm do SOHND as well. 1.4 Lip reconstruction: many methods, e.g. Abbe, Estlander, Webster.
  2. RT: see indications for adjuvant RT.
  3. Chemotherapy: in cases with node extracapsular spread and/or positive margin.

## Palate (p.329)
- Mostly SCC, especially from minor salivary glands.
- Kaposi sarcoma is the most common tumor at this site in AIDS patients... as printed: although palate is the commonest site of Kaposi sarcoma in AIDS, SCC is still the most common tumor of AIDS patients at the palate.
- Treatment: WLE + bone resection + reconstruction (difficult) + postoperative RT as indicated.

## Alveolar ridges (p.330)
- Includes the gingiva and the dental surface of the maxilla or mandible.
- Treatment: WLE + segmental resection of bone + postoperative RT as indicated.

## Floor of mouth (FOM) (p.330)
- Figure: floor of mouth with sublingual veins, frenulum, openings of submandibular duct, tumor.
- Treatment: 1. WLE + en bloc resection of involved tongue and mandible; 2. T2 and up: bilateral SOHND; 3. postoperative RT as indicated.

## Oral tongue cancer (p.330-331)
- Means the anterior 2/3 of the tongue; the tongue base is part of the oropharynx and is treated like the oropharynx. Usually presents as an ulcerated exophytic mass; often spreads to submandibular and upper cervical lymph nodes.
- If there is invasion of the lingual nerve and hypoglossal nerve: numbness and tongue deviation (tongue deviates toward the side of the cancer when protruded).
- Treatment: 1. WLE if lesion is small, may cut with a knife or CO2 laser to a free margin then suture; if the lesion is large do partial or hemiglossectomy; 2. mandibulectomy if tumor invasion; 3. T2 and up, or depth >4 mm, or perineural invasion: SOHND; 4. postoperative RT as indicated.

## Buccal mucosa (p.331)
- Very uncommon nowadays; used to be found in grandmothers who chew betel and hold it in the cheek. Rare disease.
- Treatment: 1. WLE + SOHND for T2 and up, usually needed because it spreads to nodes quickly; 2. postoperative RT as indicated.

## Oropharyngeal cancer (p.331)
- Cancer at the base of tongue, tonsil, posterior pharyngeal wall, soft palate. (Figure: anatomy of pharynx.)
- Workup: HPV testing, CT or MRI with contrast; if stage 3 or 4 add PET.
- Treatment: choose definitive RT; if residual disease, resect the remainder; choosing surgery as primary treatment is usually harsher.

## Hypopharyngeal cancer (p.332)
- Approach like oropharyngeal cancer.

## Nasopharyngeal cancer (p.332)
- Send MRI with contrast of the skull base and nasopharynx, plus EBV testing.
- Treatment is non-surgical: T1 (<2 cm) N0 M0: RT alone is enough; otherwise concurrent chemoradiation.

## Laryngeal cancer (p.332)
- Send thin-cut CT of the larynx; if in doubt add MRI.
- Treatment: 1. carcinoma in situ: endoscopic resection rather than RT; 2. T1-2 N0: RT first; if surgery, only partial laryngectomy; 3. T3 and up: choose RT, because surgery would mean total laryngectomy.
- Note: for cancers in the last few sites (larynx etc.), the main treatment is radiation supplemented by chemotherapy.

## Lymphatic spread (p.333-334)
- Neck node levels (from Memorial Sloan Kettering; 7 levels, some subdivided into a and b; residents need levels 1-6):
  - Level 1: submental and submandibular (IA submental, IB submandibular).
  - Level 2: upper jugular (IIA, IIB).
  - Level 3: middle jugular, below the hyoid bone.
  - Level 4: lower jugular, below the cricoid cartilage.
  - Level 5: posterior triangle (VA, VB).
  - Level 6: anterior or central.
  - Level 7: upper mediastinum.
- Figure: primary sites by node level: lip, oral cavity and skin cancers -> level I; oropharynx, larynx, hypopharynx cancers -> II, III; scalp, pinna, thyroid and nasopharynx cancers -> V; thyroid and infraclavicular cancers -> IV/VI; upper aerodigestive cancers can metastasize to level IV nodes.
- Cancer spreads from upper to lower in the neck, and skip metastasis is found in 15%.
  1. Lip and oral cavity: to level 1, 2, 3. 1.1 Upper lip at the midline: the primary drains to the preauricular node before other groups. 1.2 Lower lip at the midline drains to submental nodes.
  2. Oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx: to level 2, 3, 4 (larynx rarely goes to levels 1 and 5; even less to level 6, 7... as printed: very unlikely to level 6, 7 is wrong, i.e. residents should not choose 6, 7).
  3. Nasopharynx: level 2, 3, 4, 5.
  4. Subglottic, thyroid, cervical esophagus: level 6, 7.
  5. Skin of the face: parotid node at the cheek, then levels 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.
  6. Skin of the back of the head: posterior auricular and suboccipital nodes first, then levels 2, 3, 4, 5.

## Cervical metastasis (p.334)
- Incidence of occult metastasis: cancers with >20% chance of nodal spread, especially T2 N0, should be considered for prophylactic neck dissection.

| Primary site | Occult node metastasis |
|---|---|
| Hard palate, lip | 10% |
| Alveolar ridge, FOM*, oral tongue* | 30% |
| Soft palate, retromolar trigone | 45% |
| Buccal mucosa* | 50% |
| Oropharynx, hypopharynx | 65% |
| Tonsillar fossa | 76% |
| Base of tongue | 78% |
- For an isolated cystic neck node in an adult, look for a primary from the tonsil and base of tongue.
- Bilateral metastasis (* sites above): midline lower lip, FOM*, soft palate*, base of tongue*, piriform sinus, supraglottic larynx, tonsil.

## Neck dissection (ND) (p.335)
- Classification: 1. by indication: elective (prophylactic) ND, therapeutic ND. 2. By technique (Medina classification): 2.1 comprehensive ND: radical ND (RND), extended RND, modified RND (MRND); 2.2 selective ND.
- Currently still open surgery, but some have begun doing it endoscopically; selective ND may soon be done by scope.

### Elective or prophylactic neck dissection (p.335)
- Used for N0 (nodes not palpable): consider selective ND if: 1. risk of occult metastasis >20% in: 1.1 T2 of oral tongue, FOM, buccal mucosa, alveolar ridge, piriform sinus, retromolar trigone; 1.2 T3 of lip and hard palate; 1.3 oral cavity cancer: at least levels 1-3; 1.4 oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx: levels 2-5. 2. Vertical thickness (depth) >4 mm (an RCT has begun and results say >3 mm already warrants it). 3. Surgery of the primary lesion that goes through the neck (approach via the neck). 4. SLN positive.
- N1-N2a: choose between selective or comprehensive ND. N3: comprehensive ND.

### Therapeutic neck dissection (p.336)
- Used when nodes are palpable or imaging shows positive nodes.
- CT features suspicious for pathologic nodes: 1. size >1.5 cm; 2. cystic or central necrosis; 3. round node; 4. peripheral enhancement.
- N1 and N2: selective or comprehensive ND; N3: comprehensive ND.

### Radical neck dissection (RND) (p.336)
- Not often used now, including extended RND.
- Besides removing nodes of levels 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, RND removes the SCM, internal jugular vein and CN 11; even the cervical plexus, submandibular gland and parotid tail are removed with it. Not removed: phrenic nerve and carotid artery.
- Indications still used: 1. multiple or extensive node involvement; 2. severe extracapsular spread.

### Modified radical neck dissection (MRND) (p.336)
- Neck disease control as good as RND, but better functional outcome and cosmetics.
- Structures removed are mostly the same as RND; 3 types depending on what is preserved: Type 1: preserve CN 11 only; Type 2: preserve CN 11 and SCM; Type 3: preserve all 3: CN 11, internal jugular vein and SCM.
- Of the 3, CN 11 should be preserved most.
- If bilateral MRND is needed, preserve at least one internal jugular vein; ligating both causes raised intracranial pressure, swollen face and neck, airway swelling and breathing difficulty, possibly death; also ligating both IJVs may cause blindness from central retinal vein occlusion; SIADH has been reported too.

### Selective neck dissection (SND) (p.336)
- Removes nodes by the drainage pattern of a specific primary site, removing only what is necessary, so cosmetic and shoulder function are better than with comprehensive ND.
- SND types:
  1. Supraomohyoid ND (SOHND): removes levels 1, 2, 3 (above the hyoid bone's omohyoid); suits N0 oral cavity cancer.
  2. Extended SOHND: as SOHND plus level 4; both used for oral cavity cancer.
  3. Lateral ND (LND): removes levels 2, 3, 4.
  4. Posterolateral ND: as LND plus level 5; suits cancers of the pharynx, larynx and thyroid.
  5. Central ND: removes level 6; commonly used in thyroid cancer surgery.
- Remember: an SND should yield at least 6 nodes for examination, while RND and MRND must yield at least 10 nodes.

| Type of neck dissection | Structures removed |
|---|---|
| Classic radical ND | All lymph-bearing tissue (levels I-V), spinal accessory nerve (CN XI), SCM, internal jugular vein |
| Modified radical ND | ND with sparing of one or more of the above |
| Type I | CN XI spared |
| Type II | CN XI and internal jugular vein spared |
| Type III (functional ND) | All three spared (CN XI, IJV, SCM) |
| Selective: lateral | Levels II-IV |
| Selective: posterolateral | Levels II-V |
| Selective: supraomohyoid | Levels I-III |
(Source cited in book: Medina and Rebual, Cummings Otolaryngology. Note the book's table lists Type II as sparing CN XI and IJV, whereas the text above says Type 2 spares CN 11 and SCM; both are as printed.)

### Contraindications for neck dissection (p.338)
- Absolute: 1. patient too unfit, can only be given a tube; 2. Horner syndrome; 3. invasion of phrenic nerve, vagus nerve or brachial plexus; 4. prevertebral muscle invasion.

### Operative technique (p.338-339)
1. Incision: 1.1 hockey stick (half apron): the line continues from the cervical collar line up to the side; suits MRND and can be extended bilaterally; 1.2 modified apron: U shape across the middle of the neck, suits SOHND, also bilateral; 1.3 MacFee: pretty but poor exposure and may cause lymphedema.
2. Subplatysmal plane dissection: upper to the mandible; lower to the clavicle; anterior to the omohyoid in the submental area; posterior to the trapezius edge.
3. Sharp dissection; avoid injury to vital structures.
4. Take the fat pad and nodes off from below upward, from medial toward lateral and posterior.
5. Before closing do not forget a closed suction drain.
6. Beware at level 4: on the left behind the internal jugular vein is the thoracic duct (enters there); keep eyes sharp.
7. Watch good teachers and you will learn MRND is only slightly different (a few levels); for thyroid, salivary gland or skin cancer with clinically palpable nodes a general surgeon with training could do MRND.

## Metastatic cancer of unknown primary (p.339-340)
- Difficult, challenging problem.
- Workup of an asymptomatic unilateral neck mass: history, physical examination of the oral cavity, oropharynx, nasopharynx, hypopharynx, larynx, thyroid, salivary gland, skin; if suspicious of a primary in the ENT area do fiberoptic examination; panendoscopy = laryngoscopy, esophagoscopy, nasopharyngoscopy and bronchoscopy; wherever a suspicious lesion is found take a biopsy; occult cancer often hides at the tonsil, piriform sinus, base of tongue and nasopharynx, so take blind (random) biopsies.
- If nothing found do FNA, the most important workup (sensitivity 92%, specificity 98%): choose it as a single choice; beware blood vessels, use US guidance for safety.
- Remember: avoid excisional biopsy (a wrong approach if done first) because it creates problems when cervical lymph node dissection is done later.
- FNA negative: open biopsy then treat according to result.
- FNA positive: reports SCC 70-85%, adenocarcinoma 13-22%, undifferentiated (anaplastic) 10%; the rest are thyroid carcinoma, lymphoma, melanoma.
- SCC has good prognosis (25% survive >5 years), adenocarcinoma has poor prognosis, dies quickly.
- If CT finds a cystic node beware it may come from tonsil, base of tongue or cervical esophagus.
- For SCC, if guessing the primary, think of the tonsil first: higher chance of being the primary than others.
- SCC case: CT chest, CT or MRI from skull base to thoracic inlet; if all routine methods find no primary, how to proceed is still debated: some advise random biopsy through the nasopharynx, choana, piriform sinus and consider bilateral tonsillectomy, believing a primary may be found in up to 25%; if still no primary, treat as a group. Others say treat the node at once without waiting for the primary, since the primary may be found later, and keep treating the node first, then manage the primary when it emerges.
- Neck node treatment depends on N stage: early lesion: ND, or if unfit, RT; advanced lesion, N2 or N3: combined treatment LND + RT; if inoperable, give chemo.
- Adenocarcinoma: the primary is usually far away: 1. in men usually lung, prostate, pancreas; in women usually breast and ovary; 2. treatment is usually combined treatment, but only if the primary is from breast does it look better; otherwise use palliative or non-harmful methods.

## Neurological complications (p.340-342)
- Trigeminal nerve (CN V): motor to mastication muscles; sensory to the whole face via V1, V2, V3 (V1 ophthalmic, V2 maxillary, V3 mandibular). Figure: motor root, sensory root, chorda tympani, facial nerve; sensory territories.
- V3 is important: the mandibular nerve gives the important lingual nerve, which may be cut when removing the submandibular gland; if cut, the anterior tongue and the floor of mouth lose sensation (numbness, no feeling).
- Facial nerve (CN VII): exits the stylomastoid foramen into the parotid and divides it into superficial and deep lobes; inside the parotid about 1.5 cm it divides into the upper temporofacial trunk and the lower cervicofacial trunk, which subdivide into 5 branches:
  1. Temporal branch: frontalis muscle (raises eyebrow).
  2. Zygomatic branch: orbicularis oculi (eye closure); preserve this branch above all; if cut the eyelid cannot close, eye dries and may go blind.
  3. Buccal branch: muscles of the upper lip.
  4. Mandibular branch: lower lip, especially in submandibular gland surgery; if cut the corner of the mouth droops; prevention: incision should be about 2 cm below the lower border of the mandible.
  5. Cervical branch: platysma.
- Finding CN VII: 1. at the tympanomastoid suture at the back of the ear, find it at its origin; 2. retrograde dissection: for those who prefer, find the mandibular branch running below and parallel to the lower border of the mandible about 1 cm, crossing the common facial vessel; this approach suits large tumors.
- Glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX): sensation of the posterior 1/3 of the tongue; motor to stylopharyngeus; if cut: numbness of the tongue base, swallowing difficulty and absent gag reflex.
- Vagus nerve (CN X): runs down between the internal jugular vein and carotid artery; when ligating the vein, beware CN X; if cut the cord is paralyzed (see thyroid chapter, complications of thyroidectomy).
- Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI): leaves the jugular foramen, runs down near level 2 under the SCM obliquely, lying above the internal jugular vein, then passes the posterior border of the SCM to supply the trapezius. Figure: cranial roots, spinal roots C1-C6, external branch to SCM and trapezius.
  - A pure motor nerve; if cut there is no sensory loss. Usually injured at neck dissection (node clearance at the neck). Result: shoulder drop, arm abduction cannot be raised, trapezius atrophy, often with neuropathic pain; if both are cut the patient cannot dress, needing a relative's help.
  - CN XI injury is often diagnosed late; if the nerve is cut at surgery, repair improves functional outcome. Remember: do not cut any structure larger than 2 mm in the posterior triangle, since it may be this nerve.
  - Finding CN XI in the posterior triangle (level 5): 1. at the posterior border of the SCM it lies about 2 cm above the great auricular nerve; 2. lower, at the point where it enters the trapezius, it lies about 2 cm above the clavicle.
- Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII): exits the hypoglossal foramen at the skull base, curves down to levels 1 and 2 between the carotid bifurcation, then supplies all tongue muscles except palatoglossus. Beware injury in surgery at the carotid bifurcation: branchial cleft cyst and fistula type 2 (opening in front of SCM), SCC metastasis, carotid body tumor.
- Great auricular nerve: lies in the upper lateral neck, fairly superficial, often cut in parotid surgery; if cut: numbness at the ear and cheek.
- Phrenic nerve: lies deep, on the medial side of the middle scalene muscle; normally should not be injured unless the hand is careless and digs more than necessary.
- Auriculotemporal nerve: a branch of CN V; after parotid surgery, if this nerve is cut, Frey syndrome may result (read in the salivary gland chapter).

## Carotid artery rupture (p.343-344)
- Rare but the most serious, because 1/3 of patients die before treatment; mostly at the common carotid artery.
- Predisposing factors: 1. SCM removed; 2. previous neck RT; 3. untreated orocutaneous fistula; 4. Y incision over the carotid bifurcation.
- With skin necrosis and wound breakdown the carotid is exposed; like a "sentinel bleed": first small bleeding, then rupture with massive spurting that may kill.
- At rupture: control with a single finger, or insert a Foley catheter and blow the balloon, and tamponade temporarily; ordinary gauze packing does not work.
- If the patient has not died, rush to OR and do not try to repair: ligate at once, with a risk of hemiplegia about 50%.
- Discuss the outcome with relatives beforehand.
- Prevention: if the SCM must be removed, try to cover the carotid artery with other muscle flap beforehand.

## Complications of radiotherapy (p.344-345)
- Three to know: 1. xerostomia (dry mouth); 2. soft tissue necrosis; 3. osteoradionecrosis.

### Xerostomia (p.344)
- Dry mouth syndrome, often seen with RT for nasopharyngeal cancer, where the parotid also gets radiation, causing salivary gland dysfunction.
- Salivation begins to fall 2-3 days after RT starts; after about 5 weeks of RT, saliva stops permanently; luckily other salivary glands not irradiated may compensate.
- Treatment: conservative, mouth rinse and oral hygiene.

### Soft tissue necrosis (p.344-345)
- Mucosa and submucosa of the mouth become ischemic; easy ulceration; the wound is fairly flat, painful, tender.
- Treatment: try conservative: keep oral hygiene, no food debris; antibiotics may be needed; most heal within 2 weeks; remember: in the early period do not rush to biopsy, since the wound heals slowly.
- Beware cancer ulcer: it has a rolled edge, a painful ulcer with spread; if >2 weeks of conservative treatment fails, then biopsy.

### Osteoradionecrosis (p.345)
- Bone death from RT: bone becomes thin; if radiation >60 Gy the chance is about 2% of bone necrosis.
- Denser bone absorbs more radiation; in the face and neck the mandible gets radionecrosis often, especially with many teeth (more necrosis).
- Diagnosis: a non-healing wound with exposed brown dead bone at the base; painless unless infected; the mouth smells foul. Figure: extensive bone loss of the mandible on film.
- Treatment: 1. best conservative: oral hygiene, mouth rinse, wound irrigation, debride dead tissue and keep food debris out; if infected give antibiotic; healing about 50%; some use hyperbaric oxygen to increase oxygen in tissue, which may help. 2. Do not operate in the first 2-3 months, because such an avascular wound will not heal; consider surgery if conservative treatment for 2-3 months fails: debride the dead tissue and bone, close the defect with a muscle flap, and avoid bone graft.

## Points to remember (book p.346)
1. Neck mass in children: 90% benign, 10% cancer, especially lymphoma.
2. Neck mass in adults: the rule of 80: 80% of non-thyroid masses are tumor; 80% of tumors are cancer; 80% of adult neck masses larger than 2 cm are cancer; 80% of cancers are SCC; 80% of primary lesions of SCC lie above the clavicle.
3. A neck node in an HIV patient is inflammation or hyperplasia more than tumor; if tumor, number one is B cell lymphoma, number 2 Kaposi sarcoma.
4. Bad teeth, poor oral hygiene is a risk factor for cancer but betel chewing (mouth) is a risk for cancer of the cheek and tongue, not the nose.
5. Cause of death in head and neck cancer is usually local invasion.
6. Head and neck cancer likes to spread to lymph nodes; distant metastasis is seen infrequently; lung metastasis is the commonest, then bone, liver, brain.
7. CT or MRI is the investigation of choice for a unilateral nasal mass since it may be a vascular tumor such as angiofibroma, for which biopsy may be dangerous.
8. Do not do excisional biopsy of a neck node directly, because: 8.1 if the result is SCC, you do not know where it came from; 8.2 skin contamination gives high recurrence; 8.3 MRND becomes harder since the previous incision must be removed; 8.4 RT after surgery is always needed unnecessarily.
9. Safe margin of oral cavity cancer surgery is 1-2 cm in all 3 dimensions.
10. A mouth ulcer lasting more than 3 weeks should be biopsied.
11. Summary treatment of oral cavity cancer:
   11.1 For cancers at the sites named last (nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx) with N0 or node-negative: answer RT; if N+ (N1-3): concurrent chemoRT (the best one); salvage surgery is for residual disease.
   11.2 Surgical treatment of WLE is almost 100%, but with invasion of or attachment to the mandible do mandibulectomy.
   11.3 T3 N0 or above: do LND in every case.
   11.4 T2 N0, except lip and hard palate only (node risk only 10%, so LND not needed); otherwise do LND for all.
   11.5 All T1 N0: no LND.
   11.6 A midline cancer with chance of spreading to both sides, e.g. FOM: do bilateral LND.
   11.7 T3 or above (size >4 cm) needs adjuvant RT after surgery.
