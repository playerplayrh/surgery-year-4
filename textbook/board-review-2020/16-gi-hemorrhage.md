# Gastrointestinal Bleeding 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 914-956 (PDF pages 918-960)

## Introduction (book p.914)
- GI bleeding from mouth to anus is a topic a surgical resident must know; patients come almost daily; when taking after-hours GI-bleeding consults it is like holding a bomb without knowing when it will go off or when help will come.
- The chapter uses standard textbooks together with the author's own experience; a resident should adapt it to the real situation, especially provincial hospitals with few staff and limited readiness (a "center 5-star" cannot be assumed).

## Upper GI bleeding (UGIB) (p.914)
- A common emergency; average mortality 3.5%; a leading cause of death in peptic ulcer patients.
- Luckily 80% stop bleeding by themselves.
- UGI bleeding means bleeding from a site above the ligament of Treitz (duodenojejunal junction).
- Etiology: no. 1 is PU 48% (usually DU bleeds more than GU, but in the elderly GU > DU); next: varices, gastritis, duodenitis, esophagitis, AVM, etc.

### Variceal vs non-variceal bleeding
| Variceal | Non-variceal |
|---|---|
| Painless bleeding | Pain or painless |
| Usually hematemesis | Hematemesis or melena |
| >90% tachycardia and hypotension | Vary |
| >90% Hct <30% | Vary |
| Signs of chronic liver disease | Without signs of chronic liver disease |

### Basics: vomit, stool, blood (p.914-915)
- Melena: black, tarry stool from degradation of hematin by enteric bacteria; smell is very foul; wet, sticky like rubber tar; needs bleeding of about 10 cc for FOB positive and about 50 cc for black stool; bleeding from small bowel or colon may also give melena if blood stays in the gut >12 hours.
- Maroon stool: dark red-brown/maroon, like blood and feces; bleeding site is usually small bowel or right colon.
- Hematochezia: stool is red, flowing blood like a ripe fruit color; if from UGI the bleed must be >750-1,000 cc to appear red; if from right colon only 175 cc makes the stool red.

### Medical and drug history (p.915)
- NSAIDs and ASA can cause ulceration anywhere in the GI tract.
- Anticoagulation therapy, especially at supratherapeutic level, may cause spontaneous mucosal bleeding.
- History of Crohn's disease or cancer: in obscure bleeding suspect recurrent disease.
- History of large hiatal hernia: mechanical trauma causes mucosal tears (Cameron's erosion).
- History of AAA with open repair with graft: bleeding from aortoduodenal fistula.
- History of angiectasia: new lesions may develop anywhere in the GI tract.

## Approach to UGIB (p.915-)
- Initial treatment: use the same principle as trauma, ATLS: look at breathing, beware of aspirated blood, record vital signs, treat shock, correct coagulopathy, insert Foley catheter, cross-match blood; do not over-resuscitate, since it makes bleeding worse.
- History: duration and frequency, which may help tell the cause and the severity of bleeding: 1. history of drinking alcohol, jaundice, taking NSAIDs, anticoagulants; 2. history of liver injury, may bleed from hemobilia; 3. amount, including the color of the vomitus; bright red means the bleeding is probably severe.
- Physical examination: 1. vital signs: tachycardia, hypotension: a bad sign; 2. signs of chronic liver disease; 3. PR: look at the stool color: fresh red, flowing, black and sticky, any hemorrhoid or mass.
- Lab: 1. Hct, platelet count, LFT, PT, PTT, blood chemistry, cross-matching; 2. CXR, EKG, especially in the elderly.
- Insert an NG tube to see what the content is; if no blood is seen, think of LGIB or a UGIB that has stopped already.

### Gastric lavage or not
- Whether to do it routinely: consider case by case; many fear lavage may dislodge a clot or that bleeding that has stopped will rebleed; but large studies found this untrue; so advise lavage with tap water, since it has 3 benefits: 1. helps confirm it is really UGIB; 2. evaluates whether the bleeding is still active (bright red = very active); 3. prepares for gastroscopy, otherwise the scope sees only blood.
- Acid suppression: PPI is clearly better than H2 blocker; give PPI as soon as possible; PPI keeps pH >4 so that pepsin does not lyse the clot, and PPI also stabilizes the clot so platelet aggregation is firmer; dose of PPI as intensive therapy: 80 mg IV bolus + 8 mg/hr IV drip for 72 hr.
- Where to admit, ward or ICU: 1. ordinary ward: hemodynamically stable, or EGD shows only clean base or pigmented spot; 2. ICU: hemodynamically unstable, significant comorbid illness, or very active bleeding, e.g. red NG tube, hematochezia, or Hct drop >6% (the author adds that at his hospital ICU beds are few, expensive and hard to get).
- After admitting and controlling the situation until the patient and relatives are stable, assess severity: low risk or high risk, using prognostic factors.

## Prognostic factors (p.916-918)
- 2 big groups: high risk group, which has a chance of rebleeding and death, and a chance of urgent operation; and low risk group, who are safe, most treated like an OPD case and sent home.
1. Clinical prognostic factors (Rockall score): if positive more than 4 items (below), the chance of death is 22.4%; fewer than 4 items, death 3.2% (remember): 1.1 age >60 years: prognosis not good; 1.2 HR and BP, shock or not: if shock or prolonged shock, bad; 1.3 comorbid disease such as heart or liver disease: bad; 1.4 severity of bleeding: active or oozing continuously; 1.5 blood transfusion >=6 units: bad.
2. Endoscopic prognostic factors: from the appearance of the ulcer at endoscopy using the Forrest classification, in grade 1a, 1b, 2a: the recommendation of the GI medicine group is to consult a surgeon or someone with experience early.

| Forrest | Appearance | Grade | Rebleeding (%) | Chance of surgery (%) | MTR (%) | Endoscopic treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clean base | | III | <3 | 0.5 | 2 | Observe |
| Ulcer with black spot | flat spot | IIc | <8 | 6 | 3 | Observe |
| Adherent clot | | IIb | 22 | 10 | 7 | +/- |
| Non-bleeding visible vessel or end on artery | | IIa | 43 | 34 | 11 | Must treat |
| Active bleeding, spurting or oozing | | Ia, Ib | >55 | 35 | 11 | Must treat |
- Alternative table in the figure (risk of rebleeding): I active hemorrhage: Ia spurting 85-100%; Ib oozing 10-27%. II signs of recent hemorrhage: IIa visible vessel 50%; IIb adherent clot 30-35%; IIc hematin-covered flat spot <8%. III no signs of hemorrhage, clean bed of ulcer <3%.
- Note: Forrest does not mention the size of the ulcer; the author thinks size matters: if >2 cm, there is a high chance of surgery.
- Combining clinical factors with endoscopic factors gives the Blatchford score (as printed) and a newer score, the Rockall score, which aims to tell the risk of rebleeding, the need for transfusion, and the chance of surgery and death, dividing patients into low and high risk; the higher the score, the worse (renal failure or being on a ventilator is extremely high risk).
- The author says no need to memorize these scores (they are for the examinee's brain); only Forrest classification must be remembered.

## Endoscopy (p.918-)
- Objectives: 1. diagnosis; 2. investigation, e.g. CLO test or biopsy of the stomach ulcer; 3. treatment.
- Before scope the patient should be stabilized first; protect the airway well, beware blood running into the lungs; in a confused or elderly patient who cannot protect himself, place an ET tube at once.
- Timing for endoscopy: 1. a stable patient, clinically low risk, stable vital signs: scope within 24 hours; studies found even with earlier scoping, e.g. within 6 hours, rebleeding, death and surgery chance do not differ; 2. but if the patient is older than 60 with shock, rapid heart rate, vomiting fresh red blood: scope within 6-12 hours.
- If the scope finds the cause and the bleeding spot and the bleeding has stopped, the patient can take a PPI.
- Question: in a stable patient is a second look scope needed to be sure bleeding really stopped? Answer: not necessary, no second look; start feeding within 24 hours and then send home with oral PPI; high-risk patients must not be sent home: admit at least 3 days with IV high-dose PPI, which reduces rebleeding and the need for surgery.
- Indications for repeated endoscopy: 1. rebleeding (remember)
  2. The first look was unclear, possibly because of heavy bleeding; 3. the patient must resume anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy.

## Therapeutic endoscopy (p.919-921)
- By Forrest classification, the lesions that need treatment are active bleeding and non-bleeding visible vessel.
- Adherent clot: whether to treat a clot still sitting there is debated; some say leave it, it has stopped bleeding; others say scope people are itchy and will poke at it; recommended: irrigate with water first; if the clot is tightly stuck and does not come off, either of 2 approaches is acceptable since RCTs support both: 1. leave it: withdraw the scope, give PPI high dose 80 mg IV over 5 minutes followed by PPI continuous infusion 8 mg/hr for 72 hours; 2. remove the clot: inject adrenaline around the clot, then gradually cut the clot with a snare; if a visible vessel is found, treat at once; use this method only if experienced; if the clot is at a site hard to scope, e.g. posterior duodenal bulb wall, there must be OR, ICU and blood in the bank ready, and the tube large enough; if it fails, go to the OR.
- First scope: stops bleeding in 75% and 15-20% rebleed; if rebleeding, repeat scope treatment can stop bleeding again in 50-73% (remember).
- After scope treatment, biopsy the antrum for H. pylori.
- Rebleeding is usually found with: ulcer larger than 2 cm; vessel larger than 2 mm; GD artery bleeding.

### Endoscopic techniques (p.919-921)
1. Injection: relies on a tamponade effect, so the type of solvent is not important as much as the injection method: inject 4 points at 0.5 cc each until a white sign is seen; bleeding usually slows or stops temporarily; when the effect wears off it may bleed again, so injection alone is not enough; do not be content, quickly apply a clip or heater probe next. Solvents used: adrenaline 1:10,000, alcohol, glue, NSS, sclerosing agent; most commonly adrenaline 1:10,000.
2. Thermal coagulation: 2.1 contact: monopolar, bipolar, laser, microwave and heater probe; 2.2 non-contact: APC (argon plasma coagulator), which coagulates only about 2 mm deep, so it suits superficial lesions such as antral vascular ectasia or watermelon stomach; APC is not suitable for active bleeding from a deep ulcer.
3. Mechanical technique: hemoclip, especially suited to bleeding that is spurting or a visible vessel.
4. Combination treatment: popular: injection mixed with another method gives the best result in stopping bleeding.

## Surgical management (p.921-)
- Indications for surgery: 1. hemodynamically unstable, persistent hypotension; 2. no one to scope, or there is but not available; 3. little blood in the blood bank, especially a rare group; 4. rebleeding after endoscopic treatment 2 times; 5. continuous bleeding requiring transfusion >4-6 units per day or >6 units in the first 24 hours.
- In the past, before a second episode of bleeding in the same admission, surgery was advised at once; now one may try endoscopic treatment once more first; but if bleeding again, the patient has to be taken to the OR, since elderly patients recover poorly; embolization may be tried first, but if the patient is unstable, take the patient to surgery.
- Consider surgery earlier if UGIB fails endoscopic treatment and: 1. age >60 years; 2. admitted with shock; 3. ulcer larger than 2 cm; 4. DU at the duodenal bulb and GU at the lesser curve: those places erode an artery and bleeding hardly stops by itself.
- Aim of surgery: control bleeding securely, more important than preventing ulcer recurrence.
- The surgical method for bleeding DU & GU differs in each text; the author prefers Shackelford 2019 and Schwartz 2019 as more practical.

## Bleeding DU (p.922)
- The most common complication of DU.
- If we know before surgery from endoscopy that it is a bleeding DU: operate, open the duodenum and suture the bleeding point to stop; if the patient's condition is good, do definitive surgery right away, which helps reduce the rebleeding rate; but if poor risk, hurry: suturing to stop bleeding alone is enough.
- Procedure: suture ligation together with: 1. TV + P: the most popular now; ulcer recurrence 10%; 2. TV + A: ulcer recurrence <1% but not suitable in an emergency; 3. HSV: ulcer recurrence 10-20%, takes long, not suitable in an emergency.
- Figure: bleeding duodenal ulcer -> suture ligation of the bleeding vessels, pyloroplasty, and truncal vagotomy.
- TV and HSV are done to reduce acid secretion from vagal stimulation and acetylcholine; nowadays more people doubt whether TV is needed, since postoperatively treating H. pylori and stopping NSAID can reduce rebleeding quite well.
- Antrectomy removes the source of G cells that secrete gastrin.
- Technique of suturing the GD artery: a 3-point ligation: points 1 and 2: upper and lower stitches to control GD artery bleeding; point 3: a central U-stitch to control the transverse pancreatic artery. (Figure: duodenotomy with 3 sutures.)

## Bleeding GU (p.923)
- Figure: the 5 GU types with operations (as in the stomach chapter: Type I partial gastrectomy and Billroth I, or ulcer excision and HSV; Type II similar to duodenal ulcer: truncal vagotomy and antrectomy, or HSV; Type III truncal vagotomy and antrectomy; Type IV if ulcer within 2 cm of GE junction Kelly-Madlener or Csendes, for more distal lesions Pauchet's procedure; Type V if medication cannot be stopped, excision and truncal vagotomy).
- Low risk: 1. type 1, type 4: partial or distal gastrectomy including the ulcer; 2. type 2, type 3: do as for type 1 and type 4 but add TV.
- High risk: do only suturing to stop bleeding together with biopsy of the ulcer, accepting that rebleeding is 20-40%.

## Stress gastritis (p.923-)
- Found in critically ill patients: on ventilator, MOF, severe burn: small bleeding points all over the stomach.
- Mechanism: believed to arise from reduced gastric mucosal blood flow, e.g. in shock, so the gastric mucosa becomes ischemic then ulcerates.
- Remember: stress gastritis is not related to hyperacidity or H. pylori infection.
- Found less nowadays because of better care of patients.
- Often bleeds within 2 days after major trauma, ARDS, SIRS.
- Bleeding site: usually the fundus area (remember); if severe, it extends down to the distal stomach.
- The lesion is only in the mucosa layer, shallow, often multiple sites; high chance of bleeding but low chance of perforation.
- Cushing ulcer: an ulcer found in patients with intracranial injury or brain surgery; usually a single ulcer but rather deep, so a high chance of perforation.
- Curling ulcer: a stress ulcer found in burn patients.
- Treatment of stress ulcer bleeding: 1. gastric lavage (NG lavage) then send for scope and try to stop bleeding with injection plus heater probe or APC; 2. if that fails, try embolization of the left gastric artery or infusion of vasopressin; 3. if still not controlled, operate: open a gastrotomy and suture the bleeding points; 4. if after suturing it still cannot be controlled, harsher measures: 4.1 localized bleeding: partial gastrectomy; 4.2 diffuse bleeding: subtotal or total gastrectomy; 4.3 a newer option: gastric devascularization by ligating almost all arterial blood supply, leaving only the short gastric vessels; reported to have lower mortality than total gastrectomy and rebleeding 10%; advised for patients who are not stable at the time of surgery or have many comorbidities; 4.4 Sabiston 2017 says TV/P is enough (the author agrees).
- PPI use: by mechanism PPI would not seem to help much, but in common practice it is still given; Schwartz 2015 says at least keeping intragastric pH >4 to stabilize the clot helps reduce bleeding.

## Mallory-Weiss syndrome (p.924-925)
- Occurs after drinking alcohol followed by violent vomiting, causing a mucosal tear.
- Appearance and site of tear: a linear partial-thickness tear along the length of the lesser curve below the EGJ; found less at the greater curve. (Figures: Mallory-Weiss tear oozing; hemostasis with heater probe.)
- The patient vomits blood after retching; diagnosis by gastroscope; luckily bleeding usually stops by itself >90%.
- Treatment: 1. if bleeding has stopped: supportive treatment with PPI; 2. if still bleeding or a visible vessel is seen: treat through the scope with a heater probe or clip as convenient; if it does not stop, repeat endoscopic treatment once more; 3. less than 1% fail endoscopic hemostasis and are operated on: 3.1 a stable patient: open a long proximal gastrotomy, but not across the EGJ, then suture and ligate the bleeding point; do not do a definitive acid-reducing surgery (remember); 3.2 consider embolization or vasopressin if the patient cannot tolerate surgery.

## Dieulafoy's lesion (p.925)
- Found <2%: an abnormality of a submucosal artery that protrudes through a small mucosal defect; when erosion occurs it bleeds; found most in the stomach; small bowel and large bowel less.
- Location: usually within 5 cm of the EGJ: 98% in the proximal stomach.
- Clinical: found in middle-aged men with chronic intermittent bleeding, bleeding then stopping; during bleeding it is usually active more than occult.
- Endoscopy: a flat or protuberant vessel; if not bleeding may look normal, but when bleeding there is arterial spurting while the surrounding mucosa shows no abnormality (remember). (Figure: bleeding Dieulafoy; non-bleeding, only a small vessel mound.)
- Treatment: 1. endoscopic injection of adrenaline and tattoo to mark it, so that if surgery is needed it is easy to find; 2. embolization: 2nd choice; 3. surgery: the last resort: open and suture, or wedge resection; remember: no need for an acid-reducing procedure.

## Watermelon stomach (p.926)
- Sabiston calls it gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE): a vascular ectasia like angiodysplasia of the right colon; found often in old women who bleed at the antrum; since it is venous bleeding it is not active like Dieulafoy.
- 25% of patients have chronic liver disease and an association with autoimmune disorder.
- Bleeding is chronic blood loss, not spurting as in Dieulafoy.
- Endoscope: looks similar to portal gastropathy but portal gastropathy is found mostly in the proximal stomach and is treated like portal hypertension, whereas watermelon is found at the distal stomach as many small bleeding points all over. (Figures: watermelon; scope view; APC.)
- Treatment: 1. endoscopic treatment with APC works well; 2. if surgery is needed, antrectomy is the usual choice to control bleeding.

## Leiomyoma and leiomyosarcoma (p.926)
- A soft tissue tumor in the submucosa that often bleeds from central ulceration. (Figures: bleeding tumor with central ulceration.)
- Symptoms: epigastric pain for weeks or months, then UGI bleeding.
- Treatment: wedge resection with margin 2-3 cm; if locally invading adjacent structures, do en bloc but no lymphadenectomy needed.

## Aorto-enteric fistula (p.927)
- (Figure: aortic graft eroding into the duodenum (L1-L5), fistula, side view; endoscopic view of graft at duodenum; operative photo.)
- At first it bleeds as a warning but not very active; on EGD one sees the aortic graft at the duodenum; do not delay: act quickly; soon after, bleeding will be as if a dam burst, with a high chance of death.
- Treatment: open and remove the graft, close the fistula, and make an extra-anatomical bypass (axillobifemoral), or use an antibiotic-impregnated in situ graft (details in the arterial disease chapter). (Figures: axillobifemoral graft.)

## Hemobilia (p.928)
- Found rarely, usually after liver biopsy, ERCP or TIPS.
- Clinical triad: biliary colic, obstructive jaundice, melena.
- Treatment: angiographic embolization. (Figures: duodenoscopy showing blood from the ampulla; angiography.)

## Hemosuccus pancreaticus (p.928)
- A rare cause of UGI bleeding: bleeding from the pancreas, whatever the cause (pancreatitis, pseudocyst, cancer), flowing into the main pancreatic duct and out through the ampulla. (Figure: EGD shows blood coming out of the ampulla.)
- Symptoms: abdominal pain, black stool, past history of pancreatitis.
- CT shows extravasation of contrast into a pseudocyst.
- Treatment: angiographic embolization; if it fails and the bleeding site drains from the distal part, then distal pancreatectomy.

## Summary of UGI bleeding and practical points (p.929)
- Treatment principles follow standard textbooks; the author's summary from his own feeling, teachers' advice and current practice:
  1. DU: do less; less is often better.
  2. GU: operate according to the modified Johnson type; do not forget tissue biopsy at the ulcer edge to rule out cancer.
  3. DU with complication should have H. pylori eradication.
- If we know exactly the bleeding site from gastroscope: when we must open the abdomen to find the bleeding point it is not hard; but if scope was not possible beforehand because the patient must be rushed to surgery, or off-hours scope is not available: the site, DU or GU, may show from outside as a scar or an abnormal wall; if intraoperative gastroscope is possible, so much the better.
- In some cases looking from outside shows no hard ulcer or abnormal wall, e.g. bleeding from acute gastritis or stress ulcer, and scope cannot be done in the OR: open a pyloroduodenotomy first; if a cause such as DU bleeding is found, deal with it; but if the distal side does not show the cause of UGIB, open the stomach at 2 sites proximally: the author advises a gastrotomy at the body on the anterior side, gradually along the greater curve then extending upward to the side, so the whole stomach interior can be examined; beware missing the EGJ area.
- Controlling bleeding can be done in 4 ways: 1. open a gastroduodenotomy then suture-ligate from the lumen side; 2. extraluminal ligation of the vessels around the stomach; 3. ulcer excision: suitable for bleeding GU; 4. gastric resection including the ulcer.
- If on gastrotomy bleeding has stopped, with only an adherent clot stuck at the base of the ulcer: remove the clot, wipe and wash it; even if the ulcer is found not bleeding, he still advises suture ligation.
- Figure (algorithm): acute upper GI hemorrhage: consider orotracheal intubation; fluid resuscitation, nasogastric tube, labs (type and cross match, hematocrit, coags), history (liver failure, alcohol, PUD, NSAID intake). Massive hemorrhage preventing endoscopy -> OR. Monitored setting: IV H2 blocker/PPI, Foley -> endoscopy: positive: varices (see variceal figure); DU/GU: endoscopic control by electrocoagulation or injection: succeeds -> observe; fails, rebleeds -> OR; AGML: medical management: succeeds / fails -> OR; Mallory-Weiss tear: endoscopic therapy. Negative: evaluate for LGIB.

# Lower GI bleeding (LGIB) (book p.930-)
- Means the cause of GI bleeding lies below the ligament of Treitz; the patient usually presents with hematochezia or blood clots out of the anus.
- Definitions: 1. acute LGIB: bleeding within 3 days; 2. chronic LGIB: bleeding more than 3 days; 3. massive LGIB: bleeding with unstable vital signs, needing blood >=4 units; 4. occult LGIB: bleeding not visible to the eye, found only by FOB; 5. obscure LGIB: bleeding for which we have not found the cause.
- Luckily it is found less than UGIB (UGIB : LGIB : OGIB = 75 : 20 : 5), but LGIB treatment is troublesome, since the bleeding point is hard to find and hard to treat; LGIB patients are often elderly with many diseases.
- In 6% of LGIB, the bleeding point is never found, and exploration is needed.
- LGIB treated conservatively stops in 80-90%; rebleeding 10-20%.
- Features of severe bleeding: 1. patients older than 70 years; 2. taking aspirin; 3. heart rate >100; 4. SBP <115 (faint).
- If bleeding is slow, blood stays in the colon long, making the stool black like melena, so one may think UGIB.
- Location: colon 80-85%; anorectal 10%; small bowel 5%; the rest unknown.
- Etiology by age: 1. young adult: no. 1 Meckel, then IBD and polyp; 2. adult up to 60 years: no. 1 colonic diverticulum, then IBD and tumor; 3. age >60 years: no. 1 angiodysplasia, then diverticulum and tumor.
- Etiology by character of bleeding: 1. acute LGIB: think diverticulum, angiodysplasia, ischemic colitis, anal disease such as hemorrhoid; 2. chronic LGIB: think tumor, angiodysplasia, IBD.

## Management (p.931-932)
1. Initial: like UGIB: assess the patient, resuscitation and stabilization; once the patient is stable, talk to the relatives, then find the cause of bleeding.
2. History: IBD, taking NSAID, taking anticoagulant, suspicion of colon cancer or family history of cancer, IBD, previous AAA surgery or not (ask as a package).
3. Physical exam: look with an anoscope, rule out anal bleeding; if not use a speculum for the vagina... go to item 4 (as printed).
4. Next, rule out UGIB, especially in massive bleeding with bright red stool: about 10-15% of those thought to be LGIB turn out to be from UGIB; the easy way is to place an NG tube then irrigate: if the NG shows bright red, it is UGIB: do upper endoscopy and treat along UGIB lines.
5. But if the NG is clear or contains bile, then UGIB is unlikely; the GI bleeding in this case is LGIB: next step colonoscopy.
6. Nowadays colonoscope works well: with a working channel we can inject, clip, or if surgery is needed we find the lesion more easily without wasting time.
7. If colonoscopy does not see the bleeding point because there is a lot of blood, bad bleeding, blood blocking the view: advise bowel preparation with polyethylene glycol (PEG) via NG tube, waiting for the stool to come out as water; so nowadays massive LGIB is no longer a contraindication to colonoscopy.
8. If colonoscopy finds the bleeding point, treat through the scope with injection, thermal or mechanical modalities such as clip.
9. But if colonoscope does not find a bleeding point, or the bleeding is so active it blocks the view: send for scan & angiogram next; if lucky and we see the bleeding point on angiogram, treat with vasopressin infusion or embolization.
10. If angiogram or scan still does not find the bleeding point and the patient is still stable, suspect small bowel bleeding; consider push scope or capsule endoscopy.
11. If suspecting bleeding from Meckel's, send a Meckel scan.
12. Try everything, if the bleeding point is still not found or the patient is unstable, the situation is critical, then one must go in and do an intraoperative endoscopy: measure with the OR.
- American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) 2014 classifies bleeding from the rectum into 4 types as a guide to investigation (from Shackelford 2019 p.1815): figure: lower GI bleed -> occult bleed: colonoscopy; negative colonoscopy + UGI symptoms: EGD. Melena: EGD; negative EGD: colonoscopy. Scant intermittent hematochezia: age <40 AND no symptoms concerning for malignancy: flexible sigmoidoscopy (negative -> colonoscopy); age >50 OR symptoms concerning for malignancy: colonoscopy. Severe hematochezia (see next figure).
- Scant intermittent hematochezia: fresh red blood out of the anus, intermittent, disappearing, for days or weeks; they divide patients into 2 groups: the first group aged less than 40 without alarm symptoms of cancer: advised digital rectal exam, anoscopy, flexible sigmoidoscopy is probably enough; the other group aged >50 or with alarm symptoms of cancer: colonoscopy.

## Guidelines in standard textbooks (p.933)
- Since lower GI bleeding is an unglamorous topic few update it; most texts still use the guideline that came out around the year 2000; the author takes them for viewing; the one he likes, Shackelford, is the most up to date.
- Guideline in management of LGI bleeding (Schwartz 11e 2019, figure): acute colonic bleeding -> volume resuscitation plus blood transfusion -> NG aspirate negative / positive (positive: gastroduodenoscopy + endoscopic treatment) -> proctoscopy, rule out anorectal bleeding -> three branches: 
  - bleeding stopped or slowed down: elective colonoscopy: positive: endoscopic treatment (rebleeding: segmental resection); negative: observe (rebleeding: see moderate or massive bleeding);
  - moderate bleeding continued: (either) 99mTc RBC scintigraphy: positive: mesenteric arteriography: positive: vasopressin or emboli (fail: segmental resection); negative: explore, intraoperative endoscopy: positive: segmental resection; negative: total colectomy; scintigraphy negative: explore, intraoperative endoscopy: positive: segmental resection; negative: total colectomy; (or) urgent colonoscopy: positive: colonoscopic treatment or explore, segmental resection; negative: explore, intraoperative endoscopy: positive: segmental resection; negative: total colectomy;
  - massive life-threatening bleeding: mesenteric arteriography: positive: vasopressin or emboli (fail: segmental resection); negative: explore, intraoperative endoscopy: positive: segmental resection; negative: total colectomy.
- Steps of treatment in Schwartz: they rule out UGIB first with an NGT aspirate: 1. if bile-free blood is obtained, EGD then treat according to cause; 2. if not obtained, rule out anorectal bleeding by passing a proctoscope.
- Severity of bleeding is divided in 3 grades: 1. minor bleeding or bleeding that has stopped: elective colonoscopy; 2. moderate bleeding continued: 2 options: urgent colonoscopy or Tc RBC scan; 3. massive life-threatening bleeding: send for mesenteric angiography.

### Sabiston 20e 2017 guideline (p.934-935)
- Looking back, this guideline has been in use since Schwartz 9e; it was taken from Gordon & Nivatvongs, Surgery of the Colon, Rectum and Anus 2e 1999 (a proctology text), which is old: about 20 years; in that era RBC scan was still used and there was no CTA.
- Figure (algorithm): acute lower GI bleeding -> assess for anorectal outlet bleeding (digital rectal exam and anoscopy): yes -> initiate appropriate therapy; no -> rule out upper GI bleeding (NGT aspirate or EGD): positive -> upper GI bleeding algorithm; no -> minor bleeding (intermittent) or major bleeding (persistent).
  - Minor: colonoscopy: lesion visualized -> initiate appropriate therapy; no lesion visualized and/or continued bleeding -> small bowel series, enteroclysis, enteroscopy, capsule endoscopy: positive -> appropriate therapy; negative -> repeat colonoscopy if rebleeds.
  - Major: stable -> tagged RBC scan: positive -> angiography and treatment -> segmental resection; negative -> small bowel work-up as above. Unstable -> operating room: source uncertain -> localize bleeding: serial clamping or intraoperative enteroscopy followed by resection; colon or small bowel identified as source -> subtotal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis or small bowel resection.
- Steps in Sabiston differ from Schwartz: they rule out anorectal bleeding first with digital rectal examination together with anoscopy: 1. if bleeding is found, treat per cause; 2. if not, rule out UGI bleeding with NGT aspirate or EGD.
- Severity of bleeding is divided into 2 grades: 1. minor or intermittent bleeding: elective colonoscopy; 2. major (persistent) bleeding: 2 options: if the patient is still stable, Tc RBC scan; if unstable, send to the OR.
- (Looking at this guideline in edition 20, going back to older Sabiston, it is old, just like edition 19.)

### Harrison 20e guideline (p.935)
- The medical doctors' view: ASGE 2014, which Shackelford 2019 uses as a reference, originates from the GI medicine group; one interesting change: they took the tagged RBC scan out and put CTA in instead.
- Figure (management of lower GI bleeding, Harrison 20e): No hemodynamic instability: age <40: flexible sigmoidoscopy if minimal bleeding (some suggest colonoscopy for any degree of rectal bleeding in patients <40 as well), or colonoscopy if more copious bleeding, family history of colon cancer, iron deficiency; age >=40: colonoscopy. Then: site identified, bleeding stops; site identified, bleeding persists -> angiography -> bleeding persists -> surgery; site not identified -> work-up for small intestinal/obscure bleeding site. Hemodynamic instability: upper endoscopy; no upper GI source: able to prep -> colonoscopy; too unstable to prep -> angiography (may perform CT angiography first) -> bleeding persists -> (able to prep: back to colonoscopy) / instability persists -> surgery (with intraoperative endoscopy if site has not been identified).

### Guideline in management of severe hematochezia (Shackelford 8e) (p.936)
- In Shackelford 2019 the emphasis is massive hematochezia, the thing that makes resident blood hot at night and haunts them; the author adapts ASGE 2014 for residents handling consults; note RBC scan is gone and CTA takes its place.
- Reasons RBC scan is not preferred: it cannot pinpoint the bleeding point; the study takes long, up to 24-36 hours; those who are busy will go ahead first; while CTA is more available, takes little time, and gives data about where it bleeds and the cause.
- CTA sensitivity 86%, specificity 95%, reliable; so the author's personal opinion favors Shackelford.
- Figure (severe hematochezia): resuscitation and evaluation (large bore IV access, physical exam, CBC/coags/type and crossmatch) -> NGT lavage: positive -> EGD and management of UGI bleed; negative -> evidence of active bleeding? no -> bowel preparation and colonoscopy; yes -> hemodynamically stable? no -> resuscitation -> fluid responsive? yes -> CT angiography; no -> IR for angiography; yes -> CT angiography -> bleeding localized? yes -> directed colonoscopy with endoscopic therapy (epinephrine injection, bipolar or argon beam coagulation, hemoclips) -> persistent bleeding -> IR for angiography -> successful embolization -> observation; refractory bleeding -> surgery; bleeding localized? no -> bowel preparation and colonoscopy.

## Diagnostic modalities (p.936-)
- For localizing the site and cause of bleeding, several modalities; choose as appropriate.

### NG tube lavage (p.936-937)
- Helps rule out UGIB: if bile is seen, that bleed is likely from LGI; but if bright red blood is obtained, it is likely UGI: do EGD.
- Beware: in bleeding DU with pyloric stenosis the NGT lavage may be negative; if unsure and unable to rule out UGI bleeding, use upper endoscopy.

### Colonoscopy (p.937)
- Used for diagnosis, localization of the bleeding point, and efficient treatment; suited to non-massive bleeding; finds the cause of LGIB in about 45-90%.
- Optimal timing is still controversial; in the view of most: prep bowel quickly then do early colonoscopy within 6-24 hours; a patient to be scoped should not have very active bleeding, since nothing can be seen.
- When the bleeding point is found it can be treated by: 1. thermal: heater probe or laser; 2. injection: adrenaline or sclerosant; 3. metallic clip; 4. combined.
- Treatment by colonoscopy success 70-90%.
- LGIB cases suitable for colonoscope: bleeding slow or stopped; for moderate bleeding, scope if the patient is stable.
- In the past emergency colonoscopy did not work since blood blocked the view; now, if the endoscopist is good and the patient stable with good bowel prep, emergency scope can still be done.
- Emergency bowel prep method: 1. feed Na-phosphate solution or 0.9% NSS via NG tube, 2 liters in 2 hours; 2. do not use mannitol prep, since gas forms in the colon and may explode, especially when using heater probe cautery: dangerous, may blow up the bowel; 3. do not prep with enema or rectal wash, since it pushes blood into the right colon, making it harder to find the lesion.

### Radionuclide scan (p.937-938)
- (Figures: injection of tracer; scan images with arrow at bleeding point.)
- Technetium labeled with sulfur or RBC: good sensitivity but localization is not very good.
- In the past, when CTA did not exist, scan was used as a screening test to decide whether to proceed to angiogram, since if the scan is negative the angiogram also often cannot see the bleeding point.
- Advantages of scan: no bowel prep needed, safe, non-invasive, and used as screening for angiogram.
- Disadvantages: in terms of accuracy to pinpoint the bleeding it is not exact: hard to do since bowel peristalsis mixes, and if the colon is redundant the pictures overlap further; also scan cannot tell what disease the bleeding comes from, offers no treatment, takes long, so unsuitable for unstable patients; and scan depends on a nuclear medicine physician, who may not work outside office hours; given these limitations, plus CTA works better, scan is left only for occult or obscure bleeding.
- Label with sulfur colloid: needs a bleeding rate of only 0.1 cc/min to be positive; but sulfur has a short half-life, not more than 5 minutes, then the RE cells of liver and spleen clear it all (rapid clearing); so sulfur colloid detects only at the time of bleeding, seen in a single moment while scanning; if bleeding is intermittent, stopping at times, the sulfur scan finds nothing; sulfur goes to the liver and spleen, so a bleeding point at the splenic or hepatic flexure or upper abdomen is hidden behind them.
- Label with the patient's own RBC: injected IV, takes 1-2 hours to be seen in circulation; needs a bleeding rate of about 0.5 cc/min to see the bleeding point, so if it is seen the bleeding is fairly severe with a higher chance of surgery; the half-life of RBC scan is long, 24-36 hours, so it suits LGIB, whose bleeding often comes and goes, and it can be repeated; but the sharpness of RBC scan is poorer than sulfur.
- Remember: scan positive should be followed by angiogram or scope; do not take the patient to the OR on the scan alone, since scan results are often wrong; if a young resident does that, he is guaranteed to be scolded by the teacher until he shrinks to the size of a sesame seed.

## Selective Mesenteric Angiography
- Can find the bleeding point only if the bleeding rate is above 0.5-1 ml/min.
- Resuscitate before sending; catheter goes in through the femoral artery; inject the SMA first, then the IMA, and the celiac axis last.
- Sensitivity 34-80%; localization about 70%; shows the bleeding site more clearly and certainly than a scan.
- If a cause can be named, in LGIB:
  1. Diverticulum: contrast pooling and extravasation into the diverticulum.
  2. Angiodysplasia: dense opacified intramural vein (90%); vascular tuft and early filling vein (70%).
- If the bleeding point is found, treat with vasopressin or embolization.
- Selective arterial infusion of vasopressin: aim is vasoconstriction so the bleeding stops, or at least slows enough to buy time to prepare the patient for exploration.
  - Drawbacks of vasopressin: (1) rebleeding 50%; (2) CHF, pulmonary edema, hypertension, cardiac arrest; so patients with MI or PAD (peripheral arterial disease) should not get vasopressin.
- Embolization: stops the bleed in 80%, rebleeding 20%; suits bleeding diverticulum.
  - Drawbacks: bowel infarction 7%; may lead to perforation or late stricture; so follow the patient closely after embolization, and if there are signs of abdominal pain suggesting perforation or peritonitis, take to the OR promptly.
  - If superselective embolization still fails and the patient is likely headed to the OR, the author advises injecting methylene blue first; at laparotomy the bleeding segment of bowel then shows as blue-stained.

## CT Angiography
- With newer machines, especially 3-D CTA, bleeding can be seen if the rate is above 0.3 cc/min.
- Limitations: it diagnoses and shows where the bleeding vessel is, but cannot treat; contrast nephropathy risk.
- Per the ASGE guideline, CTA is for patients who are still stable.
- CTA positive: shows the bleeding site and cause, helping choose the right treatment.
- CTA negative: the bleed is not very active; prep the bowel and do colonoscopy.

## Barium Enema
- Do not use BE during active bleeding: barium coats the film white and hides everything; if angiogram is needed afterward nothing is visible, and colonoscopy shows only barium with no clear bleeding point.
- If BE must be ordered, it should be at a community hospital without endoscopy and done after the bleeding has stopped; where colonoscopy is possible, skip BE and go to colonoscopy.

## Choosing investigations in LGIB
- Many tools exist, so choose by whether the patient is stable, the severity (slow or active), the hospital's capability and equipment, and endoscopist availability.
- Bleeding slow or stopped: prep bowel, colonoscopy; if the bleeding point is found, give endoscopic treatment.
- Continuous or active bleeding:
  1. Patient still stable:
     - 1.1 Moderate bleeding: in theory scan screening, in practice colonoscopy.
     - 1.2 Active bleeding: may skip the scan and go to angiogram.
     - 1.3 Urgent colonoscopy.
  2. Patient not stable: resuscitation fails to bring them up; go to the OR with intraoperative endoscopy prepared.

## Operative Treatment of LGIB
- Indication for surgery is not clear-cut as in UGIB, but rough criteria:
  1. Blood loss over 2,000 cc/24 hr, or over 1,500 cc, to maintain vital signs; or the Hct on arrival below 6 gm/dL (the author believes bleeding this severe means the patient may die without surgery).
  2. Rebleeding during the same admission or within 1 week.
  3. Continuous bleeding for more than 3 days (72 hours).
  4. Failure of nonoperative treatment, or small bleeding but cannot refer.
  5. Elderly patients should not wait long.
- Emergency operation: first ask whether the location is known before operating; if known, treat according to the site and cause. The problem is that often it is not known, and the surgeon must go in blind.

## Approach to Unknown Bleeding Site
- Intraoperative assessment of bleeding site:
  1. Palpate the bowel during the operation to feel for a mass that may be the cause.
  2. Transillumination of bowel segments.
  3. Segmental clamping.
  4. Intraoperative endoscopy.
  5. Multiple colostomies (some suggest dividing the colon at the midtransverse colon and seeing whether blood comes from the right or left side, then resecting that side); the author says this should not be done because it makes a mess and fills the abdomen.
- Operative steps:
  1. Patient in lithotomy position, set up for intraoperative endoscopy or colonoscopy.
  2. Midline incision, careful palpation, then transillumination starting from small bowel down to colon; use the scope if nothing is found.
  3. A pediatric colonoscope is passed by mouth while the surgeon pulls the bowel down onto the scope, with the head-end assistant watching; view the whole small bowel; mark any bleeding site found.
  4. Then do colonoscopy; if there is much blood and the view is poor, do colonic lavage through the appendiceal stump (appendicostomy) with warm NSS 0.9% about 3,000 cc.
  5. Do segmental resection at the bleeding site.
  6. If no bleeding point is found and no bleeding in the small bowel but only blood in the colon, and the patient is unstable, be quick: total or subtotal colectomy; if the patient is very poor, make an ostomy and come back another day.
  7. If the hospital has no scope ready, use the scope for intraoperative endoscopy anyway, when forced into the OR because bleeding will not stop and referral is impossible; if dark blood is found in the small bowel, distinguish blood from bile-like fluid, which can look similarly dark.
     - If no intraoperative endoscope is available: milk the small-bowel contents down into the colon until the small bowel collapses, then use intestinal clamps for segmental clamping every 10-15 cm; see which segment fills (becomes distended) and which is empty; the segment that fills with blood is the bleeding segment to resect; while waiting, palpate the small bowel for a firm mass or ulcer, which may be the bleeding point.
     - Another way: multiple enterotomies 75-100 cm apart and watch which segment the blood comes from; resect that segment.
     - All this applies to life-threatening LGIB bleeding in the small bowel where the surgeon is in a hospital without a scope or the bleeding site is hidden (rarely found) and referral is impossible.
     - For hospitals with a scope and small bowel bleeding: see the Obscure GI bleeding topic, intraoperative endoscopy.

## Approach to Known Bleeding Site
- Post polypectomy bleeding: acute bleeding usually needs immediate scope; inject or clip; delayed bleeding often comes from the patient being active; 70% stop by themselves; if not, endoscopic treatment by loop ligation, band ligation, injection or clip, as convenient.
- Bleeding diverticulosis:
  - Risks: constipation, low-fiber diet, NSAID, ASA, older age.
  - Although diverticula are found more on the left than the right, bleeding is more on the right than the left (arterial bleeding).
  - 70% stop spontaneously; 30% need scope or surgery.
  - Figure: diverticulum with the penetrating artery at the neck, showing normal blood supply, lining and muscle layer (the bleeding vessel is the penetrating artery).
  - Treatment:
    1. Endoscopic treatment first: inject diluted adrenaline 1:10,000, bipolar probe, or hemoclips to clamp the bleeding point; do not forget to inject methylene blue or India ink tattoo to mark the spot; if it cannot be controlled and surgery is needed, this tells where it bled.
    2. If endoscopic treatment fails, try angiographic coil embolization; may stop bleeding in 85%.
    3. Open surgery is the last resort; once the bleeding site is known, do segmental resection of that part; if the site is still unknown, use intraoperative endoscopy to help.
- Bleeding angiodysplasia:
  - A degenerative lesion; from chronic colon contraction squeezing and dilating the submucosal vein until venous drainage is poor; the vein gradually becomes more dilated; this is venous bleeding.
  - Bleeding from angiodysplasia is found more on the right than the left; luckily it stops spontaneously in more than 80%, but rebleeding is common; since the vessels are mucosal venules, the bleeding point is seen only by scope.
  - Treatment: 1. Endoscopic treatment first, which usually stops it. 2. If it does not stop, open surgery.
- Rectal Dieulafoy's ulcer: the stomach is the commonest site, but it can occur in the rectum; diagnosed by rigid or flexible sigmoidoscope, seeing pulsatile arterial bleeding that bleeds and stops intermittently, as in the stomach; treat with endoscopic treatment.
- Radiation proctitis: especially CA cervix patients who had RT more than 3 months earlier; mucosa becomes friable, hyperemic, telangiectatic with small ulcers. Figure: endoscopy showing ectatic vessels and active bleeding (skip).
  - Treatment options: hydrocortisone enema, APC; the best way, per the author, is to stop the bleeding by formalin chemical coagulation.
  - Small, scattered bleeding spots: cotton soaked in 4% formaldehyde placed in the rectum for 30 seconds to 3 minutes; mucosa turns white and the bleeding stops.
  - Diffuse bleeding all over the rectum: put in a proctoscope and instill formalin, leave it 30 minutes, then irrigate the rectum with NSS to wash the formalin out.
  - In animals formalin is absorbed, peaking in serum at 30 minutes then returning to baseline; no long-term ill effect and no problem with excretion.

## Obscure GI Bleeding (OGIB)
- Definition: GI bleeding whose cause is not found by both upper endoscopy and colonoscopy; most (nearly all) OGIB is believed to come from the small bowel.
- OGIB is about 5% of all GI bleeding.
- Symptoms resemble colonic bleeding but localization and treatment of small bowel bleeding are much harder; seeing an OGIB patient is like holding a bomb.
- Small bowel bleeding tends to be intermittent (bleeds then stops), so it is hard to catch.
- Occult bleeding: chronic, slowly oozing bleeding, little blood; some present with iron deficiency anemia; FOBT positive at checkup.
- Overt bleeding: luckily less common; bleeding is brisk and continuous, with melena or hematochezia, or vomiting blood; often with other GI symptoms.
- Cause: use age as a guide:
  1. Age over 50: angiodysplasia is most common and overall is the number 1 cause of OGIB; then tumor; telangiectasia and amyloidosis are rare.
  2. Age 30-50: tumor is most common here, but overall tumor is number 2 as a cause of OGIB; then ulcerative colitis and vascular lesions.
  3. Age under 30 or children: Meckel is number 1; then hereditary disorders; beware Dieulafoy.
- Other causes of OGIB: Crohn, RT, NSAID, hemangioma, AVM, TB, thyroid, vasculitis, Dieulafoy, aortoenteric fistula, jejunoileal varices.
- Management of OGIB follows general GI bleeding principles:
  1. Initial assessment and resuscitation by ATLS.
  2. Localization of OGIB.
  3. Definitive treatment, one or several methods combined: medication, endoscopy, interventional radiology, surgery.
- Difficult problem: the small bowel is the hardest organ to examine; scope is hard, and even if localized, treatment is hard.
- Remember: do not explore blindly when the bleeding point in the small bowel is not yet localized; exceptions are bleeding so brisk that the patient will not become stable and cannot be referred; then one must go in and clear the family beforehand about outcome.

### Guideline of Diagnostic Evaluation
- After the OGIB patient is stabilized, the next steps are to localize the bleeding and find the underlying disease, in these steps:
  1. Differential diagnosis: guess which disease bled, enough to give a rough site, e.g. Crohn usually bleeds near the terminal ileum.
  2. OGIB patients have usually already had upper and lower scope without a finding: repeat upper endoscopy and colonoscopy once more; if no bleeding point again, it is not UGI or colon, so likely small bowel; go to step 3.
  3. Push enteroscopy; if nothing again, capsule endoscopy or 3-D CT scan (CTA) of the abdomen.
  4. If step 3 still finds nothing: radioactive RBC scan or mesenteric angiography.
  5. If all methods still fail to find the cause, go to the OR, open the abdomen, and do intraoperative endoscopy.
- Enteroclysis and GI follow through: if tumor or polyp is not strongly suspected, do not waste time; the chance of finding a cause is under 20%, and GI follow through under 5%; most OGIB cause is angiodysplasia, which these studies do not find.

### Differential Diagnosis of OGIB
- Go back and review history, symptoms and whether bleeding is occult or overt:
  1. Drug history: e.g. NSAIDs cause ulceration; anticoagulants at supratherapeutic level can cause spontaneous bleeding; often stopping or reducing the dose stops the bleeding by itself, and then no further investigation is needed.
  2. Previous investigations: re-review for anything suspicious or equivocal reports; take the films to review with the radiologist again.
- Diseases that may be diagnosed from history plus physical examination:
  1. Meckel's diverticulum:
     - 1.1 Think of painless bleeding in a patient under 25 years (memorize), the bleeding often brisk or overt; because 70% of Meckel has ectopic gastric mucosa secreting acid that makes an ulcer at the adjacent small bowel; few bleed with abdominal pain.
     - 1.2 If suspected, do a Meckel scan, since ectopic gastric mucosa takes up 99m-Tc pertechnetate. Figure: bleeding Meckel diverticulum and a Meckel scan with an arrow at the bleeding point (skip).
  2. Radiation enteritis: history of cancer, e.g. CA cervix, treated with RT to the lower abdomen over many courses, then recurrent bleeding that comes and goes with repeated hospital stays; luckily it is usually occult bleeding and stops by itself.
  3. Peutz-Jeghers syndrome:
     - 3.1 Think of bleeding in a patient under 20 years, usually occult from hamartomatous polyps in the small bowel; classic sign is perioral pigmentation; there may be a history of obstructive symptoms from intussusception with a hamartomatous polyp as the lead point.
     - 3.2 Send for enteroscopy; enteroclysis or GI follow through can help but the chance of a positive result is low.
  4. Small bowel tumors: polyp, GIST, lymphoma, small bowel cancer, metastatic melanoma may present with bleeding, occult or overt.
     - 4.1 Suspect in patients aged 30-50 with weight loss, big lymph nodes or evidence of distant metastasis; in the young it is usually benign; in the elderly think of cancer.
     - 4.2 Localize the tumor with CT scan, enteroscopy, enteroclysis, GI follow through.
  5. Crohn's disease:
     - 5.1 Patient aged 20-50, bleeding may be occult or overt with abdominal pain, weight loss, recurring chronic symptoms that come and go; extraintestinal manifestations such as joint pain, skin lesions, ocular inflammation.
     - 5.2 Send for scope or imaging.
  6. Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome: age 20-40 with overt bleeding; classic features are family history, epistaxis, and hemangioma/telangiectasia seen in the mouth, skin and fingers; in the GI tract it is AVM. Figure: photos of lip and tongue telangiectasia and gastric lesions (skip).

### Repeated Upper Endoscopy and Colonoscopy
- Even if done once and nothing was found, repeat: a bleeding lesion may be found, because the first scope is often limited by blood or retained food.
  1. Upper endoscopy: look for:
     - 1.1 Dieulafoy's lesion: the first scope may miss it because Dieulafoy bleeds on and off.
     - 1.2 Cameron's erosion: from mechanical trauma of the diaphragm against a sliding hiatal hernia, giving small linear erosions that bleed chronically; treat with PPI first; if that fails, stop the bleeding and add an antireflux procedure. Figure: hiatal hernia with Cameron erosion (skip).
  2. Colonoscopy: even while bleeding, a colonoscope can be pushed in by placing an NG tube and giving a rapid bowel prep first; when scoping, go to the terminal ileum to look for tumor, Crohn or infection such as TB.

### Push Enteroscopy & Capsule Endoscopy & CT
- Explained because some people are unfamiliar and may be asked by the teacher; do this set of investigations when repeated upper endoscopy and colonoscopy find no lesion that could cause OGIB.

#### Push enteroscopy
- Uses a long endoscope, i.e. a colonoscope passed by mouth instead of by anus (do not let the patient know it is a lower-GI scope).
- Reaches only about 60 cm of upper jejunum; the rest of the small bowel needs capsule endoscopy.
- Advantage: biopsy and intervention possible, so it is more specific and sensitive than the capsule.
- To lessen the limitation of push, a 3-4 meter scope was made, called Pull or Sonde enteroscopy: a long thin fiberoptic scope that the patient swallows and then it is pulled up.
  - Sonde enteroscopy is no longer used: (1) the long scope is hard to maintain and expensive; (2) the whole procedure takes 6-8 hours before the examination finishes, the patient is kept a long time and suffers; (3) no working channel, so it diagnoses but cannot treat.

#### Double balloon enteroscopy
- Uses 2 adjustable balloons for traction along the lumen length of the bowel to advance the enteroscope down. Figure: scope with two balloons (skip).
- Japanese experts (Yamamoto level) report the whole small bowel seen successfully in up to 86%.

#### Capsule endoscopy
- Views the whole small bowel; the FDA has approved it as a substitute for Pull or Sonde.
- Capsule weighs 3.7 gm, size 11 x 27 mm; very expensive, each capsule costs about tens of thousands of baht.
- It has a camera and lens powered by a battery lasting about 7 hours; takes 2 pictures per second continuously until the battery runs out; pictures go wirelessly to a recorder worn on the patient's waist belt.
- The capsule moves with peristalsis to the colon in about 7 hr, producing about 50,000 pictures; reading takes a viewer about 30-90 minutes, and the viewer's eyes tire; if the vessel is thrombosed or broken the picture may be missed; images are stored on a computer.
- Advantages:
  1. Non-invasive, painless, well tolerated, low complication risk.
  2. Can be done as an OPD case without sedation.
  3. Serves as a guide for intraoperative endoscopy.
- Small bowel problems that benefit from capsule: OGIB; evaluation and assessment of small bowel Crohn; malabsorption, tumor, chronic diarrhea; surveillance of small bowel polyps.
- Disadvantages:
  1. Cannot control direction and transit time.
  2. Cannot say exactly which part of the bowel the disease is in.
  3. No tissue obtained.
  4. Very expensive and cannot be reused; residents should not wash and reuse a capsule retrieved from the stool.
  5. Limited if the patient has motility problems, obstruction, ileus, or is uncooperative.
  6. A patient with active bleeding would be dead before waiting 6-7 hours for pictures to be read.
  7. Patient has a pacemaker.
- After the capsule study, the next day a plain abdominal film or fluoroscopy may be taken to see whether the capsule has passed out yet;
  - about 1% of capsules stay in the bowel, e.g. lodged in a diverticulum that will not let go; there are 2 cases among many tens needing surgery because of the diverticulum's inflammation; since the numbers are small, if the patient has no abdominal pain from bowel obstruction and no obstructive symptoms, nothing needs to be done except observe symptoms.

#### Spiral CT scan
- In OGIB use IV and oral contrast CT.
- Suits OGIB patients who have bleeding with abdominal pain or obstructive symptoms.
- Diseases for which to use CT: 1. Malignancy; 2. Inflammation; 3. Diverticular disease; 4. Aortoenteric fistula.

### Radioactive RBC Scan & Mesenteric Angiography
- Same principle as LGIB: suited to patients with severe active bleeding.
- Angiogram: if a bleeding point is found it can be treated by embolization, or dye can be left as a landmark showing where the bleed is, so when the abdomen must be opened the bleeding point is found more easily.

### Intraoperative Endoscopy
- The last method, after all investigations still find nothing and the patient keeps having continuous or recurrent bleeding, transfusion dependent.
- After opening the abdomen: clamp the ileocecal valve so the colon does not become distended with air; then pass the scope by mouth or through an enterotomy, pushing the colonoscope in while the surgeon plicates (gathers) the bowel onto the scope so it advances easily; view the small bowel mucosa while the scope is being inserted rather than while withdrawing, to avoid misreading iatrogenic mucosal injury.
- While viewing, have the room lights dimmed a little; the bleeding site is then seen more clearly; mark the bleeding site with a serosal stitch, continue scoping until the whole small bowel is seen, then resect that segment of bowel. Figure: intraoperative enteroscopy, scope inside bowel with surgeon plicating bowel over it (skip).

### Treatment of OGIB
- Endoscopic treatment: suits bleeding from vascular lesions.
- Surgical resection: suits bleeding from tumor, Meckel, or a vascular lesion that the endoscope cannot reach.
- Remember: 1. It is not necessary (and should be avoided) to explore without knowing where the bleeding point is. 2. Exception: severe bleeding or hemodynamic instability; even if the site is unknown, consider exploration.

### Summary guideline for OGIB management (Schwartz 11th ed)
Figure (algorithm), as steps:
1. Obscure GI bleeding: first rule out upper and lower GI bleeding with EGD and colonoscopy.
2. Minor bleeding (intermittent): enteroclysis, enteroscopy, capsule endoscopy.
   - Positive: start appropriate therapy.
   - Negative: repeat EGD/colonoscopy if rebleeds.
3. Major bleeding (persistent):
   - Stable: tagged RBC scan. Positive: angiography and treatment. Negative and patient stable: go to the minor-bleeding pathway (enteroclysis, enteroscopy, capsule endoscopy).
   - Unstable: operating room. If the source is uncertain: localize bleeding by serial clamping or intraoperative enteroscopy followed by resection. If the source is identified: treat the source, e.g. small bowel resection.
