## Pectus Excavatum (Funnel Chest)
- Thai lay name โรคอกหลำ (กรวง); better to use the textbook term. Most common chest wall deformity. Excavatum = posterior angulation; anterior angulation = pectus carinatum.
- M:F = 4:1
- 2% of pectus excavatum have Marfan, while 65% of Marfan have this condition.
- Symptoms: visible depression deformity (as printed "deformily"), tires easily, shallow breathing.
- Chest film PA and lateral view help diagnosis.
- **Indication for surgery**
  1. Compressed breathing, poor lung function
  2. Limit physical activity
  3. Patient wishes (cosmetic)
- **Surgical treatment**
  1. Children < 15 years: MIS is popular — clamp passed behind the sternum, or insertion of steel struts, then a bar as a frame to correct the deformity
  2. Open surgery or Nuss procedure (as printed): bilateral thoracic incision, cartilage resection + sternal osteotomy

## Primary Chest Wall Tumors
- Primary chest wall tumor = 5% of thoracic neoplasms; half benign, half malignant.
- Mean age: benign about 26 years; malignant about 40 years.
- **Osteschondrorma** (as printed) — most common primary benign tumor; arises from rib cortex at the costochondral junction along the sternum; teens, about 20 years; malignant degeneration reported — remove if enlarging or symptomatic.
- **Fibrous dysplasia** — most common tumor of rib; slow-growing, mostly asymptomatic, no need to remove.
- Malignant chest wall tumors are mostly metastases rather than primary.
- Most common primary malignant tumor = chondrosarcoma.
- Chest wall sarcomas that respond to chemo Rx — 4: osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, primitive neuroectodermal tumor and Ewing sarcoma (written up as needed).
- Approach to a chest wall mass: always rule out cancer.
  1. Diagnosis fairly clearly not cancer → CT/MRI; if resectable, excise with margin ≤ 2 cm (as printed "ไม่เกิน 2 cm")
  2. Diagnosis fairly clearly suggests cancer → needle biopsy or incisional biopsy (technique in the soft tissue sarcoma chapter). If malignant, find the type: osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, PNET → neoadjuvant chemo first; other types do not need it. Then wide local excision.
- Guideline (Schwartz edition 11 p. 721) — Figure: chest wall mass algorithm, as steps —
  1. Chest wall mass → CT or MRI or both.
  2. Diagnosis is NOT clear → needle biopsy or incisional biopsy.
  3. Nonrhabdosarcoma (fibrosarcoma, malignant fibrous histiocytoma, liposarcoma, synovial cell sarcoma, desmoid) → wide surgical excision.
  4. Osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, nonrhabdomyosarcoma (as printed), PNET/Ewing's sarcoma → preoperative chemotherapy → wide surgical excision.

## Chondrosarcoma
- 50% of malignant chest wall tumors.
- 80% in the rib at the costochondral junction; 20% in the sternum.
- M:F = 2:1; age 20-40 years.
- **Risk factors**
  1. Previous RT
  2. Repeated chest wall trauma
  3. Fibrous dysplasia
- CT chest: classic radiolucent lesion + stippled calcification.
- **Treatment**
  1. No lung met → wide surgical resection, margin 4 cm
  2. RT for inoperable cases; chemo is useless

## Ewing Sarcoma
- Common in children.
- Symptoms: tender chest wall tumor, fever, fatigue.
- Lab: raised WBC, raised ESR.
- Film: very classic onion skin appearance — multiple layers of periosteal elevation — with bone destruction.
- Diagnosis: percutaneous needle biopsy.
- Treatment: surgery + RT + chemo.

## Trachea
- Only likely exam topics are chosen; beyond this, just guess.

## Anatomy
- Length 13 cm, 18-22 rings.
- Main blood supply: inferior thyroid and bronchial arteries.
- Segmental blood supply limits mobilization.
- Resection: up to 6 cm (50%) of trachea can be removed with 1° anastomosis; more than this is too much tension to join.

## Post-Intubation Tracheal Injury
- Residents should know 2:
  1. Tracheal stenosis
  2. Tracheoinnominate artery fistula (TIAF) — a regular MCQ topic

## Tracheal Stenosis
- Usually after tracheostomy, at the stomal site.
- Critical stenosis: diameter < 4 mm.
- **Risk factors**
  1. Women (more than men), elderly
  2. Opening at the cricothyroid or first tracheal ring
  3. Transverse tracheotomy (vertical opening — less often)
  4. Previous neck irradiation
  5. Previous excessive corticosteroid therapy
- **Symptoms**
  1. Usually 1-6 wk after tracheostomy tube insertion (not 2-3 months)
  2. Initial symptom: dyspnea on exertion, then stridor
- **Treatment**
  1. Start by trying dilation, laser or stent; if better, follow up
  2. Significant stricture → most appropriate treatment = resection with 1° anastomosis
  3. Prevention: vertical tracheotomy, smallest feasible tube, then promptly downsize the tube

## Tracheoinnominate Artery Fistula (TIAF)
- Rare, but whoever meets it is unlucky: mortality 86%. Occurs 2-6 wk after tracheostomy.
- Cause: tracheostomy placed too low → the cuff erodes the anterior tracheal wall into the innominate artery.
- Symptoms: begins with a warning bleed (herald bleeding), then massive hemoptysis.
- **Management**
  1. First step: hyperinflation of tracheal tube cuff (must remember — ต้องจำ)
  2. If still bleeding: some advise extending the tracheostomy incision, then pushing the index finger in to press the innominate artery against the manubrium; do not remove the tracheostomy tube.
  3. Take the patient to OR to control airway and operate: change tracheostomy tube to orotracheal tube, then median sternotomy.
  4. The innominate artery is soggy and infected — do not repair; resect it and oversew proximal and distal ends.
  5. Cover the tracheal hole with healthy adjacent soft tissue.
- In real life mostly only the cuff can be blown up, since the patient arrests at once; the author has seen 2 cases, both died (as printed: tracheostomy done by someone else, but it lands on us). Remember: never place a tracheostomy below the 3rd tracheal ring, or there will be trouble.

## Tracheoesophageal Fistula (TEF)
- 2 forms:
  1. Congenital TEF — more common than acquired
  2. Acquired TEF — from endotracheal cuff erosion and cancer
- **Management of acquired TEF**
  1. Before surgery, wean the patient from the ventilator first; then determine whether the acquired TEF is benign or malignant
  2. Benign cause:
     - 2.1 Segmental resection of the tracheal defect
     - 2.2 1° closure of the esophagus
     - 2.3 Soft tissue interposition between trachea and esophagus
  3. Malignant TEF: place a stent to close the hole

## Tracheal Tumors
- Primary tracheal tumor is very rare, about < 0.2% of respiratory tract cancer.
- M:F = 7:3
- **Benign tumor**
  1. Adult: most common = papilloma
  2. Children: most common = hemangioma
- **Malignant tumor**: more common than benign in adults; most common = SCC, related to smoking; then adenoid cystic carcinoma; in children, carcinoid.
- 50% present at an advanced stage.
- SSC (as printed) type usually already has lymph node metastasis and often cannot be resected.
- Adenoid cystic carcinoma grows slowly and is less aggressive than SCC.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Common: progressive cough and hemoptysis
  2. Next: stridor, and symptoms from invasion of nearby organs, e.g. recurrent laryngeal nerve, esophagus
  3. Less common: hoarseness and dysphagia
- **Investigation**
  1. Start with neck and chest CT — shows tracheal stenosis
  2. Then rigid bronchoscopy for diagnosis and resection planning
- **Treatment**
  1. Best: open tracheal resection with 1° reconstruction
  2. PO RT to the mediastinum; chemo is not much use
  3. Unfit for surgery → endoscopic laser as palliative Rx
- Management guideline of tracheal neoplasm (Schwartz edition 11 p. 667) — Figure, as steps:
  1. High index of suspicion (cough, dyspnea, hemoptysis, stridor and hoarseness) → flexible/rigid bronchoscopy (± debridement and/or laser ablation).
  2. Complete staging: computed tomography/PET scan/mediastinoscopy.
  3. Tumor resectable:
     - Performance status adequate for surgery → tracheal resection. Principles: may resect up to 50% of tracheal length; anterolateral mobilization only; suture head in forward flexion for 7 days; laryngeal and hilar release as needed for relief of tension.
     - Poor performance status → radiotherapy 50 Gy (± chemotherapy).
  4. Tumor unresectable — (1) probable grossly positive tracheal resection margin, (2) metastatic disease, (3) length of resection precludes safe reconstruction, (4) invasion of unresectable adjacent organs → radiotherapy 50 Gy (± chemotherapy) (primary treatment or postoperatively).

## Benign Lung Tumors
- First = infectious granuloma 70-80%; second = hamartoma 10%.

## Hamartoma
- Usually asymptomatic.
- Most common benign tumor of the lung (70%) (as printed; cf. 10% above); usually diagnosed on film, plain film or CT chest. Malignant change is extremely rare.
- **CT chest**
  1. Linear nodular, irregular calcification = popcorn calcification, with fat density in the mass
  2. Small, peripheral location
  3. Follow-up film over 2 years usually unchanged (stability over time)
- Diagnosis relies on CT; biopsy not needed, but if done it shows cartilaginous material.
- **Treatment**
  1. Asymptomatic → follow-up CT chest in 6 months
  2. Symptomatic or enlarging → local resection or sleeve resection

## Solitary Pulmonary Nodule (SPN)
- A single coin lesion: well circumscribed, spherical, ≤ 3 cm on chest film or CT, surrounded by lung, with no atelectasis, hilar enlargement or pleural effusion.
- **Differntial diagnosis** (as printed) — disorders of:
  1. Congenital
  2. Neoplasm
  3. Inflammation
  4. Vascular
  5. Trauma
- Key point: if there is a chest film from 2 years ago, review it. If the same nodule was there unchanged, or it shows benign calcification such as popcorn, plus smooth contour → clearly benign → follow up.
- If it is a new lesion on routine chest x-ray not in old films, look at its features for suspicion of cancer.
- **Chest x-ray suspicious malignancy**
  1. Irregular speculated border — fine linear strands 4-5 mm long extending from the mass; this strongly suggests cancer
  2. Larger nodule size > 3 cm, lobulated shape without calcification
- New SPN on chest film → CT chest to define the mass and nodes, and its contrast pattern; enhancement suggests cancer.
- If film and CT suggest cancer, consider whether the patient is high risk, e.g. smoker; Schwartz then classes it as high probability for cancer, with SPN malignancy > 60%.
- Age < 50: cancer risk 5%; age > 50: > 50%.
- In the high-probability group fit for surgery, tissue diagnosis is not really needed — malignant or benign, it will be removed anyway. So Schwartz recommends VATS resection with F/S; if malignant, proceed to anatomical resection.
- For a lung mass, some advise CT-guided biopsy or thoracoscopic biopsy, or bronchoscopy before CT-guided biopsy, before surgery — either is fine.
- Greenfield recommends PET scan in every case to be sure there is no distant spread:
  1. PET -ve and fit → operate
  2. PET +ve, only nodes in the subcarinal, aortopulmonary or inferior pulmonary region → EUS-FNA, then chemo RT, reassess; if then resectable, operate
- **Recommended management in solitary pulmonary nodule** (Schwartz edition 11 p. 686) — Figure, as steps:
  1. New SPN (8 mm to 30 mm) identified on CXR or CT scan.
  2. Benign calcification present or 2-year stability demonstrated? Yes → no further intervention, except patients with pure ground glass opacities, in whom longer annual follow-up should be considered. No → step 3.
  3. Surgical risk acceptable? No → establish diagnosis by biopsy when possible; consider XRT or monitor for symptoms and palliate as necessary. Yes → assess clinical probability of cancer.
  4. Low probability (< 5%) → serial high-resolution CT at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months.
  5. Intermediate probability (> 5%-60%) → additional testing: PET imaging if available; contrast-enhanced CT depending on institutional expertise; transthoracic fine-needle aspiration biopsy if nodule is peripherally located; bronchoscopy if air-bronchogram present or operator has expertise with newer guided techniques. Negative tests → serial high-resolution CT as above. Positive tests → as high probability.
  6. High probability (> 60%) → video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: examination of a frozen section, followed by resection if nodule is malignant.

## Lung Cancer
- Formerly divided into 2 main types:
  1. Non small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) 80%
  2. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) 20%
- Now divided by light microscopic criteria into 2 groups:
  1. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), 4 types (rough idea; really there are about ten, as printed):
     - 1.1 Adenocarcinoma 30%
     - 1.2 Squamous cell carcinoma 30%
     - 1.3 Large cell carcinoma 10-20%
     - 1.4 Salivary gland type neoplasm (from submucosal bronchial gland)
  2. Neuroendocrine tumor, 4 grades: typical carcinoid, atypical carcinoid, large cell type, and the most aggressive last grade — formerly called small cell lung cancer (SCLC) or oat cell carcinoma.
- **Risk factors**
  1. 90% smoking
  2. Chemicals, e.g. aromatic hydrocarbon and vinyl chloride
  3. Radiation, e.g. radon, uranium
  4. Asbestos, coal tar, petroleum products
- **Screening for lung cancer in high risk population**: asymptomatic, age 55-74, smoking 30 pack-years, for at least 15 years — USPSTF recommends yearly low dose CT chest; reduces lung cancer deaths by 14%.
- **Paraneoplastic syndrome in lung cancer** — usually in neuroendocrine tumor grade 4 or small-cell lung cancer:
  1. Metabolic, e.g. hypercalcemia, hyponatremia, Cushing syndrome
  2. Neurologic, e.g. peripheral neuropathy, polymyositis
  3. Skeleton, e.g. clubbing finger, hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy — bone pain in hands, feet, arms
  4. Hematologic, e.g. anemia, DIC
  5. Skin, e.g. hyperkeratosis, acantosis nigrirans (as printed)
  6. Lambert-Eaton syndrome or myasthenia-like syndrome
- (This page of the PDF carries handwritten annotations about pain management that are not part of the printed text; not included.)
- Symptoms: early usually none; at diagnosis 85% already have spread. Symptoms found:
  1. Pulmonary symptoms: most common cough (70%), then dyspnea; some present with pneumonia or lung abscess
  2. Non-pulmonary symptoms: from invasion of nearby structures, e.g. phrenic nerve, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, SVC syndrome, bone pain, weight loss; tumor next to the pleura → pleuritic pain or localized chest pain
- **Tissue diagnosis**
  1. Sputum cytology or bronchoscopy — malignant cells found in 20-80%
  2. CT-guided needle aspiration or thoracoscopy + biopsy — for peripheral lesions
- **Staging**: after diagnosis, use CT, PET (N, M), bronchoscopy for endobronchial invasion, thoracoscopy to sample lymph nodes, and mediastinoscopy to sample N2 and N3 groups. CVT residents need to know much more; general surgery residents just the outline.
- MRI has no advantage over CT.
- PET scan: CVT recommends it in every case; detects 15% more metastasis.

## Non Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC)
- Treatment depends on stage: early, locally advanced, or advanced.
- **Early stage** = T1-2 N0-1 or T3 N0 → anatomical resection; adjuvant chemotherapy for T2 (mass > 3 cm) or any T N+.
- **Locally advanced** = T4 (mass > 7 cm) or any T N2, with +ve mediastinal or subcarinal node → induction chemotherapy first, then surgical resection + adjuvant chemo.
- **Stage 4** = M present → chemo.
- **Treatment options**
  1. Best = surgical resection
     - 1.1 Open or VATS
     - 1.2 Segmentectomy, lobectomy or pneumonectomy
     - 1.3 Regional LND
  2. **Absolute contraindication for surgery**
     - 2.1 Malignant pleural effusion
     - 2.2 Bilateral endobronchial tumor
     - 2.3 +ve contralateral mediastinal LN
     - 2.4 +ve supraclavicular LN (N3)
     - 2.5 Poor general condition (would surely die on the table)
     - 2.6 Metastasis present
  3. Inoperable because of poor condition or +ve N3 → RT + chemo

## Preoperative Evaluation of Pulmonary Function
- Before surgery assess how much resection the patient can tolerate. Simplest and most practical: brief history and, if possible, watch the patient:
  1. Walks on level ground without dyspnea, no rest, no oxygen → can have lobectomy
  2. Climbs at least 2 flights of stairs without stopping, without oxygen → can have pneumonectomy
- Then check lung function, especially FEV1 — best predictor of respiratory complication risk before lung resection:
  1. FEV1 > 2 L → can tolerate pneumonectomy; no additional diagnostic testing needed
  2. FEV1 > 1.5 L → can tolerate lobectomy
- Guideline for preoperative evaluation of pulmonary function (Schwartz edition 11 p. 694) — Figure, as steps:
  1. Perform spirometry.
  2. FEV1 > 1.5 L (lobectomy), > 2 L (pneumonectomy), > 80% predicted → unexplained dyspnea or diffuse parenchymal disease on CXR/CT?
     - No → average risk.
     - Yes → measure DLCO: > 80% predicted → average risk; < 80% predicted → step 4.
  3. FEV1 < 1.5 L (lobectomy), < 2 L (pneumonectomy), < 80% predicted → step 4.
  4. Estimate %ppo FEV1 and %ppo DLCO:
     - Both > 40 → average risk.
     - %ppo FEV1 or %ppo DLCO < 40 → perform CPET: VO2max > 15 mL/kg/min → average risk; 10-15 mL/kg/min → increased risk; < 10 mL/kg/min → increased risk.
     - %ppo FEV1 < 30, or %ppo FEV1 × %ppo DLCO < 1650 → increased risk.
- Invasion of chest wall, pericardium or diaphragm → neoadjuvant chemo RT before surgery.

## Pulmonary Metastasis
- Pulmonary metastasectomy in well-selected patients, with all lung metastases removed: 5-year survival about 36%.
- **Indications for pulmonary metastasectomy**
  1. Primary tumor must be controlled
  2. Able to tolerate general anesthesia
  3. Metastases must be completely resectable
  4. No extrapulmonary tumor burden
- Single brain metastasis in a fit patient is debated; accepted Rx: resect the lung lesion, and craniotomy or stereotactic radiosurgery for the brain.
- Serious complication after lung resection: bronchial stump leak with bronchopleural fistula. Suspect with sudden dyspnea, hypotension, subcutaneous emphysema on the operated side, coughing up pus, mediastinal shift, air leak, pleural effusion on chest film. Confirm with bronchoscopy-directed single lung ventilation (as printed "bronchoscopey ... ventrilation").
- Treatment: hemodynamic stabilization first; reduce the gradient between airway and pleural pressures; if ventilation is needed, use one-lung tube to isolate and protect the good lung; then reassess whether the leak will close by itself — if not, resuture.

## Pancoast Tumor
- NSCLC at the posterior apex of the chest at the superior sulcus, giving a syndrome typical of lung cancer in this position.
- Reported by Henry Pancoast 1932; symptoms from invasion of the 1st rib, chest wall, nerves and vessels. Chest wall involvement from the 2nd rib down is not classed as Pancoast tumor.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Pain in upper chest wall, shoulder or arm, with atrophy of hand muscles
  2. Horner syndrome — invasion of the sympathetic chain:
     - 2.1 Unilateral enophthalmos (not exophthalmos)
     - 2.2 Ptosis
     - 2.3 Miosis
     - 2.4 Facial anhidrosis
- **Investigation**: best = MRI, shows invasion of brachial plexus and vertebral body clearly; then biopsy and staging.
- **Treatment**
  1. Start with induction chemo RT for about 5 weeks
  2. After chemo RT, if tumor stable or smaller and no contraindication (heart, kidney, lung, performance status acceptable) → OR for thoracotomy with:
     - 2.1 Lobectomy
     - 2.2 En bloc chest wall resection
     - 2.3 Chest wall reconstruction
  3. Inoperable because of M or N2 → definitive chemo RT

## Small Cell Lung Cancer (SCLC)
- Now classed as grade 4 neuroendocrine tumor; 20% of lung cancer.
- 90% believed caused by smoking (so the cause is not well known — as printed).
- Besides tumor symptoms, 40% have a paraneoplastic syndrome; most common SIADH, though Greenfield says Cushing is more common.
- So a lung mass with anorexia, nausea, lethargy, hyponatremia, low serum osmolarity but high urine sodium and urine osmolarity → think SCLC.
- Hypercalcemia is the least common paraneoplastic syndrome; SCLC with high serum calcium → think bone metastasis rather than PTH-like substance.
- Hypertropic (as printed) pulmonary osteoarthropathy: pain and swelling at ankles, feet, arms, hands from periosteal inflammation; in SCLC it appears months before lung symptoms.
- Lung mass with bulky mediastinal lymphadenopathy → think SCLC more than non-small cell.
- Diagnosis: flexible bronchoscopy + biopsy.

| | NSCLC | SCLC |
|---|---|---|
| Incidence | 80% | 16% |
| Origin | Epithelial cells of the lung | Nerve producing cells of the lung (bronchi) |
| Classification | Squamous cell; large cell; adenocarcinoma | Limited stage; extensive stage |
| Metastatic potential | Less than SCLC | Rapid metastatic potential |
| Treatment | Surgery; chemotherapy; radiotherapy | Chemotherapy; radiotherapy |
| 5 year survival | Stage I: 57-67%; Stage II: 39-55%; Stage III: 5-25%; Stage IV: < 1% | Limited 20%; extensive < 1% |

- Figure captions: SCLC usually a large mass in the mediastinal lymph nodes and may compress the superior vena cava; PET shows a typical small cell cancer with a large mediastinal mass.
- **Treatment (SCLC)**
  1. Chemo RT — partial or complete response about 75% (one best answer: chemo)
  2. Usually already spread at diagnosis — so choosing surgery is wrong
- Prognosis: extremely poor; most survive about 18 months.

## SVC Syndrome
- From extrinsic and intrinsic compression of the SVC; mostly malignant — most common is invasion by lung cancer, SCLC more than NSCLC; then lymphoma and germ cell tumor.
- Non-malignant causes: histoplasmosis, TB, blastomycosis, nocardia, etc.
- Figure: SVC syndrome causes —
  - External compression, malignant: lung cancer, lymphoma, sarcoma, metastatic cancer, leiomyosarcoma, plasmocytomas
  - External compression, benign: tumors, mediastinitis, cardiovascular, pulmonary, trauma
  - Intraluminal fibrosis, benign: long-term indwelling catheters, pacemakers/defibrillators, hypercoagulable state, idiopathic, syphilis, tuberculosis
- Venous pressure usually > 20 mmHg.
- **Symptoms**
  1. **Acute complete obstruction** — usually from trauma, before collaterals form: neck swelling, neck vein engorgement, airway obstruction, cerebral edema
  2. **Chronic obstruction** — usually from tumor:
     - 2.1 Headache, cough, dyspnea, fatigue, numbness
     - 2.2 Exam: swelling of face, neck, arms; dilated superficial collateral veins on neck, arms and upper anterior chest wall; conjunctival edema
- **Treatment**
  1. Malignant — depends on cancer type:
     - 1.1 NSCLC → Rx of choice = RT
     - 1.2 SCLC, germ cell tumor, lymphoma → chemotherapy (จำ)
     - Note: RT or chemotherapy takes about 2-4 weeks to respond, so suits only partial obstruction, which is most cases.
  2. Urgent cases — complete SVC obstruction, no return of venous blood to the heart, the patient will surely die → emergency endovascular stent in the innominate vein and SVC; relieves symptoms within 24-72 hours.
  3. Adjuncts to reduce swelling: steroid, diuretic (supportive) (followed by a garbled string as printed — [unclear]).
  4. Surgery has no role in malignant SVC syndrome (choosing it is wrong); used only in benign causes, e.g. severe fibrosis where an endovascular stent cannot be placed — median sternotomy and graft bypass of the obstruction.

## Lung Abscess
- Collection of pus in a cavity from destruction of lung tissue, diameter at least 2 cc (as printed); organisms both aerobes and anaerobes.
- **Most common causes**
  1. Aspiration — right lung > left, especially posterior segment of RUL and superior segment of RLL; > 50% of aspiration pneumonia organisms are anaerobes only
  2. Pre-existing lung cavity then infected, e.g. TB, lung cyst
  3. Lung infarction → necrosis → abscess cavity
- Symptoms: fever, fatigue, weight loss, then pleuritic chest pain and cough with foul-smelling sputum.
- Chest x-ray: air-fluid level in a cavity; helps distinguish lung abscess from tumor; then CT for location, size, extension, and may find FB or tumor.
- Suspected endobronchial obstruction (after FB or tumor) → bronchoscopy.
- **Management**
  1. IV antibiotic for at least 3-12 wk, or until the cavity resolves on serial films, with postural drainage: about 95% successful. Remember: early Rx is medical (rushing to surgery is wrong).
  2. **Indication for surgery** (numbered as printed, 2.3-2.9)
     - 2.3 Persistence of an abscess or increasing in size despite adequate Rx (cavity not smaller after 6 wk)
     - 2.4 Empyema associated with a bronchopleural fistula
     - 2.5 Inability to exclude a cavitating carcinoma
     - 2.6 Hemoptysis, rupture, pyopneumothorax
     - 2.7 Necrotizing infection with multiple abscesses
     - 2.8 Abscess diameter > 4-6 cm (Greenfield uses 6 cm) or abscess under tension
     - 2.9 Contralateral lung contamination
  3. **Surgical approach**
     - 3.1 Percutaneous catheter drainage under CT or US guidance — used in > 90%
     - 3.2 ICD for abscess that ruptured into the pleural space
     - 3.3 Surgical resection now < 10%; usually lobectomy — don't forget a one-lung tube

## Pulmonary Tuberculosis
- HIV is a strong risk factor for active TB.
- Symptoms: chronic fever, night sweat, weight loss, cough, sometimes hemoptysis.
- Chest x-ray (as printed "x-way"): upper lobe infiltration ± cavitation.
- Diagnosis: sputum AFB +ve.
- **Treatment**
  1. Isoniazid, rifampicin 6 months; pyrazinamide for the first 2 months; response 95%
  2. Multidrug resistance (MDR): add ethambutal (as printed) or injected streptomycin
  3. Indication for surgery in complicated pulmonary TB:
     - 3.1 Failure (as printed "ailure") of optimized medical therapy, e.g. lung destruction, lung gangrene
     - 3.2 Persistent cavity disease
     - 3.3 Intracavity aspergillosis superinfection
     - 3.4 Massive hemoptysis
     - 3.5 Exclusion of lung cancer
     - 3.6 Bronchial stenosis
     - 3.7 Bronchopleural fistula
     - 3.8 Middle lobe syndrome
     - 3.9 Atypical mycobacteria — especially M. avium, often drug-resistant and often needing surgery

## Massive Hemoptysis
- Massive hemoptysis = bleeding > 600 cc in 24 hours.
- Exsanguinnating (as printed) hemoptysis = bleeding > 1000 cc or > 150 cc/hr.
- Mortality 30-50%; death from asphyxia > from blood loss.
- **Causes**
  1. 75% benign, e.g. bronchitis, lung abscess, CHF, TB, TIAF, bronchiectasis, pulmonary catheterization — TB is the most common of these
  2. Malignancy
- Bleeding vessel usually the bronchial artery, since its pressure is higher than the pulmonary artery.
- **Investigation**: bronchoscopy to find the cause and guide treatment (one best investigation).
- **Management**
  1. Protect airway: Trendelenburg position, lying on the side expected to bleed
  2. Stabilize the patient; humidified O2 with aerosolized epinephrine
  3. Localize bleeding site: review chest film or CT chest
  4. Try to control hemorrhage: suction bronchoscopy to localize bleeding; during scope, ice water lavage slows bleeding; then place a tube in the non-bleeding side so it does not get flooded with blood. Highly capable centers may use endobronchial laser or bronchial occlusion.
  5. Then bronchial angiography (not pulmonary angiography) and bronchial artery embolization — stops bleeding in almost all early on, but rebleeding about 50%
  6. Bronchial embolization can cause paraparesis, since part of the spinal cord blood supply comes from the bronchial artery
  7. If embolization fails → OR, double lumen tube, resection of the site known from preoperative bronchoscopy or scoping in the OR
- Mortality of surgery depends on the amount of bleeding before surgery — bleed more, die more (as printed, blunt).

## Pulmonary Sequestration
- Congenital lung anomaly: that part of lung has no communication with the tracheobronchial tree → recurrent infection (pneumonia).
- 2 types:
  1. Intralobar type 75%
  2. Extralobar type 25%
- **Intralobar type**
  1. Blood supply from the descending thoracic aorta
  2. No own pleural covering
- **Extralobar type**
  1. Blood supply from the abdominal aorta
  2. Own pleural covering (as printed "coverting"), separate from normal lung
  3. Associated with other congenital anomalies, e.g. congenital diaphragmatic hernia
- Treatment: lobectomy.

## Primary Spontaneous Pneumothorax
- **Primary**: usually young adults, thin, smokers (tall, thin, young male) without significant underlying lung disease; most common cause = rupture of small apical bleb (> 80%).
- **Secondary**: patients with underlying lung disease, e.g. COPD — always needs ICD, regardless of % of air in the pleural space.
- Symptoms: chest pain & dyspnea, often lasting hours or days.
- **Calculating % pneumothorax**
  1. Method of Peter and Sako — compare with a table; too fiddly, not used.
     - Figure (Rhea method, with nomogram): average the distance between the pleural line and chest wall at 3 points — apex, midpoint of upper lung, midpoint of lower lung — then read % pneumothorax from the nomogram. Example: 4 cm + 2 cm + 1 cm = 7 cm ÷ 3 = 2.3 cm → 25%.
  2. Simple method in use: distance between lung and inner chest wall < 2 cm = small pneumothorax; > 2 cm = large pneumothorax; roughly 1 cm = 10%.
- **Management**
  1. Observation: for < 20% with no symptoms; air absorbs 1-2% per day. Observe 6 hours, repeat chest film; if not larger, discharge home.
  2. When intervention is needed, 1st-line Rx = needle aspiration, then follow-up chest film within the next 6 hours; if asymptomatic and no further leak, discharge; if still leaking or symptomatic → ICD.
- Meta-analysis: needle aspiration is no different from ICD (which we usually do), but for the general surgery board, one best answer = ICD.
- Recurrence risk (for interest):
  - 2.1 After 1st pneumothorax 20%
  - 2.2 After 2nd pneumothorax 60%
  - 2.3 After 3rd pneumothorax 80%
  3. **Indication for surgery** (prefer VATS with bleb staple resection + talc pleurodesis)
     - 3.1 First episode with complete lung collapse
     - 3.2 Recurrent pneumothorax — operate at the 2nd episode (จำ)
     - 3.3 Prolonged air leak > 48 hr (Sabiston 2017, Schwartz 2019 use 72 hours; some texts > 7 days; the author follows Schwartz)
     - 3.4 Untreated bilateral pneumothorax
     - 3.5 Previous pneumonectomy
     - 3.6 Occupational hazard, e.g. pilots, divers, mountain climbers, or travel where there is no medical care
- Some hard-core surgeons advise operating at the first episode — the author thinks it too much. Others recommend CT chest in every spontaneous pneumothorax and operating if multiple small bullae or a large bleb is found, even at the first episode; as printed, what counts as "multiple" or the cut-off bleb size would need the constitutional court to rule (joke).
- Surgery need not be lobectomy: VATS stapling of the bleb with endoscopic staplers plus mechanical pleural abrasion gives very good results; recurrent pneumothorax < 1%.
- First episode: no surgery yet, unless bilateral (very unlucky).

## Pleural Effusion
- **Normal pleural fluid**
  1. About 20 cc in each pleural cavity
  2. pH 7.59-7.65
- Chest x-ray: blunted costophrenic angle in upright position ≈ 300 cc fluid.
- Fluid > 500 cc usually symptomatic, e.g. tiredness, difficult breathing.
- **Approach**
  1. History: fever, chest pain, shallow breathing
  2. Physical examination
  3. Chest film upright and lateral decubitus
  4. Key: thoracentesis — look at colour and turbidity first, then send for cell, cyto, organisms, pH, glucose, protein, LDH, amylase, TG
- Bloody fluid → think trauma, malignancy, pulmonary embolism.
- Milky fluid → think chylothorax.
- **Transudate**: protein-poor fluid from altered fluid balance in the pleural space; most common in CHF, then nephrotic syndrome and cirrhosis; sp.gr < 1.016, protein < 3 gm/dL.
- **Exudate**: protein-rich fluid from disrupted reabsorption by the pleura or lymphatic system; in malignancy, trauma, infection, pancreatitis.
- **Exudate by Light's criteria**
  1. Pleural protein / serum protein > 0.5
  2. Pleural LDH / serum LDH > 0.6
  3. Pleural fluid LDH ≥ 1.67 times serum LDH (as printed)
- **Interpreting pleural fluid** — the simple grassroots way:
  1. pH < 7.2 → severe infection, usually needs ICD
  2. Glucose < 60 mg/dL → think infection, especially TB
- **Indication for ICD in parapneumonic effusion**
  1. Chest film: fluid > hemithorax (as printed)
  2. Fluid chemistry: pH < 7.2, glucose < 60
  3. Fluid microbiology: grossly obvious pus, gram stain or culture positive
- **Summary of pleural effusion treatment** — Figure, as steps:
  1. Diagnostic thoracentesis.
  2. Any of: PF/serum protein > 0.5; PF/serum LDH > 0.6; PF LDH > 2/3 upper normal serum limit?
     - Yes → exudate → appearance of pleural fluid, pH & glucose, cytology and differential cell count of pleural fluid.
     - No → transudate → treat CHF, cirrhosis, or nephrosis.

## Empyema
- Purulent pleural fluid, usually from a parapneumonic process following lung infection, bacterial or TB; next most common cause is trauma. Fluid is exudate at first, then gradually turbid, thicker, then loculated.
- **Source of infection**
  1. Mostly from the lung: pneumonia then parapneumonic effusion
  2. Less often from the abdomen or chest wall
- Most common organism: strep pneumonia.
- Symptoms: fever, fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, shallow breathing, cough, yellow sputum, decreased breath sounds.
- **Investigation**
  1. Chest x-ray PA and lateral view: opacification
  2. CT chest: shows whether the empyema is loculated; also used to guide intervention
  3. Thoracentesis: pus, foul smell, organisms on stain, pH < 7.2, LDH > 1000, low glucose (< 40), pleural glucose / serum glucose < 0.5

| | Transudate | Empyema |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Clear | Cloudy or purulent |
| Cell count (per mm³) | < 1000 | Often > 50,000 (cell count has limited predictive value) |
| Cell type | Lymphocytes, monocytes | Polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) |
| Lactate dehydrogenase | < 200 U/L | > 1000 U/L |
| Pleural fluid/serum LDH ratio | < 0.6 | > 0.6 |
| Protein > 3 g | Unusual | Common |
| Pleural fluid/serum protein ratio | < 0.5 | > 0.5 |
| Glucose | Normal | Low (< 40 mg/dL) |
| pH | Normal (7.40-7.60) | < 7.10 |
| Gram stain | Negative | Occasionally positive (less than one-third of cases) |

- **Empyema — 3 phases**
  1. **Exudative or free floating phase**: first 3 days; no loculation or septation yet; low-viscosity fluid. Rx: ICD + IV antibiotic.
  2. **Fibrinopurulent phase**: 3 days to 2-4 wk; heavy fibrinous deposit, thicker fluid, loculation and some septa. Rx: VATS with debridement & drainage of the pleural space — septa must be broken, so ICD alone is not enough.
  3. **Chronic or organized phase**: capillary ingrowth and collagen trap the lung; lung stuck firmly to the pleura. Rx: VATS is hard because of dense adhesions; usually open thoracotomy + decortication.
- **Indication of open thoracotomy in empyema**
  1. Chronic or organized phase (after 14 days)
  2. Bronchopleural fistula (จำ) — needs a muscle flap to close it
  3. TB with lung entrapment

| | Exudative | Fibrinopurulent | Organized |
|---|---|---|---|
| Character | Thin, serous | Purulent | Thick |
| pH | > 7.3 | < 7.1 | < 7.1 |
| Glucose | Normal or low | Low | Low |
| WBC | < 1,000 | > 5,000 | Varies |
| Bacteria | ± | Present | Varies |

## Malignant Pleural Effusion
- Primary: in women, 1st breast, 2nd lung; in men, 1st lung.
- **Investigation**
  1. Decubitus ± CT chest
  2. Thoracentesis + cytologic evaluation
- **Management** — aim to improve QoL; several options:
  1. Repeated thoracentesis
  2. ICD
  3. ICD with pleurodesis
  4. VATS pleurodesis — best results
- Most effective agent for pleurodesis = talc: controls malignant fluid in > 90%, better than other sclerosing agents — tetracycline, bleomycin, or laser.

## Reexpansion Pulmonary Edema
- Life-threatening complication of ICD from rapid lung re-expansion after ICD for pneumothorax or effusion, when > 1-1.5 L drains in the first go.
- Signs of heart failure, mostly quickly; some delayed up to 24 hours.
- **Treatment**: supportive — oxygen; if needed, intubate and ventilate; usually self-limiting.
- **Prevention**: do not drain more than 1 L initially; if fluid remains, stop, wait 2-4 hours, then drain more.

## Malignant Mesothelioma
- Rare tumor from the mesothelial cell lining of serosal surfaces of body cavities — pleura and peritoneum.
- **Histology** — 3 subtypes:
  1. Epitheloid type
  2. Sarcomatoid type
  3. Mixed type
- **Risk factors**
  1. Major: asbestos exposure for > 20 years (80%)
  2. Other 20% non-asbestos related: RT, virus, genetic, pleural inflammation & scarring
- Age > 50; M:F = 4:1.
- **Symptoms**
  1. Chest pain
  2. May secrete ectopic hormones, but paraneoplastic syndrome is uncommon, unlike lung cancer
- **Chest x-ray**: pleural effusion 75%; then CT and PET scan to determine extent; then pleural biopsy or incisional biopsy via thoracoscopy or open techniques. (CT figure: red bars measure thickness of pleural disease.)
- **Treatment**
  1. Ideal Rx = surgery + RT + chemo; surgery = pleurectomy with decortications and extrapleural pneumonectomy, the best local Rx — but mostly a lost cause; survival about 4-12 months
  2. Chemo and RT do not work
  3. So treatment is mostly palliative, e.g. analgesics, ICD

## Mediastinal Tumor
- 3 locations: anterior, middle and posterior.
- Anterior is most common, and tumors here are often malignant.
- Middle: malignancy about 30%; posterior: less.
- Most common mediastinal tumor in adults & children = neurogenic tumor.
- Anterior mediastinal tumors are mostly the 3T: thymoma (age > 40; most common anterior mediastinal tumor), teratoma (age < 40) and thyroid (goiter or neoplasm).
- **Location of primary mediastinal masses in adults and children**

| Compartment | Masses |
|---|---|
| Anterosuperior | Thymoma, lymphoma, germ cell tumor, lymphangioma, hemangioma, lipoma, carcinoma, thyroid adenoma, parathyroid adenoma |
| Middle | Pericardial cyst, bronchogenic cyst, lymphoma |
| Posterior | Neurogenic tumor, enteric cyst |

- **Investigation**
  1. Tumor markers: AFP and beta HCG
  2. Chest film PA and lateral
  3. CT chest
  4. Echo for cystic lesions needing guided biopsy
  5. PET for suspected malignancy
  6. Chamberlain procedure (anterior mediastinoscopy, as printed; figure labels it anterior mediastinotomy) under local anesthesia, to biopsy a central mass or lymph node via a small transverse incision through the intercostal space; used when CT-guided biopsy fails to give an answer
- **Observations** (ข้อสังเกต)
  1. 3T (thymoma, thyroid, teratoma) are usually in the anterior compartment; a choice placing them posterior is wrong
  2. Esophagus is posterior (not anterior)
  3. Germ cell tumor: usually young men, mass with raised HCG/AFP
  4. Lymphoma: B symptoms, many enlarged extrathoracic nodes
  5. Middle lesions usually present with dyspnea, wheezing, hemoptysis; most common = mediastinal granuloma

## Thymoma
- Most common anterior mediastinal tumor, but 2nd overall.
- Men and women about equally.
- Paraneoplastic syndrome 50-70%:
  1. Myasthenia gravis — most common
  2. Red cell aphasia (as printed) 5%
  3. Aplastic anemia
  4. Cushing syndrome
  5. Scleroderma
  6. Addison disease
  7. Hypogammaglobulinemia
  8. Polymyositis
- Symptoms: fluctuating weakness, early fatigue, ptosis, diplopia.
  1. Compression of mediastinal structures in 50%, e.g. pain, cough; less often SVC syndrome
  2. Muscle weakness: 50% of thymoma have myasthenia gravis, but conversely only 15% of myasthenia gravis have thymoma
  3. Malignancy of thymoma is judged not by histopathology but by evidence of local invasion or capsular invasion
- **Indication for thymectomy**
  1. Presence of thymoma
  2. Myasthenia gravis without thymoma refractory to medical Rx
  3. Patient cannot tolerate or is noncompliant with medical Rx
- Preoperative biopsy is not needed — it only adds tumor contamination.
- **Incission** (as printed): median sternotomy (as printed "stenotomy"), partial sternal split, VATS; do not use posterolateral incision — too low, poor exposure.
- Surgery must achieve complete resection; if no malignant features and pathology confirms a well-encapsulated mass, follow-up is enough.
- If malignant → postop chemo RT.

## Myasthenia Gravis (MG)
- A little more, as some points are worth knowing for general surgery residents.
- Autoimmune neuromuscular transmission disorder: specific autoantibodies block the acetylcholine receptor complex at the motor end plate → muscle fatigue (as printed "fatique") and weakness of involuntary (as printed) muscle.
- 10% have thymoma; thymic hyperplasia up to 85%; malignancy only 15% (so "mostly thymic cancer" is wrong).
- **Symptoms**
  1. Eye symptoms, especially diplopia — most common
  2. Then muscle weakness, starting in the face, finally generalized weakness
- Lab: +ve antinicotinic acetylcholine receptor antibody.
- Operate on cases with both eye signs and muscle weakness, not ocular weakness alone.
- Best surgical results: MG without thymoma — better than MG with thymoma.
- After thymectomy, muscle weakness improves; younger patients do better than those > 60; sex makes no difference.
- Good results when operated within 8 months of symptom onset.
- After surgery, complete remission 20-25%; the rest still need drugs, at lower doses.
- Drugs for MG: anticholinesterases, e.g. neostigmine.

## Germ Cell Tumors
- Benign lesion residents should know: only teratoma.
- Malignant lesions: seminoma and non-seminoma.
- Young males; mass with raised HCG or AFP.

## Teratoma
- Most common mediastinal germ cell tumor; 2/3 of such tumors in adults are teratoma.
- 3 germ layers:
  1. Ectoderm: skin, hair, sweat gland, teeth
  2. Mesoderm: fat, muscle, bone, cartilage
  3. Endoderm: bronchial, intestinal epithelium, pancreatic tissue
- Any age; most common 20-40 years; men and women about equal.
- 3 types: benign (as printed "enign") teratoma — often found by chance on film, few symptoms; immature — immature component > 50%, may metastasise; malignant — malignant phenotype or metastasis. Luckily most teratomas are benign.
- Gross: well-defined margin, smooth, lobulated mass; may contain fat and cartilage; hair is pathognomonic of thymoma (as printed).
- Tumor markers not raised.
- Core needle biopsy: ectodermal, mesodermal & endodermal tissue.
- Film: classic mature bone or teeth in an anterior mediastinal tumor.
- **Treatment**
  1. Benign teratoma → median sternotomy + resection
  2. Malignant teratoma → en bloc resection; prognosis poor; often needs PO chemo

## Mediastinal Seminoma
- Most common malignant germ cell tumor in the mediastinum.
- Usually found in the testis; mediastinum much less often.
- Symptoms: compression of mediastinal structures, so by the time of symptoms the mass is usually large.
- **Tumor marker**
  1. AFP not raised
  2. Beta HCG not raised (slightly raised in some)
- **Treatment** — extremely radiosensitive:
  1. Localized seminoma → RT
  2. Nodal metastasis (locally advanced) → chemo ± RT
  3. Distant metastasis → chemo
- So a surgical choice for seminoma should be marked wrong first; surgery is rarely used, usually resection of residual disease.

## Mediastinal Non-Seminoma
- Young man; chest film shows widening of the superior mediastinum.
- Tumor marker: both AFP and beta HCG raised.
- **Treatment**
  1. Chemo RT (one best: chemo)
  2. Surgery kept for residual disease
- Summary: mediastinal mass with raised AFB (as printed), HCG → diagnose non-seminoma.

## Neurogenic Tumor
- Most common mediastinal tumor.
- Mostly in the posterior mediastinum, upper half of the paravertebral sulcus.
- Children: 75% benign, 25% malignant; aggressive type = neuroblastoma.
- Adults: 90% benign; 40-65% are nerve sheath tumors, 75% of which are schwannomas (neurilemmomas).
- **Schwannomas** on CT: round, firm, encapsulated, well-defined; low malignant potential.
- High-risk features — symptoms, mass > 5 cm, previous chest irradiation, CT or MRI showing invasion of adjacent tissue → operate; without high-risk features, observation is possible (as printed: per "คุณยาย" — [unclear] whose advice).
- **Common subtypes**
  1. Peripheral nerve tumor
     - 1.1 Schwannoma or neurilemoma: nerve sheath tumor from proliferation of endoneurium, covered by perineurium; may show cystic degeneration
     - 1.2 Neurofibroma: non-encapsulated homogenous tumor
  2. Next: ganglioneuroma and paraganglioma, which produce catecholamines
- Symptoms: spinal cord compression, paresthesia, Horner's syndrome, muscular dystrophy.
- Investigation: CT first; if intraspinal extension is suspected, MRI — shows the dumbbell shape more clearly.
- Treatment: although malignancy is uncommon, resect even if asymptomatic.
