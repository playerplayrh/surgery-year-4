- Intro: the body of knowledge in colorectal surgery is big, heavy and broad; many textbooks exist; in the last 2-3 years new classifications, consensus statements and guidelines have appeared; the author condensed it in one style; for the Board a resident should read what is written here; fellows must know more. ## Embryology
- The GI tract begins to develop at about 4 weeks of gestation, from endoderm; it rolls into a tube by week 6 and divides into 3 segments: foregut, midgut, hindgut.
- Midgut becomes small bowel, ascending colon and proximal transverse colon, supplied by the SMA.
- Hindgut becomes distal transverse colon, descending colon, rectum and proximal anus, supplied by the inferior mesenteric artery.
- Malrotation of the midgut is covered in the pediatric chapter and helps in understanding GI anomalies.

## Anatomy
### Colon
- From the IC valve to the proximal peritoneal reflection; length 3-5 ft; muscle in 2 layers, OLIC (outer longitudinal, inner circular).
- The outer longitudinal layer forms the tenia coli, starting at the cecum, 3 bands (longitudinal muscle covering the bowel only incompletely), making the large bowel puckered into sacs (haustra). Near the rectosigmoid the tenia fuse and cover the rectum all round again; this is where the rectum starts.
- Haustration: the folds that make the colon sacculated; not found in cecum and rectum.
- Appendices epiploicae: fatty tags on the serosal surface, most numerous near the sigmoid.
- Mesentery exists only for the transverse and sigmoid colon.
- Cecum: widest diameter, 7.5-8.5 cm. Sigmoid: narrowest, 2.5 cm.
### Rectum
- Continues from the sigmoid where the tenia fuse, from the sacral promontory following the curve of the sacrum down to the puborectalis, where the anus begins; length about 12-15 cm.
- Valves of Houston: 3 transverse folds, left, right, left.
- Peritoneal reflection: upper 1/3 covered in front and at the sides; middle 1/3 covered in front only, then turns over the bladder (male) or uterus (female) forming the cul-de-sac / pouch of Douglas; lower 1/3 has no peritoneal cover.
- In front of the lower rectum, between rectum and urogenital organs, lies thick Denonvilliers' fascia (important in TME); behind is the rectosacral (Waldeyer's) fascia.
- Endopelvic fascia, 2 layers: visceral layer (thin fascia wrapping the mesorectum with its nodes = fascia propria) and parietal layer covering the sacrum = presacral fascia.
- Position of the rectum measured from the anal verge: lower 4-8 cm; middle 8-12 cm; upper 12-16 cm.
### Blood supply
- Colon has less blood supply than small bowel (about 50%), so is prone to ischemic injury; 80% supplies mucosa and submucosa, so ischemia shows as black mucosa and slough.
- SMA supplies the right colon to the mid transverse colon; IMA supplies the left colon; connected by the marginal artery of Drummond (present in 15-20% of normal people as the linking vessel, as printed).
- Rectum: upper from the superior rectal (hemorrhoidal) artery, a branch of the IMA; middle from the middle rectal artery, branch of the internal iliac; lower from the inferior rectal artery, branch of the internal pudendal artery from the internal iliac.
- Venous drainage follows the arteries, except the IMV which joins the splenic vein.
- Lymphatics follow the arteries up to the cisterna chyli; lower rectum drains in 2 directions: up along the IMA (as upper and middle rectum) and laterally along the internal iliac artery; also to inguinal nodes.
### Autonomic nerves and function
- Sympathetic: T7-T12 form the splanchnic nerves, join the celiac plexus, give branches along the SMA; on the left, fibers from L1-L3 join to form the hypogastric nerves, running behind the rectum, splitting into 2 branches to the pelvic side wall. In men, injury causes failure of semen delivery (retrograde ejaculation into the bladder, dry ejaculation); in women, vaginal dryness.
- Parasympathetic: right and transverse colon from the vagus; left colon from S2-S4 fibers forming the nervi erigentes, concerned with erection and bladder contraction; injury in men gives impotence.
- Bladder emptying: sympathetic (hypogastric) nerve is most at risk at the lateral ligament and at high IMA ligation; injury makes the bladder small and contracted. Parasympathetic injury: atony, urine retention, needs a long Foley.
- Lateral stalks during LAR or APR contain the middle rectal artery; they are not a true ligament but help support the lower rectum.
- Watershed areas: Griffith's point at the splenic flexure (SMA/IMA junction); Sudek's point at the rectosigmoid junction (superior/middle rectal junction). Right colon is vulnerable in systemic low-flow states; Drummond's marginal artery is poorly developed in about 50% there (figure text).
### Anus
- Anus: end of the large bowel from the lower rectum at the anorectal ring to the anal verge. Valves of Houston = rectal ampulla; anatomic anal canal vs surgical anal canal; anorectal ring to dentate line 1.0-1.5 cm; dentate line to anal verge 1.0-1.5 cm.
- Pelvic floor muscle = levator ani: puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, ischiococcygeus. Puborectalis starts from the pubic tubercle, loops behind the rectum as a U-shaped sling; relaxes at defecation.
- Anal canal: from puborectalis (anorectal ring) to the anal margin (junction of internal and external sphincter, matching the anal verge), length about 4 cm. Perianal skin: from the verge outward 5 cm.
- By histology with the dentate (pectinate) line: above it columnar cells; below it squamous epithelium; transitional cells only in the genitourinary system (as printed).
- Above the dentate line: blood and nerve supply as the rectum (autonomic, painless); the transitional zone about 1 cm above has slight pain sensation, so rubber band ligation placed lower than this is very painful. Venous drainage via superior hemorrhoidal vein to IMV. Mucosa has columns of Morgagni; between them are anal crypts, where anal gland ducts open.
- Below the dentate line: 2 cm, pain-sensitive (reason a proctologist trains for this). Upper 1.5 cm below the line is stratified squamous epithelium but not true skin: no hair, no sebaceous or sweat glands = anoderm; lower 0.5 cm is skin-like, dark, thick, with hair and glands. Venous drainage via middle and inferior hemorrhoidal veins to pudendal and internal iliac veins.
- Internal sphincter: from the inner circular muscle, smooth muscle, from the lower rectum to about 1 cm below the dentate line; helps rest continence.
- External sphincter: striated, voluntary; deep, superficial, subcutaneous parts (text lists deep and subcutaneous).
- Anal glands: 10-15 around the canal, mostly opening into anal crypts at the dentate line; secrete a KY-jelly-like lubricant.
- Spaces (levator ani as divider): supralevator, ischioanal, intersphincteric, perianal spaces; transverse septum. Figure: coronal drawing of these spaces.
- Spaces (continued): 1. supralevator space; 2. infralevator space, subdivided into ischiorectal (ischioanal), perianal, intersphincteric, superficial and deep postanal spaces.
- Nodal metastasis of anal canal: upper 2/3 to internal iliac and pelvic nodes; lower 1/3 to inguinal nodes.

## Physiology
- Absorption (mostly at the proximal colon): 1. water; 2. sodium (Na reabsorbed mostly at the right colon); 3. chloride (the reason colon is not used as a conduit in cystectomy: chloride absorption causes chronic acidosis, so urologists use ileum); 4. ammonia, from protein and urea broken down by colonic bacteria; 5. oxalate; 6. short chain fatty acids, from bacterial fermentation of polysaccharides; SCFA are the energy source of colonic mucosa and help sodium transport; 7. glucose, some amino acids and some drugs.
- Secretion: 1. bicarbonate (exchanged for chloride absorption); 2. potassium (exchanged for sodium absorption); 3. mucus.
- Rectum: main function is a reservoir of fecal content.
- Anal continence: needs at least one side of the 3rd sacral nerve root preserved; if S3 is cut on both sides, incontinence occurs.
- Bacteria: the newborn colon is sterile; by about 3 weeks it has adult-type bacteria from the mouth; 1 g of stool holds 10^11-10^12 organisms; colonic bacteria degrade protein and urea. Most common organism is B. fragilis. Beware: after colon surgery, severe sepsis within 48 hr with severe wound pain suggests severe SSI from Clostridium perfringens.

## Flatus
- Gas sources: 1. swallowed gas (nitrogen, oxygen, CO2); 2. gas from bacterial fermentation in the colon. Swallowed air contributes more than bacteria, so nitrogen is the most abundant.
- Passed 200-2,000 cc per day. Foul smell comes from sulfides.
- Reducing foul flatus: 1. avoid gas-producing food, especially starch, yam, beans; 2. avoid strongly smelling food (durian, shrimp paste); 3. avoid constipation; 4. keep perineum and anal verge clean and dry; 5. exercise anal sphincter/pelvic muscles to improve control.
- Explosive gas: hydrogen and methane in the colon and rectum can explode with electrocautery during colonoscopic polypectomy or biopsy; so suction colonic air first; in the past bowel prep with mannitol (fermented by bacteria into explosive gas) was used; mannitol is no longer used for bowel prep.

## Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA)
- A tumor marker used mostly in CRC; normal not over 5 ng/mL.
- Other cancers also raise it: lung, stomach, pancreas, breast, ovary, and others.
- Not useful for screening early stage CRC (stage 1-2), since it is usually not raised; when CEA is high the disease is usually late stage 2 or stage 3; so CEA is not suitable as a screening or diagnostic marker.
- CEA is used to monitor treatment response and detect recurrence/metastasis: check before treatment and after surgery (should return to normal within 6 weeks); then every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months to complete 5 years.
- If CEA rises after surgery: repeat in 1-2 months; if still high, look for where the recurrence is: 1. history and examination (appetite, weight loss, bleeding, abdominal mass); 2. rule out local recurrence (anastomosis) or new lesion by colonoscopy; 3. CT chest, abdomen, pelvis with contrast (lung, liver, abdominal masses, anastomosis, nodes, ovary, pelvic masses, presacral thickening); 4. PET scan when CT gives no answer, since it detects occult metastasis or recurrence better than CT; NCCN 2017 still does not recommend it as routine, only case by case; 5. if still no cause, repeat full CT every 3 months, or PET/CT; if a metachronous lesion is found and resectable, resect; if not resectable, up-grade chemo, reassess; give last chance to resect; otherwise systemic treatment or palliative care; 6. if CEA falls on follow-up, the high value was probably a benign cause.
- CEA to monitor chemo: during treatment check every 2 months; if CEA does not fall and rises more than 2 times, chemo is failing.
- Benign causes of raised CEA: liver disease, lung disease, IBD, PU, kidney, breast cysts, smoking; but not over 10 ng/mL.
- Preoperative CEA over 10 means: 1. larger tumor volume, recurrence about 3.24 times that of normal CEA; 2. survival usually worse than normal-CEA cases, but beware poorly differentiated tumors may secrete little CEA; 3. more likely advanced stage; 4. preop CEA is not used to decide adjuvant treatment or response to chemo; use it only as a monitoring/surveillance marker.

## CT Scan
- Preferred before MRI; preoperative staging (liver, nodes, kidney with IV contrast); follow-up after surgery or chemo, especially with rising CEA; CT shows extraluminal disease well, intraluminal lesion not so clearly.

## MRI
- Better than CT for: 1. muscle or bone invasion; 2. pelvic side wall invasion, especially in rectal cancer or retrorectal tumor; 3. with endorectal coil, very useful for fistula-in-ano tract.

## PET-CT Scan
- Whole body imaging, about 30 minutes; helps separate fibrosis from cancer.
- Disadvantage: expensive, and cannot separate cancer from active inflammation (e.g. UC) since both have high FDG metabolism; if PET after surgery or to assess chemo, wait 2 months; after RT wait 3 months for inflammation to subside.
- Limitation: masses under 5 mm, or mucinous type with few cells, may not be seen.

## CT Colonography
- 3D helical CT with oral and rectal contrast to detect intraluminal masses (which ordinary CT does not see); can also see lesions proximal to a colonic obstruction, which BE and colonoscopy cannot.
- Non-invasive compared with colonoscopy, so suits high-risk patients or those on anticoagulant.
- Disadvantages: 1. needs bowel prep like colonoscopy; 2. small polyps (under 5 mm) are missed; 3. no tissue diagnosis and no treatment: any lesion found needs colonoscopy again.

## Endoscopy (lengths)
- Anoscopy 8 cm (anal canal); proctoscopy 25 cm (rigid sigmoidoscope); flexible sigmoidoscopy 60 cm (reaches splenic flexure); colonoscopy 160 cm (whole colon).

## Colonoscopy
- Indication: 1. evaluation of GI symptoms (bleeding, pain, constipation/diarrhea of unknown cause, suspected colon disease, abnormal BE); 2. diagnosis of colonic masses with tissue, and exclude synchronous lesions in polyp or cancer patients; 3. cancer surveillance in UC, FAP, HNPCC; 4. treatment: volvulus, Ogilvie's, dilatation of stricture, polypectomy; 5. ASGE recommends it for iron deficiency anemia or weight loss of unknown cause.
- Contraindication: 1. acute peritoneal inflammation with guarding/rigidity, e.g. GI perforation, peritonitis needing laparotomy, active diverticulitis and active IBD (risk of perforation); 2. acute MI, especially within 3 weeks; 3. shock or hemodynamic instability; 4. very large AAA or iliac aneurysm; 5. bleeding disorder; 6. pregnancy; 7. recent gynecologic or colonic surgery; 8. uncooperative patient.
- Operator: by US (ASGE) standard, an expert should have done over 100 cases (patients used for washing do not count).
- Bowel prep: before scoping prep so polyps are seen; RCT (AJGE 2010): polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution 2 L as a split-dose, 1 L starting 7 hr before and another 1 L about 4 hr before colonoscopy, scoping in the afternoon of the same day. Light anesthesia may be used, not necessarily routine.

### Colonoscopic complications
- Hemorrhage: most common, 3%; acute or delayed, usually after polypectomy. Treatment: conservative first (IV, blood, vital signs, serial Hct); most stop; if not, colonoscopic treatment (cautery, injection, clip).
- Perforation: 0.1%, the most serious; most common site rectosigmoid, then cecum (varies by indication). Diagnostic colonoscopy 0.02%, usually at rectosigmoid/sigmoid (sharp angulation); therapeutic colonoscopy 5% [as printed], usually at cecum or at the polypectomy site, especially sessile polyps.
  - Symptoms from minimal to peritonitis; suspect perforation if abdominal pain after the scope; large hole gives rapid symptoms. Diagnosis: examination (abdomen distended, hard) or seeing the hole/omentum/bowel loop during scoping; plain abdomen upright for free air (or perinephric air); if free perforation, go to OR; if suspected but no free air, CT.
  - Treatment: most need laparotomy; choose by patient state and abdomen: 1.1 simple suture: small hole, not over 50% circumference, no fecal peritonitis, no cancer; 1.2 colon resection: large hole with tight/edematous edges, or underlying colon disease (cancer, large polyp); anastomose if safe, otherwise ostomy; 1.3 laparoscopic treatment: reported feasible; 1.4 endoscopic closure by clip, for a hole not over 1 cm noted during the scope; covered metal stent reported in case reports.
  - Non-operative treatment (postpolypectomy coagulation syndrome / transmural burn syndrome): from thermal injury at polypectomy; transmural injury or serosal irritation: abdominal pain, fever, localized peritonitis, high WBC, no free air; success up to 73% in selected cases (patients are prepped, so little contamination). Criteria (Christie/Marrazzo): 2.1 condition not deteriorating; 2.2 no peritonitis signs (rigid, guarding); 2.3 healthy patient, bowel prepped; 2.4 retroperitoneal perforation; 2.5 CT with contrast: small contained perforation, thick bowel with fat stranding, pericolic fluid, no free intraperitoneal leakage. Admit, serial examination, WBC, x-ray, NPO, IV antibiotic; usually better within 48 hr; if symptoms worsen or abdomen rigid, open promptly.
- Cautions for colonoscopy: 1. needs training with an experienced supervisor; 2. prior abdominal surgery/adhesions, especially women after TAH; 3. women are harder than men (longer sigmoid with more angulation); 4. thin patients kink easily; obese patients need abdominal pressure; 5. diverticular disease: hard (spasm, small lumen, much insufflation), avoid if diverticulum inflamed; 6. loops/angulation, esp. sigmoid and splenic flexure; 7. good prep, no residual stool; 8. do not force (many lawsuits); 9. if expected difficult (diverticular disease, IBS, uncooperative), use anesthesia.

## Barium Enema
- Use double contrast now, after bowel prep. If perforation feared (obstruction), use water-soluble contrast though less clear; usually one only needs to know if there is an obstruction; beware rectosigmoid lesions that BE often misses.
- BE with flexible sigmoidoscopy is acceptable, but if colonoscopy is available do colonoscopy.
- BE perforation: 0.02%; from the tip of the nozzle, especially at the anterior rectal wall, too much air, too much pressure; idiopathic ones occur at weak points (cecum); with underlying disease (ulcer, cancer, IBD, diverticulum) it ruptures at the diseased point. Figure: BE with perforation at the upper rectum (skip).
- Perforation through the anus or through a colostomy opening carries the same risk: barium plus feces makes severe peritonitis; mortality up to 50%; survivors often develop stricture at the perforation site because barium causes a strong reaction.
- Treatment: extraperitoneal perforation: symptoms mild (bowel was prepped): observe, NPO, IV antibiotic. Intraperitoneal perforation: sepsis soon; explore laparotomy, wipe off the barium stuck on serosa and bowel loops as much as possible; resect colon and make a diverting colostomy.
- Prevention: 1. barium bag not higher than 1 meter above the patient; 2. nozzle tip not deep in the anus, do not overinflate; 3. if biopsy or polypectomy was done less than 7 days earlier, no BE.
- The author's experience: 3 cases of Ba peritonitis; text mortality 50% (his own all died).

## Stercoral Perforation
- Perforation of the colon from hard stool packing and pressing the colonic wall for long until pressure necrosis. Figures (skip).
- Found in the elderly who cannot care for themselves, with chronic constipation; usual site rectosigmoid junction (narrowest); hole over 1 cm (mean 3 cm), on the antimesenteric border; may see a stercoral ulcer beside; stool mass lying at the hole.
- Plain film/CT: stool-filled colon, large masses, with free air.
- Treatment: some advise closing the hole and proximal diversion; the author thinks that is not a good answer: resect and Hartmann; the hole at the rectosigmoid cannot be made an ostomy; the answer of resection then immediate anastomosis is also wrong because these patients are old and toxic (leak).

## Endorectal Ultrasound (ERUS)
- Done at OPD, no sedation, no bowel prep; a Fleet enema suffices; for the anal canal it is called endoanal US (EAUS). Figure: ERUS layers: hyperechoic balloon-mucosa interface, hypoechoic mucosa, hyperechoic submucosa, hypoechoic muscularis propria, hyperechoic perirectal fat; T3 tumor with finger-like projections.
- Advantages: 1. investigation of choice for staging rectal cancer: thickness (T) best, better than CT; node (N) good, similar to MRI, and guides biopsy; anal cancer: use EAUS; 2. assess tumor shrinkage after preop RT; 3. follow up and rule out recurrence; 4. benign conditions: assess sphincter injury in fecal incontinence; complex fistula tract and deep abscess location; find the cause of anorectal pain (small hidden deep abscess).
- Disadvantages: staging tends to over-stage more than under-stage; cannot tell the tumor type; cannot tell if an enlarged node is metastasis or inflammation.

## Colostomy and Ileostomy
- Ostomy: opening part of the GI tract through the skin to drain contents. Residents should know the complications of ostomy and how to fix them.
- Indications: 1. relieve obstruction, decompress proximal bowel; 2. protective ostomy: so contents do not pass the anastomosis (does not reduce leak but reduces its consequences); 3. keep contents away from a diseased segment; 4. as the anus after APR or when the anus must be removed (e.g. severe complicated anal Crohn).
- Types: end, loop, double barrel.
- Colostomy: colon brought out to the abdominal wall; temporary (leaving bowel at rest, bypass: complicated diverticulitis with abscess, rupture or fistula; closed when its purpose is done) or permanent (e.g. APR, or palliative in very advanced CRC).
- Ileostomy: terminal ileum brought out; contents liquid 500-800 cc/day = low output; over 1,000 cc/day = high output, which may cause fluid and electrolyte imbalance; small bowel content irritates skin strongly, so good skin protection is needed.
- Temporary ileostomy: usually a loop ileostomy as a protective ileostomy in low anastomoses, e.g. coloanal anastomosis, IPAA, sphincter reconstruction; when no longer needed, before closing do endoscopy or contrast enema to check patency.
- Permanent ileostomy: end ileostomy for disease where the rectum/colon cannot be kept:
  1. Brooke ileostomy (end or terminal ileostomy): the end of the ileum is brought out and the cut edge sutured to skin everted 2-3 cm, which reduces skin excoriation; disadvantage: content comes out all the time. Figure: eversion steps (skip).
  2. Continent ileostomy / Kock pouch: stores content in a pouch; when wanted, a tube is passed through the opening to drain it, to control the passage; but complications are many (intraabdominal abscess, peritonitis), so few do it now. Figure: Kock pouch with nipple valve (skip).

## Cecostomy
- Indications are now few: 1. Ogilvie's syndrome; 2. cecal volvulus. Purpose is decompression, not diversion (for diversion a loop ileostomy is better); a tube cecostomy is usually made.

## Complications of Ostomy
- Early: necrosis/ischemia; retraction. Late: many; the one the author says teachers like to ask: parastomal (paracolostomy) hernia.
### Necrosis or ischemia
- Cause: poor blood supply of the bowel brought out: pulled too tight, opening in the skin/fascia too narrow strangling the bowel, or too much mesentery cut. Appears within the first 24 hours.
- Treatment: 1. if only mucosal ischemia (oozing, swollen), or ischemia above the fascia (sheath): observe; to judge depth use a test-tube test (or video-assisted test tube test: flexible endoscope through the stoma): if mucosa is still viable or ischemia is above the fascia level, observe. 2. If the ischemia extends below the abdominal wall fascia or necrosis is clear, redo the stoma by cutting away the dead part.
### Retraction
- Cause: bowel brought out is pulled too much, or inflamed skin and subcutaneous tissue around the ostomy; common in the obese. Treatment: if not yet slipped back into the abdomen, observe; if it slips into the abdomen (peritonitis risk), hurry to reopen and redo.
### Skin excoriation
- Seen often in ileostomy, next transverse colostomy (alkaline, liquid contents). Mostly due to a poorly placed stoma opening so the bag does not seal, or an end ostomy whose tip stands too little above the skin (should be 2-3 cm so contents fall into the bag). Treatment: stomahesive powder or skin barrier before sticking the bag.
### Parastomal hernia
- An incisional hernia around the ostomy; small ones show bulging around the opening on coughing/straining; more in colostomy than ileostomy.
- If large, the bag cannot be applied, with pain and pulling; the fearful problem is bowel sliding into the sac; if the neck is narrow it can incarcerate.
- Most common late complication of end colostomy: after APR, followed 5 years, up to 50% (if the patient survives); obstruction or strangulation is rare.
- Cause unclear; 2 main: 1. surgical technique (opening too large, or stoma not passed through the rectus sheath); 2. patient factors: obesity, weak abdominal wall muscle, malnutrition, or raised intra-abdominal pressure (chronic cough, difficulty urinating, BPH).
- Small and asymptomatic: observe and follow.
- Treatment: 1. non-operative: for those with true problems, find a belt or a bag that fits, use a belt for temporary ostomy until closure; 2. surgical: failure/recurrence up to 20%; indications: 2.1 bowel in the sac with incarceration, strangulation, perforation; 2.2 pain; 2.3 so large the bag cannot be applied or leaks cause skin excoriation; 2.4 cosmetic.
- Surgical options: 1. local fascial repair (intra- or extraperitoneal approach): incise around the ostomy, close the fascia defect or use mesh; for the intraperitoneal approach a midline incision and repair from inside, ostomy staying in place; suits small sac and defect right at the opening, first time (not recurrent), poor tissue not suitable [as printed]; 2. stomal relocation: moves the opening to a new site; suits cases where the old site cannot be used (weak muscle/fascia), much skin excoriation, or recurrence after local repair; 3. laparoscopic mesh repair: now the most popular, lowest recurrence. Figure: laparoscopic mesh (skip).
- Prevention: opening in the skin about 2 fingers wide; stoma through the rectus sheath; suture the seromuscular layer of the bowel to the fascia; treat chronic cough and difficulty urinating.
### Diversion colitis
- Cause: no content passing the distal colon, so no short chain fatty acids (acetate, butyrate, propionate) made by bacteria, the main energy source of colonic mucosa; mucosa atrophies and gets inflamed.
- Treatment: 1. enemas of short chain fatty acids into the distal ostomy; 2. close the colostomy and restore the old route; once stool passes the distal colon, symptoms go in about 3 weeks.
### Others
- Prolapse: from a skin opening too big, often loop colostomy; if temporary and no symptoms, observe; if ischemia or obstruction, operate.
- Stenosis: try dilation; if not working, revise or redo.
- Hemorrhage, perforation, fistula, gut obstruction: seldom asked; read elsewhere.

## Optimal Stoma Site
- Complications often arise because the opening is at an unsuitable abdominal wall site.
- Suitable site:
  1. Prepare before surgery with preoperative counseling about temporary or permanent ostomy.
  2. Have the patient stand, sit, lean forward and compare with the belt line so there is no trouble with waistband or belt.
  3. The patient can see their own ostomy (in the obese, place it higher) to care for it and change bags.
  4. Skin flat, not in a fold or skin crease, so the bag sticks without leakage.
  5. Away from the surgical wound; flat, not near the umbilicus, bony prominences or costal margin.
  6. Away from the umbilicus.
  7. Exit through the rectus sheath to reduce hernia.
  8. If not marked beforehand (emergency), use points 4, 5, 6, 7.
  9. First days use a transparent bag so the bowel can be watched (fear is ischemia/necrosis).
- Generally mark at the midpoint of the line from the umbilicus to the ASIS, or place within the triangle formed by the umbilicus, ASIS and pubic bone.

## Colon Obstruction
- Always think of colon obstruction in a patient over 50 with abdominal distension, pain, obstipation, no flatus.
- Etiology: 1. malignancy 60-70%; 2. diverticulitis 15% (acute inflammation or diverticular stricture); 3. volvulus under 5%; 4. a few: IBD, Ogilvie, intussusception, stricture, fecal impaction, foreign body.
- History: 1. abrupt or insidious pain; 2. colicky pain or not, history of toxicity/fever/bloody stool (think TMC); 3. history of antispasmodic (Ogilvie); 4. bowel habit change and family history of CRC.
- PE: do not forget PR (mass within reach, or a metastatic pelvic mass from stomach cancer).
- Plain abdomen: first; whoever jumps to CT first gets scolded. Resident should say: 1. mechanical or functional (mechanical: air-fluid level with different heights in the same loop); 2. level of obstruction (bowel gas distribution, air in rectum or not); 3. incomplete or complete, closed loop or not; 4. competency of IC valve (dilated small bowel or not); 5. complication, e.g. free air = perforation.
- Rigid/flexible sigmoidoscope and colonoscope: if experience is little, beware of perforation; however the scope has a role in treatment of volvulus and Ogilvie, and placing SEMS.
- CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast enema: preferred imaging in patients suspected of large bowel obstruction; do it whenever possible, especially for malignancy and intussusception.
- Low pressure gastrografin enema: water-soluble contrast by gravity, with the operator watching himself; do not squeeze or push pressure (perforation risk); if leakage, gastrografin is safer than barium; tells location and degree of obstruction and separates true from pseudo-obstruction.
- BE should not be done: 1. barium may turn incomplete into complete obstruction; 2. if perforated by barium, mortality is very high; 3. BE before CT blocks CT interpretation.

## Colon Obstruction from Cancer
- 15-20% of CRC present with colon obstruction; 70% are on the left side, 30% proximal to the splenic flexure; 40% of left-sided are at the sigmoid colon. Figure: BE (dilated cecum, dilated small bowel, CA colon) (skip).
- Management: 1. NG tube, IV fluid, correct electrolytes; 2. Foley for urine output; 3. broad spectrum IV antibiotic. Then investigations: plain abdomen and gastrografin enema.
- If stable with incomplete obstruction: elective surgery, with bowel prep and correction/treatment of comorbid disease.
- Even complete obstruction, if there is a helper such as a stent, put the stent first to turn an emergency into an elective operation.
- Conditions where one must not wait: prepare the patient and go to OR quickly: 1. free air; 2. suspected gangrene (high fever, high WBC, rapid heart rate, rigid abdomen); 3. gas in the colonic wall (pneumatosis intestinalis).
- Before surgery do not forget DVT prophylaxis, and mark the colostomy site, since one may need it.
### Non-operative colonic decompression
- Helpers to change emergency into elective resection, 4 methods: 1. endoscopic treatment with self-expandable metal stent (SEMS), which seems to work best; 2. laser; 3. balloon dilation; 4. transanal tube.
- Choice of procedure for the best quality of life depends on: 1. patient state, cause and level of obstruction; 2. whether there is complication; 3. degree of contamination; 4. preop sphincter status; 5. surgeon's experience, team readiness, hospital.
- Good quality of life, as the author puts it: can defecate (sphincter preservation), sex still possible, control of voiding.
- If only palliation is wanted: judge whether the patient will live less than 2 months and needs only supportive care: a stent is better than a permanent colostomy, since the stent does not yet perforate before the patient dies; includes patients with much ascites (stent better than palliative colostomy). But if expected to live over 2 months and there is a plan for chemo or RT, a palliative colostomy is better.

### Right-sided obstruction
- Obstruction of the large bowel from cecum to splenic flexure.
- Good-risk patient (young, not in shock, no peritonitis, experienced team): right hemicolectomy or extended right hemicolectomy then anastomose at once; mortality and morbidity no different from non-obstructed cases.
- High-risk patient: resect, and if not joining, bring out an ostomy first.

### Left-sided obstruction
- Obstruction from the splenic flexure to the rectosigmoid. Options:
  1. Resection without anastomosis: two-staged, proximal colostomy, distal closed as Hartmann; suits elderly with comorbidity; safe, removes the cancer, no anastomosis; disadvantage: 2 operations.
  2. Resection + on-table lavage then primary anastomosis (SCOTIA study group): mobilize the whole colon and bring it onto the abdominal wall, cut the bowel above the tumor, put a sterile corrugated tube into the proximal end, cut the appendix stump (or put a Foley No. 22-24 through the appendix stump), then irrigate with NSS 6-8 L over about 30 min until the effluent is clear; then do oncologic resection and join at once. Figures (skip). Disadvantages: time-consuming, contamination, high SSI, some reports leak up to 30%; a study compared with manual decompression (squeezing stool out) found no difference in mortality/morbidity; the author does not like intraoperative irrigation much; if resecting, he prefers subtotal colectomy.
  3. Subtotal colectomy with primary anastomosis: select by criteria: 3.1 first tumor can be removed completely with a distal free margin of 5 cm; 3.2 patient has low operative risk; 3.3 massive distended colon or right colon ischemia of doubtful viability; 3.4 cecal perforation; 3.5 synchronous lesion (found 3.5-5%); 3.6 perforation in hereditary colon cancer syndrome. Disadvantage: stool frequency 2-4 times a day, usually improving within 6 months. Problem: if only a split tenia (serosal tear) of the cecum is found, do subtotal colectomy right away or not? still argued. Big names such as Prof. Santhat cheer subtotal; the author warns elderly patients with lax sphincters may have watery stool problems after subtotal.
  4. Three-staged: suits high-risk patients, severe sepsis, shock or widespread intraabdominal cancer; the simplest and safest: colostomy first; 2nd stage resection; 3rd stage closure; the author uses it as the last resort; often only the colostomy is done and the patient is not fit for stages 2 and 3.
- In the past 2-stage was preferred; at present one-stage (resection and anastomosis) is cheered more.

### Colonic stent (SEMS)
- Topic discussed at proctology conferences: role in treating obstruction from cancer. Figure: stent and specimen (skip).
- Used with left-sided obstruction as a bridge to decompression: place the stent so both ends lie about 2 cm beyond the obstruction; success 96% (88-100%); once decompressed, prep the bowel and prepare the patient for one-stage elective resection without a stoma; about 50-60% of patients are candidates; after SEMS chemotherapy can be given if needed. Disadvantages reported: perforation 4.5%, reobstruction 12%. Figure: technique steps A-D (guide wire through lesion, partial deployment, pull back, full deployment).
- Advantages: bridge to surgery then laparoscopic resection within about 2 weeks [as printed "ภายใน 2 สัปดาห์"] of stent; compared with emergency surgery: shorter hospital stay by 5-8 days, less need for ICU, less emergency surgery, fewer ostomies (primary anastomosis more feasible, less leak), less blood loss, less pain medication; bridge to subsequent resection does not worsen oncologic outcome; more lymph nodes can be examined than emergency surgery; SEMS does not add overall mortality.
- Contraindications: 1. perforation or peritonitis already (urgent surgery, no time); 2. low-lying mass under 4 cm from the anal verge (most shortest is 6 cm), since the stent will protrude through the anus and press on the external sphincter, very painful; 3. tortuous rectum; 4. right-sided obstruction, because it can be resected and joined at once, and SEMS is costly.
- Note: now SEMS is an alternative in hospitals with readiness and a person who can do it, in suitable cases; NCCN says consider SEMS as a selected case.

## Rectal Cancer Obstruction
- More trouble than colon obstruction: after resection with Hartmann, the 2nd operation is very hard; if the obstruction is very low a stent cannot be used; so the advice is to look at where the obstruction is in the rectum.
- Upper or mid rectal obstruction: 1. first step decompression: SEMS if possible; if not, transverse or sigmoid colostomy first; 2. next, give chemoRT, or in some cases pass on to LAR then join.
- Lower rectal obstruction: 1. sigmoid colostomy first; 2. chemoRT if needed; 3. finally resection and ultralow anastomosis; 4. remember: lower rectum cannot take SEMS (protrudes through the anus, very painful).

## Sigmoid Volvulus
- From redundant colon twisting around its own mesentery, which has a narrow, fixed base; a closed-loop obstruction; torsion of 180 degrees already gives clinical obstruction.
- Location: sigmoid 79%, cecum 20%, others rare.
- Typical presentation: 1. elderly living at home or in a nursing home, CVA bedridden, cannot help themselves, chronic constipation, history of taking laxatives; 2. abdominal distension, pain, nausea, low gut obstruction picture.
- Differential: Ogilvie, cancer, mesenteric ischemia, TMC.
- Plain film: classic in 80%: bent inner tube, coffee bean sign; air-fill in RUQ like omega loop or inverted U shape with no air in rectum; if air in LUQ suspect cecal volvulus. Figures (skip).
- Gastrografin enema: bird beak deformity at LLQ, contrast stops abruptly at the torsion, smooth mucosa; not necessarily barium (colonoscopy harder, and if barium packs until the bowel ruptures the surgeon is blamed); gastrografin enema as a package only where diagnosis is doubtful or to rule out cancer.
- CT: whirling of mesenteric vessels, U-shaped or spiral loop of collapsed colon; if bowel is rotten, air in colonic wall or portal vein.
- Management: if no peritonitis or indication for emergency surgery: nonoperative decompression with a rigid sigmoidoscope (or flexible sigmoidoscope/colonoscope gently pushed through the torsion, then untwist the sigmoid); the author prefers flexible; at detorsion, stool mixed with flatus gushes out.
  - Detorsion success 90%; then leave a rectal tube No. 36 F in the rectum to maintain it and help decompression; blind rectal-tube passage in such obstruction should not be done.
  - After detorsion, without operation, recurrence is 50%; so prep bowel, colonoscopy to rule out proximal neoplasm, prepare for surgery in the same hospitalization.
- Elective surgery: sigmoidectomy then anastomose; colopexy alone is not good (recurs 16-70%).
- Emergency surgery: sigmoidectomy without anastomosis, bring out an ostomy first.
- Indications for emergency surgery: 1. peritonitis (strangulation, gangrene, perforation); 2. unsuccessful decompression; 3. at endoscopy: mucosal ischemia, ulcer, bloody diarrhea, dark blood, or systemic sepsis: do not detort, drag to OR (remember).
- Watch-and-wait after successful detorsion is not appropriate, except in truly poor-risk patients: endoscopic sigmoidopexy like PEG to fix the colon so it does not twist again.

## Cecal Volvulus
- Patients are younger than sigmoid volvulus. 2 types: 1. axial ileocolic volvulus (90%): cecum and ileum twist and move to LUQ; 2. cecal bascule: the cecum flips upward in front of the horizontal plane without torsion.
- Associations: prior abdominal surgery (esp. appendectomy); highly mobile cecum from failure of fusion of the mesentery with the parietal peritoneum at the right paracolic gutter; obstruction of transverse or left colon. No association with IBD.
- Blood supply is compromised; bowel rots faster than sigmoid volvulus.
- Symptoms: abdominal pain and distension. Plain abdomen: air-filled bowel loop opposite to sigmoid volvulus, air-fill at LUQ and no air at distal colon; if suspected send CT: whirl sign, colon dilated in the mid abdomen toward the left. Figure (skip).
- Treatment: axial ileocolic volvulus: right hemicolectomy then anastomose immediately; cecal bascule: if cecum still viable, detort then fix with cecopexy or tube cecostomy (recurrence 10%); if viability in doubt, cecal resection. Decompression and detorsion by scope are hard and do not work: do not answer that. Cecopexy or suturing the cecum to the lateral abdominal wall does not work well (high recurrence, suture may tear the wall): keep for really high-risk cases.

## Transverse Colon Volvulus
- Very rare, from long mobile transverse colon with narrow-based mesocolon. Detorsion is hard; imaging resembles sigmoid volvulus but more proximal. Treatment usually transverse colon resection, anastomose if possible; colonoscopic detorsion rarely succeeds.
- Figure (algorithm, suspected colonic volvulus): sigmoid colon: rigid sigmoidoscope: rectal tube decompression, gentle mechanical bowel prep, surgery; unable to decompress endoscopically: surgery. Gangrenous bowel or perforation: surgery (resection with end stoma and Hartmann closure of rectum or mucous fistula). Remaining colon (cecum, transverse, splenic flexure): Hypaque enema, then surgery.

## Acute Colonic Pseudo-Obstruction (ACPO)
- Better known as Ogilvie's syndrome or colonic ileus: massive colonic dilation without mechanical obstruction. True cause unknown.
- Predisposing: 1. trauma, burn, or after neuro/ortho surgery with long bed rest; 2. pneumonia, sepsis, CHF, MI, MOF; 3. metastatic malignancy; 4. electrolyte imbalance (remember: hypo-, e.g. hyponatremia and hypokalemia, common in elderly on long-term diuretics); 5. neurolytic drugs: morphine, narcotics, anticholinergic, calcium channel blocker.
- Remember hypernatremia and hyperkalemia are wrong answers; the disease is not much related to anemia or mesenteric ischemia.
- Some believe it is an imbalance of ANS: normally sympathetic reduces motility and parasympathetic increases it; here sympathetic is excessive (not parasympathetic).
- Symptoms: like large bowel obstruction; mostly only distension, not much pain, not clearly colicky, tympanic abdomen, bowel sounds weak or absent.
- Evaluation: 1. exclude mechanical obstruction and TMC first; 2. peritonitis or perforation? 3. cecal diameter: under 12 cm fairly safe; over 12 cm, begin to prepare.
- Risk of cecal perforation when ACPO lasts over 6 days or cecal diameter on plain film over 12 cm.
- Plain film: colonic distension with air from cecum to rectum without a cut-off; with IC valve incompetence, small bowel air-fluid levels at the same level in the same loop. CT: dilated colon with no transition point. Water-soluble contrast enema: advised in every case to rule out mechanical obstruction.

### ACPO management
- After ruling out mechanical obstruction (water-soluble enema) and peritonitis needing surgery, usually conservative; most improve within 24-72 hr. Step by step:
  1. Conservative: 1.1 NPO, NG tube, rectal tube for gravity drainage; 1.2 correct fluid and electrolytes; 1.3 stop narcotics, anticholinergics, antidepressants, calcium channel blockers, Parkinson drugs; 1.4 position: prone or knee-chest, alternating left/right decubitus every hour; 1.5 tube decompression by a short rectal tube does not help (cannot decompress the proximal colon); 1.6 percutaneous endoscopic colostomy (PEC) needs an endoscopist with skill and still has a high failure rate (leakage, infection).
  2. If not better within 24-72 hr (more than 3 days), or cecal diameter over 12 cm from the start: IV neostigmine: cholinergic action stimulating the colon to contract (not anticholinergic); effect within 1-10 min with explosive flatus; half life 30-60 min; dose 2 mg in 100 cc NSS IV drip; clinical response 70-90% within 5 min; recurrence after the first dose 11-18% (10-40% in another place), so a second dose is allowed, with cardiac monitoring.
     - Adverse reactions: bradycardia (not tachycardia) about 20%: keep the patient lying, monitor EKG at least 30 min after, atropine ready; nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramping; sweating, salivation; increased airway secretion (beware bronchospasm); dizziness.
     - Contraindications: mechanical intestinal or urinary obstruction; peritonitis; pregnancy; creatinine over 3 (renal excretion); bradycardia (HR under 60) or arrhythmia; bronchospasm; recent MI; hypotension (SBP under 90).
     - Neostigmine does not act on neuropeptide P, so cannot treat postoperative ileus. IV erythromycin 3-10 days is sometimes mentioned but evidence is weak.
  3. Colonoscopic decompression: for failure of or contraindication to neostigmine: after suctioning gas and stool, pass a tube to the right colon, flush gently every 6-8 hr for about 3 days, drain by gravity; overall success about 88%; careful, perforation 3%; if peritonitis, do not use. Figure: tube in colon on film (skip).
  4. Surgery as the last option: 4.1 if the cecum is rotten: resect (right side: anastomose if safe, otherwise ostomy); 4.2 if the cecum is not rotten: cecostomy with a Malecot No. over 32 as for Stamm gastrostomy, flush often with NSS to avoid tube blockage; in recent years percutaneous colonoscopic cecostomy (like PEG) has similar results but 40% have problems early (wound infection, bleeding); new methods awaited, e.g. CT-guided percutaneous cecostomy.
- Figure (algorithm): acute colonic distention -> rule out mechanical obstruction, ischemia, perforation -> conservative management x 24 hours -> no improvement, or cecum over 12 cm, or distention over 3 days -> IV neostigmine 2 mg: success: stop; repeat if partial response or recurrence; no improvement: colonoscopy.

## Adult Colonic Intussusception
- Cause of about 1% of colon obstruction, 20% of them complete obstruction.
- Intussusception is found in children 95%, in adults 5%; the proximal segment (intussusceptum) slides into the distal (intussuscipiens).
- In adults there is nearly always a lead point lesion, with chance of cancer 56%, or Meckel, polyp, diverticulum; so do not use air contrast enema reduction as in children. Figure: specimen (final diagnosis lymphoma).
- CT abdomen: the most accurate imaging; classic target sign / bowel-within-bowel.
- In adults: once diagnosed, open and resect the diseased segment, anastomose if possible.

## Adult Hirschsprung Disease
- Functional obstruction from loss of ganglion cells as in children; difference is severity and age at diagnosis; adult mean age about 69 [as printed], short segment at the distal rectum, so symptoms are less severe than in children.
- Diagnosis: anal manometry shows no rectoanal inhibitory reflex (the normal response to distension of the distal rectum); no reflex means the internal sphincter tone is not lowered, causing chronic constipation; BE picture as in children.
- Treatment: proctectomy removing the aganglionic segment, with anal mucosectomy and coloanal anastomosis.

## Diverticular Disease
- A sac-like protrusion of the colonic wall through a weak point where the vasa recta penetrate the wall to reach the mucosa; chronic trauma, i.e. alternating contraction and relaxation of the muscularis propria, gives mucosal herniation; at the herniation only mucosa covers the vessel, so chronic trauma thins the media of some vessels, hence easy bleeding.
- 75% are on the left side; most have no symptoms; over 80 years found in about 70% on examination.
- Residents meet it often: pathophysiology, factors for diverticulitis, most effective treatment, antibiotic in uncomplicated disease, indications for surgery.
### Terminology
- Diverticulum (singular, one sac), diverticula (more than one).
- Diverticular disease: diverticulum with symptoms or complications (bleeding, acute diverticulitis).
- Diverticulosis: diverticulum without inflammation.
- Diverticulitis: diverticulum with inflammation.
- Cause: unclear; some blame low-fiber diet; some say abnormal colon wall; chronic contraction raises intracolonic pressure (straining at constipation); more in the elderly (long colon); obesity, NSAID, and seasonal change [as printed, a joke].
- Sigmoid colon is narrowest and the tenia (outer longitudinal muscle, 3 strips) do not cover the whole circumference, so diverticula occur there most; rectum is wrapped completely by outer longitudinal muscle and has wide diameter, so rarely.
- Incidence: West: left side mostly; Asia: right side more (Japan, Korea more on the right than our country). Age: under 40 years not over 10%; 60 years 30%; over 85 years 85%.
- Asymptomatic diverticulum: found incidentally on BE or scope; more than 50-80% never have symptoms and need no treatment, only advice: high fiber 30 g/day, much water, avoid constipation, avoid fatty food; 10-25% have mild pain; not over 25% develop inflammation (diverticulitis), 70-80% of whom are treated with drugs; about 5% are complicated.

## Diverticulitis
- In the past 10-25% of diverticulosis was thought to develop diverticulitis; the reliable figure now is only about 10%; spectrum from mild symptoms to severe complications such as abscess or perforation.
- Pathophysiology: previously an infectious process; now thought to be an inflammatory process of the colon resembling IBD-type autoimmune (mast cells in the wall, proinflammatory cytokines).
- Symptoms: age over 50 with LLQ pain, obstipation, leukocytosis: think sigmoid diverticulitis, from stasis or obstruction at the neck of the diverticulum causing inflammation; the belief that low fiber causes inflammation lacks significant statistical support (still advise high fiber).
  1. If untreated, severe inflammation, mucosal ischemia, microperforation and local pericolic infection: tenderness at the left lower abdomen.
  2. Large hole or free perforation: fecal contamination, abscess or generalized peritonitis.
- Lab: WBC up, CRP over 50 mg/L. Figure: diverticula; diverticulitis with rupture (skip).
- Plain film: little use, not specific, but still an initial test for abdominal pain/complication: may find intraperitoneal free air.
- US: sensitivity and specificity about 90%; useful for right-sided diverticulitis, which resembles acute appendicitis.
- CT abdomen/pelvis with triple contrast (oral, IV, rectal): most accurate, sensitivity and specificity 97-99%.
- If history and presentation fit, plain abdomen shows no free air or obstruction, and the case seems uncomplicated diverticulitis: advice is to treat right away without CT; do CT only with indication: 1. uncertain diagnosis; 2. complicated case, severe presentation; 3. antibiotics for 2-3 days without improvement or worsening.
- Currently some say send CT in every case to diagnose and stage severity to guide treatment; if contraindicated (allergy, pregnancy), use US or MRI.
- CT criteria for diverticulitis: 1. pericolic fat stranding, fluid at the base of mesentery, vascular engorgement; 2. colonic wall thickening over 4 mm; 3. fluid or contrast collection within the thickened colonic wall; 4. pelvic abscess and extrapelvic abscess; 5. extraluminal air or extraluminal contrast (perforation); 6. colovesical fistula.
- Remember: 1. during inflammation do not do contrast enema or endoscopy (raise intracolonic pressure and perforate); colonoscopy should be done after inflammation subsides, about 6 weeks, to rule out cancer; 2. severity classification: European Endoscopic Surgeons system and Hinchey (most use Hinchey); 3. original Hinchey (1978) by clinical and surgical findings, 4 stages; 4. now modified Hinchey with CT.

### Clinical features
1. Acute diverticulitis: fever, LLQ pain, leukocytosis (microperforation).
2. Chronic diverticulitis: symptoms of chronic obstruction, bloating, narrow stool for at least 2 months; presumably stricture, which needs malignancy excluded.
3. Complicated diverticulitis: abscess, fistula, obstruction, perforation.
4. Right-sided diverticulitis: resembles acute appendicitis.
5. Symptomatic uncomplicated diverticular disease (SUDD): abdominal pain from diverticula without inflammation; colicky pain from visceral hypersensitivity, relieved by passing flatus or stool; resembles IBS.
6. Segmental colitis associated with diverticulosis (SCAD): chronic inflammation in a segment with multiple diverticula, causing abdominal pain, diarrhea, bloody stool, often resolving on its own, resembling chronic IBD.

### Modified Hinchey classification
- Wasvary 1999 modified the original Hinchey 1978 by adding category 0 (mild clinical diverticulitis) and splitting category I into Ia (confined pericolic inflammation or phlegmon) and Ib (confined pericolic abscess, within the sigmoid mesocolon, size under 5 cm).

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | Mild clinical diverticulitis |
| Ia | Confined pericolic inflammation or phlegmon |
| Ib | Confined pericolic abscess |
| II | Pelvic, distant intra-abdominal or retroperitoneal abscess |
| III | Generalized purulent peritonitis |
| IV | Generalized fecal peritonitis |
- Kaiser 2005 matched CT findings to the modified Hinchey categories: 0: diverticula +/- colonic wall thickening; Ia: wall thickening with pericolic soft tissue change; Ib: as Ia plus pericolic or mesocolic abscess; II: as Ia plus walled-off pelvic or interloop abscess (distant, intra-abdominal or retroperitoneal); III: free air, free fluid and peritoneal thickening (generalized purulent, not walled off, non-feculent peritonitis); IV: generalized fecal peritonitis, CT same as III.
- Uncomplicated diverticulitis = stages 0 and Ia (no abscess); complicated = Ib, II, III, IV.
- Differential diagnosis in complicated cases: 1. cancer of colon; 2. colitis (ischemic, infectious); 3. IBD.

## Uncomplicated Diverticulitis
- 80-85% recover without surgery. 50-70% have no further inflammation; 30-50% have a 2nd attack: 50% of these come as the 2nd episode within the 1st year, 90% within 5 years; the 2nd attack is usually more severe than the first; in the past this pushed surgeons to advise operating at the 2nd episode; now there is more opposition because CT is used more and the number of recurrences is not an indication for surgery any longer.
- Remember: after resolution of the inflammation, wait 4-6 weeks and evaluate to rule out malignancy: colonoscopy preferred over BE.
- Mild case: mild LLQ pain, no guarding, perhaps slight rebound: OPD treatment: oral antibiotic, e.g. ciprofloxacin with metronidazole for 7-10 days, low-residue diet; improves within 2-3 days; then high-fiber diet, avoid constipation.
- Antibiotic debate in uncomplicated cases: RCTs of antibiotic vs none showed no difference in recovery, hospital stay or surgery; believers advise selective antibiotic for high-risk patients: immunosuppressed, chemo, steroid, diabetes, cirrhosis, or recent antibiotic. Those who do not give argue that it is only an inflammatory response without bacteria invading; they give anti-inflammatory 5-ASA (mesalamine) with results no better than no drug; probiotics for the intracolonic bacterial environment are not settled.
- Since no conclusion, the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS) strongly recommends antibiotics in diverticulitis until studies clearly show they are unnecessary.
- Admission: mild case: admit if elderly, women [as printed], low immunity, many comorbid diseases (DM, CKD, heart disease) or cannot eat: IV antibiotic.
- Severe case: worsening symptoms, high fever, severe LLQ pain, leukocytosis: admit, NPO, IV antibiotic, avoid morphine-type analgesics (raises intracolonic pressure); 85% improve within 3 days; if not improving suspect complication such as abscess: repeat CT.
- Indications for surgery (everyone agrees): 1. diffuse peritonitis; 2. free perforation; 3. obstruction; 4. stricture; 5. fistula; 6. immunocompromised (HIV, kidney disease, steroid, transplant), even at the 1st uncomplicated episode (the author agrees); 7. recurrence + persistent symptoms; 8. cannot be separated from cancer.
- Indications once cheered but now opposed: 1. after successful percutaneous abscess drainage (the author still recommends surgery); 2. bleeding (endoscopic treatment can stop it: see LGIB chapter).
- Uncomplicated 1st episode in patients under 50 years: once believed more severe in the young; research now shows this belief is probably not true, and the author agrees, since complication and recurrence rates in the young from large reports are no more severe than in the old.
- ASCRS concludes the number of attacks of uncomplicated diverticulitis is no longer an indication for elective surgery.
- Colonoscopy 4-6 weeks after resolution to exclude malignancy: cancer associated about 2.8% in uncomplicated; complicated 3-5%.

### Principles of Resection
- Elective resection for uncomplicated and complicated diverticulitis should be done 4-6 weeks after the attack, since infection and leak complications are fewer than with early surgery.
1. Open resection is still standard; laparoscopic reports are rising, even for uncomplicated cases; for later stages like Hinchey 3, 4 some use lap with hand assist; advantages less pain, home sooner.
2. Primary sigmoid resection then anastomosis to the rectum is best; no evidence yet on need for mobilizing the splenic flexure; the author favors mobilizing it so that the anastomosis is not tense.
3. Proximal margin: remove thick, shortened bowel, dull colour, signs of inflammation, mesenteric induration; cut to where the muscularis propria is not hypertrophic and the colon looks normal and soft; no need to remove all normal diverticula.
4. Dissect close to the colonic wall; preserve the IMA to reduce leak.
5. Distal resection margin: cut to the intraperitoneal or upper rectum, the part without tenia coli; the distal margin is very important: if not cut completely, recurrence.
6. Anastomosis: do not suture through a diverticulum (leak).
7. If cancer cannot be ruled out on colonoscopy: do an oncologic operation.
8. Dissection of the sigmoid is hard (dense adhesion): put in ureteral stents first to help identify the ureter; the stent does not prevent injury but helps tell where the ureter is and its injury, and planning repair.
9. Open or lap, either.
10. Remember the margins: proximal to healthy, soft, pliable colon; distal to the normal upper rectum.
- Recurrence after resection: 1. distal cut too short, sigmoid remaining; 2. wrong diagnosis (really IBS or IBD).

## Complicated Diverticulitis
- Frequency: abscess 40%, obstruction 30%, perforation 10-20%, fistula 4%.
- Signs of complication: obstruction, mass, fever over 39 C, WBC over 20,000, peritonitis with guarding.
- Since complicated diverticulitis has high recurrence and complications, after improving with drugs do colonoscopy then elective resection.
### Abscess (Hinchey 1 or 2)
- Suspect in uncomplicated diverticulitis not improving within 72 hours. Treatment depends on clinical condition, peritoneal contamination and infection:
  1. Small abscess: Cameron 12th cuts at size under 3-4 cm (others under 5 cm); IV antibiotic first without drainage; in the past elective surgery at 6 weeks after bowel prep; now consider case by case.
  2. Large abscess (over 5 cm): IV antibiotic plus percutaneous abscess drainage (PAD) guided by CT, watch clinical response and follow-up CT; afterwards consider elective surgery after 10-12 weeks (wait a bit longer, as it is rather sticky).
- Indication for urgent or emergency laparotomy: 1. inaccessible to PAD; 2. clinical deterioration; 3. free intraperitoneal air or peritonitis; 4. immunocompromised patients.
### Free perforation (Hinchey 3 and 4)
- With generalized peritonitis it is hard to separate purulent (Hinchey 3) from fecal (Hinchey 4) peritonitis; either way surgery to control the source of sepsis; in the past two-staged: first resect the perforated segment, close the lower end as Hartmann, bring out the upper end as an ostomy; if the patient survives, reconnect after 3-6 months; mortality 10-18%.
- Now the approach is changing:
  1. Hinchey 3: if no contraindication, resect and anastomose at once with a diverting ileostomy; except hemodynamic instability, severe diffuse peritonitis, bowel swollen or ischemic, anemia or malnutrition, immunocompromised: Hartmann then reverse is safer. Without sepsis and with a skilled laparoscopist: laparoscopic peritoneal lavage (reduce endotoxin and contamination with drain) then one stage later: mortality under 5%, but higher risk of intra-abdominal abscess than resection; current guidelines almost all do not support lavage unless the patient is unsuitable for colon resection.
  2. Hinchey 4: resect, do not anastomose: Hartmann is best.
- Suture closure of the hole alone is wrong (author's emphatic aside).
- Emergency surgery options:
  1. Sigmoid colectomy + end colostomy + Hartmann pouch: formerly the safest; for Hinchey 3, 4, large abscess, peritoneal soiling; but in a clinically stable patient with favorable anatomy, the later colostomy take-down carries significant morbidity and mortality, so it suits only unstable patients; disadvantage: 2 operations.
  2. Sigmoid colectomy +/- on-table lavage + primary colorectal anastomosis + proximal diversion (loop ileostomy): one-staged, avoiding Hartmann pouch; ileostomy closure is easier than colostomy; for patients in good condition and without contraindication to primary anastomosis.
  3. Proximal diversion + local drainage: for extremely high risk; disadvantage: leaves the diseased bowel in the abdomen.
  4. Laparoscopy + peritoneal lavage: wash the abdomen with NSS, no mobilization, no colon resection; some suggest that if a hole under 1.5 cm is found it can be sutured or clipped; JP drains beside the hole and in the pelvis with antibiotics; less invasive, quicker recovery; disadvantage: high risk of abscess or reintervention; use only in Hinchey 3 (non-feculent) without systemic toxicity; Hinchey 4 needs resection + Hartmann.
### Obstruction
- Mostly partial/incomplete, from colonic spasm or edema with ileus; with repeated inflammation a large bowel stricture may form, often pulling small bowel adherent as a package. Colonoscopy if no contraindication, to rule out cancer.
- Treatment: 1. NPO + NG suction + fluid resuscitation; partial obstruction improves; then wait 6 weeks, colonoscopy to exclude cancer, then prepare for elective resection; 2. complete obstruction: open, sigmoid colectomy + Hartmann is safest; anastomosis with loop ileostomy is a bit risky; a colonic stent followed by elective resection is debated because of a high failure rate (long diverticulitis stricture with much angulation).
### Fistula
- Between colon and adjacent organ; most common colovesical (65%), then coloenteric; colocutaneous is rare, usually after catheter drainage of an abscess; in women colovaginal.
- Most common cause of colovesical fistula: sigmoid diverticulitis.
- Symptoms: pneumaturia (classic), passing flatus/feces in urine (fecaluria), repeated UTI, LLQ pain, abdominal examination often unremarkable.
- Investigation: 1. CT with contrast is best: bladder wall and colon thickened, air in the bladder; 2. BE positive 9-20% (even if positive does not show where); 3. cystoscope positive 46%: do it to rule out bladder cancer, and colonoscopy to rule out colon cancer invading the bladder. Figures (skip).
- Differential: malignancy, Crohn, RT-induced fistula.
- Treatment of colovesical fistula: 1. preop bowel prep, ureteral stent as an option for elective surgery expected to have dense adhesion (stent does not reduce injury but helps identify and plan repair); 2. resect the affected colon segment + primary anastomosis, distal margin to the upper rectum; 3. close the bladder defect; 4. interpose omentum between colon and bladder; leave Foley 7 days; before removing, cystogram (some do not).
- Remember: coloenteric fistula may have no symptoms; investigation in the past favored cystoscope, now CT with contrast is the best.
### Bleeding
- In massive LGIB, diverticulosis is the most common cause (must be massive).
- Often in older men with hypertension and atherosclerosis, or on NSAID.
- Cause: chronic injury of the vasa recta (arterioles in the submucosa) which tear, found at the apex and neck of the diverticulum. Figure: vasa recta over diverticulum between taeniae coli (skip).
- It is arterial bleeding; luckily 70-80% stop by themselves; recurrence of bleeding about 25%; 10% of patients' bleeding does not stop and needs intervention or surgery.
- 75% of diverticula are on the left (sigmoid) but bleeding is found on the right up to 50%.
- Identifying the bleeding point and treatment: see GI bleeding chapter. BE used in the past is out of trend, should not be answered, for 2 reasons: 1. barium blocks the field if angiography is wanted afterwards; 2. BE sees diverticula but cannot say whether they are the bleeding source, and if many, which one bled.
- Treatment: 1. intensive monitoring; give blood; 2. if bleeding does not stop, endoscopic treatment first (adrenaline injection, coagulation; for a protruding vessel, endoclip; do not forget to inject India ink around the bleeding site to make surgery easier if needed); 3. first stop then rebleed (25%): conservative again for most; no conclusion on how many rebleeds merit surgery; some say over 2 rebleeds should have elective resection; 4. 10% severe bleeding not controllable: if the site is known before surgery, segmental resection; if not known, scope during surgery; if still not found, total or subtotal colectomy.

## Giant Colonic Diverticulum
- A diverticulum over 4 cm, usually on the antimesenteric side of the sigmoid; F = M, age over 60.
- Symptoms: most none; if present (60-80%) abdominal pain, because stool easily gets into the large diverticulum; may be found on CT incidentally. About 20% get diverticulitis and complications; cancer in a giant diverticulum is rare.
- Treatment even without symptoms: 1. no coexisting cancer: resect the sigmoid and anastomose (because of the size, diverticulectomy alone is not advised); 2. diverticulitis: resection + anastomosis; 3. complicated diverticulitis: resect but do not anastomose.

## Right-Sided Diverticulitis
- Common in Asia, especially Japan (USA left > right). Age about 40; solitary 81%, multiple 19%.
- Most have no symptoms; when present, as on the left: usually abdominal pain over 2 days, no migratory pain, site off to the side, little nausea/vomiting, WBC not very high.
- Diagnosis is hard (resembles appendicitis): only 20% are correctly diagnosed before surgery; at surgery 80%; symptoms differ from appendicitis: pain history over 2 days, little vomiting, tenderness lateral to McBurney's point.
- Treatment:
  1. Diagnosed before surgery (acute abdomen not typical for appendix; US or CT used). CT: inflamed diverticulum, thickened enhancing colonic wall, pericolic inflammation or abscess, fat stranding around the cecum, normal appendix.
     - 1.1 Not toxic, not complicated (no abscess/perforation): treat like left-sided uncomplicated diverticulitis; colonoscopy at 6 weeks to rule out cancer. Question: after recovery, is it necessary to take out the cecum to prevent later RLQ pain confusion? Answer: not necessary; recurrence risk of right-sided is much lower than left.
     - 1.2 Toxic, with complication (perforation, phlegmon, abscess): must open; if hole, rotten bowel, mass or abscess: right half colectomy then anastomose (differs from left, which often ends with Hartmann).
  2. Diagnosed during surgery (usually opened for appendectomy and found a normal appendix but cecal diverticulitis):
     - 2.1 A mass: do not poke (it ruptures, fecal spill), and it is hard to separate from cancer: right half colectomy and anastomose.
     - 2.2 Normal appendix and only the diverticulum inflamed: appendectomy + IV antibiotic without fussing with the diverticulitis (80% correct); some consider whether single (81%) or multiple (19%): single: do not touch the diverticulum, except some advise diverticulectomy; multiple: some surgeons advise right hemicolectomy or ileocecectomy to prevent recurrence; some say diverticulectomy only if the diverticulum is clearly seen and the surrounding cecum is not inflamed. Schwartz edition 11 advises diverticulectomy (author's aside).
     - 2.3 Complicated diverticulum (abscess, perforation): right hemicolectomy and anastomose.
- Notes: 1. ACS 2007 (ASCRS) has a diverticulitis management flow chart, worth reading; 2. the idea of operating on the 1st episode under age 50 is outdated; at the 2nd episode ("operate to keep"); 3. for bleeding see the LGIB chapter.

## Clostridium Difficile Colitis (CDI)
- Gram-positive spore-forming bacillus, part of normal gut flora that keeps other bacteria from colonizing; when other organisms are killed by antibiotic, C. difficile spores grow and release toxins A and B, causing mucosal inflammation, with pseudomembrane covering the mucosa (pseudomembranous colitis; this name is no longer preferred since not every case has pseudomembrane).
- Antibiotic-associated diarrhea, found 0.08-1.6%; clindamycin can cause it with a single dose; some patients had no antibiotic history, or had stopped antibiotic for 3-10 weeks.
- Risk factors: 1. age over 65; 2. broad-spectrum antibiotic (aminoglycoside rarely); 3. immunosuppressive drug or chemo; 4. severe illness (ICU, major surgery); 5. acid suppressors such as losec for long; 6. previous C. difficile infection: 12-24% get a 2nd attack within 2 months (thought to be a new strain); 7. NG tube (reason unknown).
- Remember: an asymptomatic carrier state in the gut does not add risk of disease.
- Symptoms: mild: abdominal cramping, non-bloody diarrhea under 10 times, no systemic symptoms; moderate to severe: profuse diarrhea, fever, nausea, bloating, abdominal pain, may have peritoneal signs, WBC up; fulminant CDI: severe abdominal distension, shock requiring vasopressors, ventilator.
- Diagnosis: 1. stool ELISA (enzyme immunoassay) for toxin A and B: positive 60-80%, sensitivity 48-96%, specificity 94-100%, result in about 1 hr but least sensitive; most used is stool PCR (DNA), sensitivity and specificity up to 95-100%; 2. endoscope (used much earlier): non-specific colitis, in severe colitis pseudomembranes: yellowish-white plaques scattered on the mucosa; now ELISA is easier and faster; colonoscopy's role is decompression and placing a long colonic tube for vancomycin irrigation; 3. plain abdomen: adynamic ileus with colon dilatation; 4. CT: colonic wall swollen and thick.
- Treatment:
  1. Stop the antibiotic in use, stop antidiarrheal drugs.
  2. Mild to moderate: oral metronidazole about 10-14 days; if not tolerated, oral vancomycin 125 mg for 10 days instead; response in 3-4 days; rectal vancomycin as a capsule is available now.
  3. Pregnant: no metronidazole in the 1st trimester; 2nd/3rd trimester can.
  4. Severe: fever, abdominal pain, dehydration, ileus, shock: admit, NPO, IV resuscitation; antibiotic IV flagyl or oral/enema vancomycin: remember vancomycin works only as oral form or enema; IV vancomycin does not work in this disease; no steroid (not IBD); in repeated cases may need a long course up to 1 month.
  5. Oral cholestyramine is reported to bind toxin but in practice not working; probiotics to add normal flora in repeated cases (not standard).
  6. Patient worsening, toxic, shock from toxic megacolon (TMC) or fulminant colitis: rush to ICU, closed observation with CBC, electrolyte, abdominal x-ray every 12 hr; if deteriorating consider surgery.
  7. Indications for surgery: 7.1 deterioration after full treatment, fulminant CDI; 7.2 overwhelming sepsis with MOF; 7.3 toxic megacolon; 7.4 peritonitis (perforation, rotten bowel).
  - Surgery: explore laparotomy + total colectomy with ileostomy, even when the serosa looks normal (since it involves mucosa); these operated cases have mortality about 60%, so talk with the family much; this is the treatment of choice. Newer, lighter option: laparoscopic loop ileostomy with intraoperative antegrade colonic lavage with polyethylene glycol (plus vancomycin): mortality 19%, and only 9% needed colectomy later; long-term results awaited.
- Notes: 1. flagyl dose 400-500 mg x 4 per day; 2. vancomycin dose 125-500 mg x 4 per day; 3. alternative names: pseudomembranous enterocolitis, antibiotic associated colitis.

## Amebic Colitis
- Caused by Entamoeba histolytica, protozoa from contaminated food (including from detox colon clinics, and men who use the anus wrongly); details: read with liver abscess in the liver chapter.
- In the colon, the organism can lodge anywhere from the terminal ileum to rectum, most often cecum and ascending colon; incubation about 7-21 days.
- Ameba has cytolytic enzyme causing mucosal and submucosal inflammation; classic ulcer on histology: narrow mouth, wide base, like a flask (flask-shaped ulcer, base in submucosa, small opening on the mucosal surface).
- Some have mucous bloody stool, frequent small-volume stools; severe: much abdominal pain, tenesmus, toxic like UC.
- Serology positive. Some form a mass called ameboma, which can occur at any part of the colon, making the physician confused: must separate from cancer or TB.
- Treatment: the same as amebic liver abscess: metronidazole (Flagyl) is the mainstay.

## TB Cecum
- Presenting with stenosis, symptoms like Crohn. Answer for treatment: 1. anti-TB drugs: the same as pulmonary TB in all drugs, doses and duration; 2. supportive care; 3. surgery only for complications, e.g. bleeding, obstruction. Detail in the Small Bowel chapter (same treatment).

## Gonococcal Proctitis
- From anal sex with Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection. Symptoms: anorectal pain, itching, mucous or bloody discharge; proctoscope: swollen red mucosa, maybe pus at the anal crypts.
- Send pus for culture or swab (do not take a swab from stool: you will get E. coli instead of GC). GC: gram-negative intracellular diplococci.
- Treatment: 1. abstain from sex; keep the anus for stool only; 2. ceftriaxone 250 mg IM single dose + doxycycline 100 mg orally BID for 10-14 days.

## Radiation Proctitis
- After RT for cervical cancer (anterior rectal wall gets much radiation); also RT for recurrent tumor in the pelvis; rectal risk is higher in RT for cancers occurring after chronic ulcerative colitis.
- Severity depends on dose, technique and volume: small bowel is most sensitive to radiation; rectum tolerates more, so if proctitis occurs it needs a high dose, especially over 55 Gy; techniques: brachytherapy and external beam.
- Acute injury (first 3 months after RT): mucosal swelling, inflammation, frequent small-volume stools, urgency, tenesmus, mucous bloody stool, abdominal cramps; often resolves on its own; some may have to stop RT first.
- Late injury: mostly within 3 years, peak 6-12 months: bleeding (fresh blood, intermittent, chronic); cause believed to be progressive obliterative vasculitis, ending with thrombosis, mucosal ischemia and necrosis (ulcer) then bleeding.
- Diagnosis: history, symptoms, scope: mucosa friable, bleeds easily, with telangiectasia (clusters of fine vessels in the mucosa). Biopsy not needed (fear of leakage); the endoscopic grade tells severity:

| Grade | Symptoms | Endoscopic finding |
|---|---|---|
| I | intermittent rectal bleeding | telangiectasia |
| II | persistent rectal bleeding | contact bleeding with or without ulceration |
| III | profuse bleeding with falling hemoglobin | fresh intraluminal blood, often with superficial ulcerations |
- Differential: think of bleeding from other causes, e.g. recurrent cancer or new CRC, even hemorrhoid.
- Treatment:
  1. As LGIB: small bleeding, tolerable: soft diet, avoid constipation, give blood if needed; topical steroid enema, sucralfate 2 g in 20 cc water enema morning/evening; hyperbaric oxygen therapy reported to help mucosal healing.
  2. Active or frequent bleeding: more invasive: chemical cauterization with a long cotton-tipped applicator soaked in 4% formalin touching the bleeding spot: stops bleeding 70-90%, but disadvantage: pain, tenesmus (rectal pain, spasm); endoscopic APC works better than the heater probe; hyperbaric oxygen to reduce tissue hypoxia also gives fair results.
  3. If still bleeding after 2: send for interventional procedures first.
  4. Surgery: the last option, for bleeding, ulcer, stricture, rectovaginal fistula, severe incontinence, frequent stools, pain not controlled conservatively: a colostomy (the most common) may not help if the mucosa keeps bleeding; if all else fails, proctectomy, escalating from light to heavy.
  5. Late case with stricture: try dilatation first; if not possible, consider surgery.

## Ischemic Colitis
- Classic: elderly, critically ill, many underlying diseases, with fever, abdominal pain and bloody diarrhea, fairly sudden. Cause known 85%, unknown 15%; 10-20% occur under age 40, often women on the pill, some unknown.
- Two groups: 1. reversible injury: only mucosa and submucosa, may bleed or ulcerate; usually heals without surgery; 2. irreversible injury: severe problems such as non-healing ulcer, stricture, fulminant pancolitis, gangrene or perforation; usually operated.
- Risk factors: post AAA surgery, evidence of atherosclerosis, CHF or arrhythmia or digitalis, low flow state (prolonged shock), vasculitis.
- Most common site: splenic flexure (watershed of SMA and IMA, vascular arcade far from the wall), next descending colon and sigmoid; if found at the right colon, it is bad.
- Symptoms: from left abdominal pain, tenesmus, mild cramping, to peritonitis from gangrene; severity depends on extent, duration of occlusion and collateral; vomiting and diarrhea may be present; lab usually normal.
- Severe pain with rigid abdomen and free air on film: operate. If time allows start with:
  1. Plain film: no specific sign but to rule out obstruction or perforation; BE was the older investigation: classic thumbprinting (75%, from submucosal hemorrhage or pericolic fat inflammation); not done much in acute pain now (fear of perforation); BE used to follow ischemic stricture. Figure: thumbprinting sign (skip).
  2. CT with oral contrast: preferred over BE; classic circumferential wall thickening 89%; if bowel is rotten, gas in the colonic wall or portal vein. Figure: bowel wall thickening (skip).
  3. Endoscopy: the gold standard, should be done within 48 hours; tells diagnosis and severity: colonic mucosa with patchy colour; but if abdomen rigid with free air or the bowel bleeds red-black, no scope: go to open and resect the rotten bowel without anastomosis.
     - If no contraindication, choose colonoscopy over flexible sigmoidoscope since it sees lesions proximal to the splenic flexure (some reports: right-sided ischemic colitis up to 30%); beware perforation: no force, little air; when swollen/oozing mucosa (or black) is seen, stop there, do not pass up.
     - Colonoscopic features separate it from IBD: ischemic colitis shows segmental involvement with abrupt transition between normal and abnormal areas, and the rectum is usually normal, unlike IBD where the rectum is usually involved.
     - Remember: mesenteric angiogram has no role in ischemic colitis.
- ACG clinical guideline 2015 advises CT with early colonoscopy.
- Treatment:
  1. Depends on degree of ischemia: rigid abdomen, hemodynamic instability, free air or portal vein gas or air in colonic wall on film/CT, black mucosa on scope: ominous, bowel rotten: rush to OR, resect the rotten bowel until normal, assess with indocyanine green or fluorescein viability; anastomose or not, if not safe close as Hartmann and proximal diversion.
  2. Mild cases, soft abdomen, hemodynamically stable, CT with oral and IV contrast showing free fluid or wall edema, scope showing mild ischemia, segmental edema, petechial hemorrhage, ulceration: observe, bowel rest, IV fluid, IV antibiotic with serial examinations; if worsening or distended abdomen, explore. If improving (usually in 24-48 hr): oral diet, discharge, full resolution usually 1-2 weeks; repeat colonoscopy (or BE) in 2-6 weeks. Superficial ischemia (mucosa/submucosa) sloughs and regenerates without sequelae; deep to muscle may leave stricture, so follow up.
- Summary, indications for surgery: 1. sepsis, continued diarrhea, bleeding not responding to conservative treatment; 2. peritonitis, usually from perforation; 3. colonoscope shows mucosal gangrene; 4. colonic stricture; 5. protein-losing colopathy lasting over 2-3 weeks.
- Ischemic colitis from angiographic embolization with superselective catheterization lowers bowel ischemia from 20% to 3-7%; within the first 24-36 hr maybe fever 38 C, WBC usually normal, hemoculture negative: observation is enough; but if sepsis, open and resect the black bowel without anastomosis.
- Figure (algorithm, suspected ischemic colitis): peritonitis: emergent operation. Peritonitis absent: CT (pneumoperitoneum, portovenous air or significant pneumatosis: emergent operation; segmental colitis: proceed) and colonoscopy/sigmoidoscopy (single-stripe sign, pallor, petechial bleeding, superficial ulcerations) -> NPO, resuscitation, close observation, possible NGT, possible antibiotics -> improvement: resume diet, discontinue antibiotics, repeat endoscopy in 6 weeks (continued colitis or stricture: operation); no improvement or deterioration: operation.

## Intestinal Endometriosis
- Ectopic endometrial tissue implanted on the serosal surface of bowel, in the pelvic space near genital organs; bleeding in the cyst follows the ovarian cycle; the cyst is stretched by bleeding, causing pain and pelvic adhesion.
- Triad of endometriosis: 1. dysmenorrhea; 2. dyspareunia; 3. infertility.
- Suspect intestinal endometriosis: 1. triad in a premenopausal woman; 2. GI complaints related to menstruation; most common site rectum: rectal and pelvic pain, constipation, painful defecation, obstruction from extrinsic compression; rectal bleeding is rare.
- No pathognomonic signs and symptoms, so investigate every suspect.
- Investigation: 1. laparoscopy: gold standard (the cyst is on the serosa while the mucosa looks normal, so colonoscopy or BE do not help, except to exclude cancer); 2. US, CT, MRI: no typical sign.
- Management: no obstructive symptom: danazole (the teacher notes this works better than going to surgery, "like a joke"); the author notes hormonal drug use is controversial; most advise drugs first since adhesions are many and surgery is difficult.
- Indications for surgery: 1. obstructive symptom even partial; 2. medical failure; 3. cancer cannot be excluded.
- Surgery: 1. resection of the diseased bowel, anastomose at once; 2. TAH to remove the source of endometrium; 3. BSO to ablate the normal cyclic hormone from the ovary that triggers symptoms.
- Premenopause: if she still wants children: GI resection only, without TAH + BSO, symptoms controlled by danazole; if no more children wanted: remove uterus, tubes, ovaries.
- Postmenopause (esp. age over 40 or menopausal): think of malignant transformation of intestinal endometriosis in future; if rectal bleeding or a mass appears, beware cancer. In postmenopausal intestinal endometriosis, en bloc bowel resection is usual, accepting it is difficult and adherent; refer.

## Colorectal Polyp
- Definition: abnormal elevation or protrusion of mucosal surface into the bowel lumen; found anywhere in the GI tract, most in the colon, 2/3 at the rectosigmoid.
- Paris classification (endoscopic shape): 1. polypoid subtype: pedunculated (large head, narrow base) and sessile (small head, broad base); 2. non-polypoid subtype: flat or ulcerative lesion. Figure (skip).
- When a polyp is found, answer: 1. benign or malignant; 2. if malignant, in situ or invasive; 3. choice of treatment: endoscopic, open radical surgery or lap.
- Natural progression: adenoma-carcinoma sequence; adenoma is the precursor of CRC; glands become abnormal and turn to cancer in about 5-10 years, so colonoscopic polypectomy is the most important factor lowering CRC incidence. Figure (genetic steps): normal epithelium (APC) -> dysplastic aberrant crypt foci -> early adenoma (K-RAS) -> intermediate adenoma (DCC) -> late adenoma (p53) -> carcinoma -> metastasis; FAP: accelerated tumor initiation (germline APC), nearly 100% CRC risk; HNPCC: accelerated progression (mismatch repair mutation, 1-3 years), 70-80% lifetime risk.
- Polyp found by sigmoidoscope: synchronous lesion 30-50%, so always do colonoscopy.
- Polyps needing special attention: 1. more than 3; 2. size over 1 cm; 3. family history of CRC; 4. polypectomy specimen read as high grade dysplasia.
- When there are too many polyps to biopsy each, use magnifying chromoendoscopy: spray dye on the suspicious area, then look at the pit pattern, which separates non-neoplastic from neoplastic polyp; only the suspicious polyps need polypectomy, reducing unnecessary polypectomy. Problem: chromoendoscopy mainly used in Japan and Korea; early scopes without magnify mode have accuracy about 70%.
- In 2011 endoscopists from Japan, Europe and America made a standard: the NBI (narrow band imaging) International Colorectal Endoscopic (NICE) classification, usable without magnification, using 3 features: color, vascular pattern, surface pattern:

| | Type 1 | Type 2 | Type 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Color | same or lighter than background | browner relative to background (verify color arises from vessels) | brown to dark brown relative to background; sometimes patchy whiter areas |
| Vessels | none, or isolated lacy vessels coursing across the lesion | brown vessels surrounding white structures | areas of disrupted or missing vessels |
| Surface pattern | dark or white spots of uniform size, or homogeneous absence of pattern | oval, tubular or branched white structures surrounded by brown vessels | amorphous or absent surface pattern |
| Most likely pathology | hyperplastic | adenoma (Vienna types 3, 4 and superficial 5; may include high-grade dysplasia or superficial submucosal carcinoma) | deep submucosal invasive cancer |
- Type 1: follow up; type 2: polypectomy / EMR / ESD; type 3: surgical operation.
- Sensitivity and specificity of NICE above 95%, accuracy up to 89%.
- Sato 2015 says NICE still has problems deciding treatment in type 2, so the Japanese subtype it; fellows should know this in detail; residents only the above.
- Figure (three-step strategy, Y Sano 2015, Kobe): step 1: NBI colonoscopy; step 2: NBI as optical chromoendoscopy (capillary pattern CP): CP type I: follow-up (NICE1); CP type II, IIIA without demarcation area: endoscopic resection (NICE2); CP IIIA with demarcation area, IIIB (NICE3): surface pattern present: go to 3rd step; absent: surgery highly recommended; step 3 (5% of cases): chromoendoscopy (pit pattern): V1 non-invasive: endoscopic resection; V1 invasive + VN: surgery; 95% need no third step.
### Histologic typing
1. Non-neoplastic polyp: 1.1 hyperplastic polyp: hypermaturation/metaplastic lesion from slow-maturing mucosal cell proliferation; 1.2 hamartomatous polyp: abnormal mixture of normal tissue, gland and connective tissue in abnormal ratio; 1.3 inflammatory polyp: does not become cancer; cancer found elsewhere than in inflammatory polyp.
2. Neoplastic polyp: adenomatous polyp (more to write since it is examined).
### Risk of cancer in adenomatous polyp
1. Type: tubular (glandular element over 75%): risk 5%; tubulovillous (glandular and villous 50% each): risk 20%; villous (finger-like projections over 75%): risk 40%. Figure (villous photos, skip).
2. Dysplasia: if severe (high grade) or carcinoma in situ, risk is high.
3. Size: under 0.5 cm: cancer risk 0%; 0.6-1.5 cm: 2%; 1.6-2.5 cm: 20%; 2.6-3.5 cm: 40%; over 3.5 cm: 75%.
4. Timing (how long the polyp has been present): over 5 years: risk 2.5%; over 10 years: 8%; over 20 years: 20%.
5. Patient age: polyp in a young person carries a high cancer risk [as printed].
- Adenomatous polyp over 2 cm has a high chance of being cancer, some reports up to 53%, especially if villous.
- Symptoms: asymptomatic (found incidentally at scope, e.g. CRC screening); symptomatic: bleeding, diarrhea, prolapse, anemia, intermittent pain from intussusception.
- When a polyp is found consider colonoscopic polypectomy as both treatment and cancer screening; open colon resection or colotomy to cut out the polyp should be used only when colonoscopic polypectomy cannot be done.
- Malignant polyp: a polyp whose cancer cells infiltrate through the muscularis mucosa into the submucosa (pT1), an important landmark; 95% of malignant polyps lie below the splenic flexure.
- pTis (in situ) has several names (intramucosal carcinoma, intraepithelial carcinoma): dysplastic cell invasion not beyond the muscularis mucosa; considered TNM stage 0, and the Vienna group classifies it as non-invasive high grade dysplasia; NCCN does not count it as a malignant polyp until pT1.
- Endoscopic features suggesting cancer: dark colour, irregular surface, dirty look, ulceration, multilobulated appearance, hard consistency, contact bleeding; at polypectomy it needs more coagulation than usual because of oozing.
- A sessile polyp, grade 1 or 2 (well or moderately differentiated), without lymphovascular invasion can be treated by polypectomy if a negative margin can be achieved.
### Polypectomy technique
1. Colonoscopic polypectomy; 2. transanal excision; 3. transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM); 4. open surgery.
### High-risk malignant polyp
- After polypectomy by any technique, check the pathology report: if a high-risk polyp and a good-risk patient, extra resection or an oncologic resection may be needed.
- High-risk criteria: 1. positive margin of resection (margin under 1 mm counts as positive); 2. pathologist reports undetermined margin (cannot assess, especially sessile polyps cut piecemeal); 3. lymphovascular or perineural invasion; 4. grade 3 or 4 histology (poorly differentiated or undifferentiated, including tumor budding); 5. mucin-producing carcinoma or signet ring cell; 6. lower rectal lesion (more positive nodes than upper/mid rectum); 7. Haggitt level 4 or Kudo Sm3.
- Haggitt level (suits pedunculated polyp): level 0: intramucosal or in situ; levels 1, 2, 3: cancer invades the submucosa (or muscularis mucosae): level 1 head, level 2 neck, level 3 stalk; level 4: at the base of the bowel wall. Figure: pedunculated and sessile adenoma with levels (skip).
- Kudo or Kikuchi level (suits sessile polyp): divides the submucosa (Sm) into 3 levels from upper to lower; deeper means more chance of node involvement: Sm1 upper third (Kudo further splits by width into 1a, 1b, 1c, a least wide to c widest); Sm2 middle third; Sm3 lower third.
### Risk of lymph node metastasis
1. Haggitt: levels 1, 2, 3: under 1%; level 4: 27%.
2. Kikuchi: Sm1 1% (1a and 1b 0%, 1c 2.4%); Sm2 6%; Sm3 25%. The Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR) now measures invasion depth in the submucosa in microns: under 1,000 micron: node positive under 2%; over 1,000 micron: node positive 14%.
3. Grade 3: poorly differentiated or mucin producing: 50%.
4. Lower third of rectum: 10-34%.
- Haggitt level 4 and Kikuchi Sm3 have high node-positive chance, so treat with radical surgery.

## Polypectomy
- Endoscopic polypectomy needs experience; use this path for removing polyp if possible; prepare clean bowel and correct coagulopathy.
- Preoperative work-up: 1. examination: DRE with rigid proctoscope for distance from anal verge and circumference; 2. complete colonoscopy to rule out synchronous lesion; 3. EUS to assess depth of invasion; 4. pelvic MRI for lymph node metastasis; 5. CT chest/abdomen to rule out metastasis.
- Colonoscopic polypectomy has 4 ways: 1. hot biopsy forceps polypectomy (forceps cuts); 2. snare wire loop polypectomy (snare placed over polyp, closed tightly, polyp removed); 3. piecemeal polypectomy; 4. transanal excision; 5. TEM, EMR or ESD (listed).
- Principles of endoscopic polypectomy: 1. complete, free margin over 1 mm; 2. single piece, not piecemeal or fragments (margin hard to see; mark or tattoo the site); 3. avoid complications (bleeding, perforation); 4. if EMR leaves residual, re-polypectomy after 3 weeks, do not rush (bowel may tear/perforate); 5. a lesion in the lower rectum can use transanal excision if within 8 cm of the anal verge; 6. contraindications like colonoscopy (e.g. acute MI); if a polyp is near cancer and needs resection anyway, do open surgery with the cancer; 7. after polypectomy, before ending, tattoo the polyp site with India ink or clip to mark it, because if oncologic resection (open or laparoscopic) is needed the surgeon can find the site; 8. if clip is used, send an abdominal film postop to know where the clip is; 9. tattoo: 2 choices: methylene blue (fades quickly, little irritation) or India ink (stays long; if injected through the wall, ink strains the bowel and spreads; irritation).
- After endoscopic polypectomy of invasive cancer (pedunculated or sessile): review pathology: if cut as complete/adequate, observe and follow up.
- Adequate endoscopic polypectomy: 1. single specimen; 2. complete removal with favorable histology and clear margin: 2.1 clear margin over 2 mm; 2.2 grade 1 or 2 (well or moderately differentiated); 2.3 no lymphovascular invasion; 2.4 Haggitt 0 to 3 or Kudo 1 to 2.
- Sessile polyp with invasive cancer: previously results were poor (incomplete, recurrence); after the NCCN V.1 2017 meeting: as long as adequate endoscopic polypectomy can be done, even for a sessile polyp, the choice between EMR and ESD is fine without needing radical surgery.
- Endoscopic polypectomy guidance: 1. polyp larger than 1/3 circumference or over 2 haustral folds: remove through the scope is hard; 2. sessile polyp: inject under the polyp to lift it from the submucosa before cutting; if the lift fails (does not rise), do not cut through the scope since invasion into the submucosa is likely.
- Invasive cancer removed as a fragmented specimen (not single) with unfavorable histology or cannot assess margin: recurrence risk about 30%; send for colectomy with en bloc removal of regional lymph nodes.
- After polypectomy the pathology: benign adenomatous polyp: screening guideline for moderate risk; hyperplastic polyp: screen as the general population (average risk).
### Postpolypectomy syndrome
- Closed perforation from full-thickness heat damage to the bowel after polypectomy of a large sessile polyp; the hole is small and bowel was prepped, so symptoms are only peritonism: fever about 24 hr with localized abdominal pain.
- Treatment: 1. no signs of generalized peritonitis and no free air: conservative: NPO, bed rest, IV antibiotic; usually better in 24-48 hr; 2. free perforation with rigid abdomen: upright abdominal film at once; free air: operate, resect the bowel without anastomosis, bring out colostomy.
### Follow-up colonoscopy
- After adequate endoscopic treatment, follow up in 3-6 months for colonoscopy, which applies to: 1. no previous colonoscopy; 2. sessile lesion.
- If colonoscopy was done before polypectomy and the result was benign or cancer that is not high-risk, especially pedunculated polyp: colonoscopy in 1 year; if normal again, every 3-5 years.
- Sessile or malignant polyp even with adequate endoscopic treatment: more frequent than pedunculated: colonoscopy every 3-6 months in the first 2 years; if normal, every 3-5 years.
- Those who needed oncologic resection after polypectomy: colonoscopy 1 year after surgery.
### Transanal technique
- Recommended by the author's teacher (Santhat): can be an OPD case, no need for anesthesia or spinal block; may give light sedation.
- Stop anticoagulants, ASA, NSAID 1 week before; PT, PTT should be normal or near normal.
- Local excision: transanal approach, 1-3 cm margin, full thickness, oriented for pathology (figure).
- Beware of flatus in the patient: besides smelling, it contains hydrogen and methane, which raises fear of explosion with cautery; in truth it is rare (the teacher advises suction of gas before cautery each time); the author's point on residents' farting is a joke (not clinical).
- No NPO needed after surgery; allow eating, except after large masses where a liquid diet 1-2 days.
- Transanal excision and barium enema: 1. before surgery 1 day is fine, with enema to wash out barium; 2. after surgery do BE only after 3-4 weeks, otherwise leakage.
### Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)
- Snare EMR suits lesions not larger than 2 cm. Beware in the colon: the wall is thinner than esophagus/stomach, so perforation is easy.
### Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD)
- Suits lesions larger than 2 cm; cut en bloc with a special knife including the submucosal layer.
### Transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEMS)
- Full-thickness excision with an operating rectoscope. EMR, ESD, TEMS: see local treatment of rectal cancer.
### Open surgery
- Colotomy polypectomy; open surgery suits: 1. large polyp (over 1/3 circumference or over 2 haustral folds); 2. residual lesion hard to cut completely; 3. associated disease needing removal of that colon (UC, complicated diverticulitis, ischemic stricture); 4. polyp in polyposis syndrome, which usually ends with radical surgery.
### Oncologic resection
1. Invasive cancer with: 1.1 margin under 2 mm or pathologist reads positive margin; 1.2 Haggitt 4, Kudo 3; 1.3 grade 3 histology (poorly differentiated or undifferentiated); 1.4 lymphovascular invasion; 1.5 mucin production or signet ring. 2. Lower 1/3 rectal cancer. 3. Good risk. 4. Patient agrees.
- Again for positive margin, NCCN 2017 gives no clear rule whether to count tumor within under 1 mm from the transected margin or under 2 mm; the author thinks use margin under 1 mm.

## Colorectal Cancer (CRC)
- Sporadic CRC 80%; hereditary CRC (genetic syndromes) 5-10%; familial CRC 15-20% (those not meeting hereditary criteria but clustering in a family).
### Genetic basis of familial CRC
- Genes involved (residents should remember):
  1. Oncogene: derived from proto-oncogene (normal human gene); stimulates excessive cell division at all times; for CRC the key one is the K-RAS gene.
  2. Tumor suppressor gene: inhibits cell growth and division at stages of the cell cycle (check points): APC, DCC, p53.
  3. DNA repair gene: controls the protein that repairs DNA; if mutated, replication errors occur with oncogene and tumor suppressor gene leading to cancer.
- Gene decoys not related to CRC: BRCA and N-myc.
- Genetic markers now help separate sporadic from inherited tumors and decide targeted therapy: K-ras mutated tumors do not respond to cetuximab.
- Do not confuse CRC in hereditary breast cancer: better survival because symptoms come early and diagnosis is usually early stage.
### Hereditary colon cancer syndrome
- Found 1% of colorectal cancer; all arise from mutation of tumor suppressor gene and DNA repair gene.
- Divided into 2 groups: [continues on next page].
1. Non-polyposis syndrome: HNPCC or Lynch syndrome (3-8%): must know; Lynch-like syndrome and familial CRC type X: leave to the proctologists.
2. Polyposis syndrome: 2.1 adenomatous polyp, e.g. FAP (1%); 2.2 non-adenomatous polyp, e.g. hamartoma, juvenile, serrated polyposis (rare).
- Residents must know the 2 syndromes that are asked: FAP and HNPCC.

| Syndrome | Inheritance | Clinical | Gene |
|---|---|---|---|
| FAP: polyps over 100 (if under 100: attenuated FAP) | AD | 1. adenomatous polyp; 2. CRC; 3. congenital hypertrophy of retinal pigment | APC |
| HNPCC: polyps over 10 [as printed] | AD | 1. adenomatous polyp; 2. cancer of GI, pancreas, KUB and endometrium | MLH1 |
- Genetic screening criteria: 1. CRC in a person under 40; 2. family history of cancer in HNPCC type 2; 3. polyps over 10 in the same person; 4. history of hereditary cancer syndrome.
- Genetic testing: DNA extracted from WBC, or molecular-level testing.
- Colorectal cancer pathways: 4 pathways believed: 1. classic adenoma-carcinoma sequence from APC mutation with chromosome instability (most common); 2. mismatch repair gene mutation in HNPCC; 3. chromosome instability with K-ras mutation and p53 error in ulcerative colitis; 4. DNA switch-off of mismatch repair gene in the progression of serrated adenoma to cancer.
### Family relative criteria
- First degree relative: parents, siblings (same parents), children. Second degree: grandparents, uncles, aunts. Third degree: great-grandparents/cousins generation.

## Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP)
- If polyps are not treated in FAP, cancer is 100%; AD from APC mutation at the long arm of chromosome 5. Mild FAP: polyps over 100; severe FAP: polyps over 1,000; found along the whole colon, left > right.
- Natural course: 1. age adenoma found 16-25 years (polyps are not present at birth); 2. symptoms at about 33 years but adenoma diagnosed about 36 years; 3. cancer diagnosed at 39 years and death at about 42 years; severe FAP (over 1,000 polyps) becomes cancer faster than usual.
- Knowing this, diagnose early and treat early. Polyps in FAP are not monitored for dysplasia since even benign ones become cancer 100%: when polyps are found in FAP, total proctocolectomy.
- After total proctocolectomy, most common cause of death in FAP is periampullary tumor, so EGD surveillance is necessary in every case.
### Clinical manifestations
1. Colon: asymptomatic to abdominal pain, bleeding, diarrhea.
2. Extracolonic: endoderm: polyps in stomach, duodenum, small bowel; mesoderm: desmoids, osteoma; ectoderm: eye lesion (CHRPE), epidermal cyst, brain neoplasm.
- CHRPE (congenital hypertrophy of retinal pigmented epithelium): discrete flat pigmented brown lesion of the retina; in FAP patients CHRPE is found 70-80%, multiple and bilateral. Normal people may have isolated unilateral lesion; CHRPE can be used for screening family members: if present, the FAP gene is in that person.
- FAP with desmoids: extra-abdominal desmoid: wide local resection with 1 cm margin; intra-abdominal/retroperitoneal desmoid: choose NSAID (sulindac, celecoxib), estrogen antagonist (tamoxifen, raloxifene); surgery should be non-resective procedures such as bypass or diversion; details in the soft tissue sarcoma chapter.
- FAP with extracolonic manifestation have syndrome names (treatment same as FAP):
  1. Gardner syndrome: FAP, desmoid tumor, epidermal cyst, mandibular osteoma or exostosis, CHRPE. Figure: mandibular osteoma (skip).
  2. Turcot syndrome: adenomatous polyp, CNS tumor (medulloblastoma or glioblastoma), hepatoblastoma, cancer of thyroid or adrenal.
### Diagnosis
1. Family history of CRC in a young person with symptoms such as bleeding.
2. Colonoscopy finds more than 100 polyps, pedunculated or sessile.
3. Genetic testing: young patient with polyps over 100: test family members for APC mutation.
- Differential: congenital: PJ syndrome, HNPCC, Cowden, Turcot; acquired: Cronkhite-Canada syndrome.
### Management
- Surgical: timing of prophylactic colectomy: before cancer arises, found rarely in those under 20 years (Shackelford 2019 p.1965):
  1. Immediate: as soon as possible, if cancer or complication of colonoscopy.
  2. Soon: within 3 months, if profuse polyposis (over 1,000), multiple large polyps (over 1 cm), high grade dysplasia in an adenoma.
  3. Sometime: observe year by year if mild polyposis (100-1,000), no symptoms, or social problem, acceptance by patient and family.
  4. Defer: avoid as long as possible if high risk of morbidity or high risk of desmoid tumor.
- Choice of operation:
  1. Total proctocolectomy with ileal pouch anal anastomosis (IPAA): the best procedure (answer this as the one best); after IPAA see the patient every 6-12 months because cancer can still arise at the anus. Not suitable for patients with high desmoid risk or surgeons with little experience. Figure: J-pouch (skip).
  2. Total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis (IRA): for mild FAP where rectal polyps may regress: colon polyps under 1,000 and rectal polyps under 20; the person scoping polyps must be very patient; after surgery scope every 6-12 months; about 6% of IRA patients need rectum removed later; advantage: shorter operation, better quality of life; disadvantage: still risk of cancer in retained rectum, surveillance of the rectum lifelong.
  3. Total colectomy with mucosal resection of the rectum: like 2 but reduces cancer risk in the retained rectum by mucosal resection; seldom done (since item 1 is easier... much work).
  4. Total proctocolectomy with end ileostomy or continent ileostomy: poor quality of life but sometimes needed: patient with existing incontinence, low rectal cancer where IPAA is not possible, or FAP with mesenteric desmoid so the ileum cannot be pulled down to the anus.
- Medical management:
  1. NSAID (sulindac, celecoxib/clinoril): reduces number and size of rectal polyps; sulindac alone does not help duodenal polyps (must combine with erlotinib, an EGFR inhibitor); sulindac reduces RAS mutation and p53 expression, used as chemoprevention (FDA approved, 300 mg/day for 4 months), though later reports say it does not work much because the dose needed is high and patients cannot tolerate; celecoxib 800 mg/day for 6 months reduces polyps; beware heart problems; remember NSAID does not change the pathogenesis of the disease.
  2. Tamoxifen: used in FAP with desmoids: 2 years, mass regresses 50%.
  3. Imatinib: recent reports say fairly good results.
- Endoscopic management: suited to examining the gastroduodenal area to prove polyps; duodenal polyposis must be known by residents (examined); gastric polyps are usually fundic gland polyps (see stomach chapter).

## FAP with Duodenal Polyp
- FAP patients have adenomatous polyps at the duodenum about 50% (rank 2 after colon), so EGD is advised from age 20-25.
- After total proctocolectomy with IPAA, do surveillance upper endoscopy right after surgery; if duodenal polyp is found, assess by Spigelman grading (points) to plan treatment and follow-up.
- 80% are Spigelman grade 1, 2; only 10% grade 4.

| Polyp size (mm) | 1-4 | 5-10 | over 10 |

- Malignant transformation of duodenal polyp in FAP is only 3-12% in lifetime, not 100% like colon/rectum polyps, so the approach is softer: no prophylactic PD or PPPD. Figure: Spigelman IV duodenal polyposis (skip).
- Indication for surgery (from Spigelman): villous polyp, severe dysplasia, rapid growth, size over 10 mm, and Spigelman grade 4.
- PPPD vs classic PD: some choose PPPD because after total proctocolectomy a PD adds severe diarrhea and poor QoL; but most choose classic PD since duodenal polyps in FAP are multiple, sessile, often right at the ampulla of Vater, so PPPD recurs (Schwartz 2019 p.1244). - If polyps are also found in the stomach and are high-risk adenomatous polyps: advised total gastrectomy + pancreaticoduodenectomy (very heavy surgery). Figure (skip).

## FAP with Gastric Polyp
- FAP patients have fundic gland polyps in the stomach; gastric adenoma found 10%.
- Because the chance of turning to cancer is small, just cut/clip polyps via the scope; radical surgery only when high grade dysplasia or malignancy is found.

## Attenuated FAP (AFAP)
- A variant of classic FAP but much less severe; AD, APC mutation 60%; polyps over 20 but under 100, flat, chance of becoming cancer under 70%. Figure: milder form: fewer polyps generally under 100, later onset, lower cumulative lifetime risk; polyps often right-sided.
- Location: usually proximal to the splenic flexure, especially cecum to hepatic flexure (Rt > Lt), which helps separate AFAP from FAP.
- Age polyps found about 44; age cancer starts 65 [as printed]; right-sided more than left; severity less than classic FAP.
- Few extracolonic and intestinal manifestations (gastric adenoma, desmoid, CHRPE).
- Management: chance of turning to cancer nearly 100% like FAP, but at older age (over 50) and the rectum is often free; approach depends on polyp number: 1. few adenomas: colonoscopic polypectomy + follow-up; 2. multiple polyps: total colectomy + IRA; 3. rectal polyp: snare it out.

## Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colorectal Cancer (HNPCC)
- Found less often than FAP but still rare; AD with genetic defect of DNA mismatch repair gene (MMR), most typically hMSH2, so DNA replication errors in the cell cycle are not corrected, leading to cancer; DNA can be extracted from WBC now.
- Differences from FAP: fewer polyps; cancer arises later (HNPCC about 40-45 years); only 70-80% of HNPCC become cancer, while FAP polyps become cancer 100%; cancer is right > left (70% proximal to the splenic flexure); overall prognosis of HNPCC is better. Figure: colonoscopic photo (skip).
- HNPCC with CRC is not worse than sporadic CRC of the same stage.
- Suspect in CRC in a young person (about 45), family history of CRC, right-sided mass, meeting Amsterdam and Bethesda criteria.
- Amsterdam criteria (now Amsterdam II): 1. relatives (blood) with CRC at least 3 persons; 2. of these at least 2 must be first degree relatives (parents, children, siblings of the same generation); 3. at least 1 must have CRC before age 50; 4. CRC in at least 2 generations; 5. FAP can be excluded.
- To make criteria more detailed, including extracolonic cancer (ovary, endometrium, stomach), the Bethesda criteria were created; if meeting both, order genetic testing.
- Mnemonic for Amsterdam criteria: "3, 2, 1": at least 3 first degree relatives, over 2 generations, and 1 case of CRC under 50.
- Lynch syndrome (reported by Henry Lynch, 1966): HNPCC with extracolonic lesions, 4 types:
  - Lynch type 1: HNPCC involving the colon only but with metachronous colon cancer up to 40%.
  - Lynch type 2: Lynch 1 with GI cancers (stomach, small bowel, bile duct, pancreas) and KUB (kidney, ureter), also Gyne (ovary, endometrium), breast, larynx.
  - Lynch type 3 (Muir-Torre syndrome): Lynch 1 and 2 with skin lesions (benign: sebaceous cyst, keratoacanthoma; or malignant).
  - Lynch type 4 (Turcot syndrome): Lynch type 1 or 2 with glioblastomas.
- Among extracolonic lesions in HNPCC, endometrial cancer is most common, then ovary.
- Table (estimated lifetime risk for cancer in mismatch repair deficiency, HNPCC): colorectal 30-80%; endometrial 25-70%; ovarian 3-13%; stomach 2-13%; urinary tract 1-12%; small bowel 4-7%; brain tumors 1-4%; biliary tract 2%.
- Management: since HNPCC does not become cancer 100% like FAP, whether to do prophylactic colectomy is controversial; cancer tends to appear about age 45; some advise follow-up only, but the author's review: most advise surgery.
- Operation: although polyps are right > left, synchronous or metachronous polyps on the left up to 40%, so right or extended right hemicolectomy is inadequate treatment; rectal involvement is small (unlike FAP), so the procedure of choice: total colectomy with IRA, then scope the remaining large bowel every 1 year.
- In women who have had enough children, or are menopausal or a "tom": add TAH with BSO.
- Siblings who are carriers: colonoscopy every 1 year starting at age 25, or 10 years before the age of the family member who got cancer.
  1. Scope finds a polyp that is cancer: total colectomy with IRA.
  2. Scope finds a polyp that is not yet cancer: polypectomy only, or do total colectomy right away: debated, since HNPCC polyps have 70% chance of becoming cancer and take about 3 years; most still advise total rather than polypectomy only.
- Chemoprevention: studies give ASA 600 mg/day to prevent CRC; still in phase 3 trial.

## Screening CRC for High Risk
- FAP: family members: genetic counseling by APC testing; if APC mutation +ve, do colonoscopy: 1. initial at age 10-12 then yearly until age 24; 2. age 24-34: every 2 years; 3. age 34-44: every 3 years; 4. after 44: every 3-5 years for life; 5. whenever a polyp is found, no matter its histology or dysplasia, since future CRC is 100%: do prophylactic colectomy; 6. upper endoscopy begins at age 25 or when colorectal polyps are first found.
- AFAP: like FAP but initial colonoscopy begins at age 20.
- HNPCC: family member found to have MMR mutation: 1. initial colonoscopy at age 20-25 or 10 years before the youngest family member with cancer, then every 1-3 years; 2. gastroduodenoscopy start at 30-35 every 3-5 years; 3. in women, to watch for uterine and ovarian cancer each year from age 25-35: transvaginal uterine and ovary US, endometrial aspiration, CA 125, earlier mammogram.
- Ulcerative colitis (bonus): start 8-10 years after onset; colonoscopy every 1-2 years with random multiple biopsy.
- Table (lifetime cancer risk and screening): FAP: CRC 100%: colonoscopy annually beginning age 10-12; duodenal or periampullary cancer 5-10%: upper GI endoscopy every 1-3 yr beginning age 20-25; pancreatic 2%: possible periodic abdominal US; thyroid 2%: annual thyroid examination; gastric under 1%: upper GI endoscopy as for duodenal; CNS under 1%: annual physical examination. HNPCC: CRC 80%: colonoscopy every 2 yr beginning age 20, annually after 40 or 10 yr younger than earliest case in family; endometrial 40-60%: pelvic examination, transvaginal US, endometrial aspirate every 1-2 yr beginning age 25-35; upper urinary tract 4-10%: US and urinalysis every 1-2 yr from age 30-35; gallbladder and biliary 2-18%, CNS under 5%, small bowel under 5%: no recommendation.
- Residents should know Peutz-Jeghers syndrome and familial juvenile polyposis; Cowden: not necessary. Hamartomatous polyposis syndromes: juvenile polyposis, Peutz-Jeghers, Cowden, Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba, Cronkite-Canada (figure).

## Familial Juvenile Polyposis
- AD, from abnormal mutation of a gene controlling cell growth; polyps are hamartomatous, 10 or more, in the colon and also often in the rectum; sometimes small bowel and stomach; they carry malignant risk.
- Number of polyps at least 10, some hundreds; family history; extracolonic manifestations: clubbing, hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy, macrocephaly, alopecia, cleft lip and palate, psoriasis, congenital heart disease, gut malrotation, Meckel diverticulum, KUB malformation.
- Cancer risk in the polyps about 9-25%, 70% of cancers at about age 60.
- Sabiston 2017 p.1368: colonoscopic surveillance every 2 years starting at age 12 (some at 15, every 2 years); if a polyp is found, remove and examine; if cancer, total colectomy + ileorectal anastomosis.
- Differs from the (sporadic) juvenile polyp, the most common polyp of children, non-neoplastic, solitary, 80% in the sigmoid and rectum.

## Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome
- AD; GI hamartomatous polyps at least 2 with classic mucocutaneous pigmentation (dark spots) on lip, face, nails; now believed lifetime cancer risk about 40%, not only in the bowel but also higher risk in organs such as the liver. Figure: classic lip pigmentation (skip).
- Polyps from stomach to rectum but most often in the small bowel.
- PJS patients should have EGD, capsule enteroscopy or double balloon enteroscopy starting age 10 every 2 years, with colonoscopy starting age 25 every 3 years.
- Treatment: no firm conclusion; polypectomy by scope where possible; capsule to look for large pedunculated polyps over 1 cm: open abdomen and do intraoperative enteroscopy, clear polyps as much as possible; prophylactic resection has little support.

## Hyperplastic Polyp
- The most common colorectal polyp, in the sigmoid and rectum, size under 5 mm, pale; non-neoplastic with no malignant potential, except size over 1 cm, or number over 20 (hyperplastic polyposis), or serrated polyp, which carry cancer risk.
- Treatment: under 5 mm: no need to remove; over 5 mm: polypectomy.
- Surveillance colonoscopy: size over 5 mm: every 5 years; size over 1 cm: every 3 years.

## Hyperplastic Polyposis
- Inherited syndrome with hyperplastic polyps mixed with adenoma (serrated adenoma); some believe it is a variant of hyperplastic polyp with malignant potential less than adenomatous polyp.
- Polyps over 20, each over 10 mm (1 cm), at the right colon.
- Treatment: not harsh, unlike FAP: polypectomy and have the pathologist look; if non-neoplastic, routine CRC screening and follow-up.

## Cronkite-Canada Syndrome
- GI hamartomatous polyposis (cancer risk 5%) with ectodermal abnormalities: alopecia, cutaneous pigmentation, atrophy of finger and toe nails. Common symptoms: diarrhea, then abdominal pain, weight loss.
- Whatever treatment is chosen, operate only when there is a complication from polyps.

## Colon Cancer
- Colon and rectal cancer come from normal mucosa, dysplasia, polyp, then cancer in sequence, taking about 10 years, but in hereditary groups only 3-5 years.
- Colon cancer is the most common cause of large bowel obstruction.
- Age usually over 50; located on the left (Lt > Rt): sigmoid most (22%), but less than rectum (46%).
- Symptoms: 1. right colon: large mass, hole like a cauliflower, with dull abdominal pain, anemia, weakness, anorexia, weight loss; right-sided lesions are usually polypoid; 2. left colon: small size, usually scirrhous type: change of bowel habit, difficulty passing stool, narrowing stool, mucous bloody stool, some present with intestinal obstruction.
- Under 40 years: found 5-8%; often presents with rectal bleeding up to 40%.
- Increased risk factors: 1. inherited polyps; 2. IBD; 3. age over 50, smoker, low-fiber diet especially high animal fat and protein, alcohol; 4. ureterosigmoidostomy (urine nitrate, amine and bacteria produce carcinogen nitrosamine; cancer after about 20-26 years, suspect cancer when there is obstruction at the implanted ureter, so colonoscopic surveillance is needed); 5. acromegaly; 6. pelvic irradiation; 7. previous adenoma, previous CRC, ovarian, uterine, breast cancer, family history of CRC.
- Do not add risk: 1. juvenile polyp and inflammatory polyp; 2. colitis from amebic, pseudomembranous, ischemic; 3. cholecystectomy (once thought linked to cecal cancer; no longer believed).
- Strongest factor reducing CRC incidence: finding polyps; polypectomy is the best way to reduce CRC.
- Diagnosis: investigations aim to answer: 1. is it truly cancer: colonoscope + biopsy; 2. synchronous cancer: found in 5%; 3. metastasis or not.
- CEA: if raised after surgery, usually recurrence; also high in cancers of lung, breast, stomach, pancreas, and in smokers, thyroid disease, IBD, cirrhosis, pancreatitis; so CEA should not be used to screen CRC.
- Colonoscopy: the most accurate, biopsy, see the whole colon, no missed synchronous lesion.
- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis: to assess spread, intra-abdominal metastasis, pelvis, liver, kidney, ureter, ovary.
- PET scan: not routine; in a case where CT shows clearly unresectable cancer, no need (does not change treatment); keep PET for cases where CT cannot tell if it is a metastatic focus; disadvantage: cannot separate infection or inflammation from cancer.

## Staging of Colorectal Cancer
- In the past Dukes staging (from St. Mark's Hospital, England) and Astler-Coller (USA); no longer used; TNM from AJCC (USA) and UICC (Europe) is used now. The author says to study TNM 2002 first, then AJCC 8th edition (2017) for what changed.
### T status
- Tx: primary tumor cannot be assessed. T0: no evidence of primary tumor. Tis: in situ, cancer not yet through the basement membrane or muscularis mucosae (reported as intraepithelial or intramucosal carcinoma). T1: through the muscularis mucosae into the submucosa (invasive cancer once through this layer). T2: into muscle, muscularis propria. T3: through muscle into the subserosa of colon or non-peritonealized pericolic or perirectal tissue. T4: adjacent organ invasion; in 2010 split: T4a tumor invades through serosa to visceral peritoneum; T4b invades adjacent organ.
### Node status (colon and rectum: at least 12 nodes should be examined)
- Nx: regional nodes cannot be assessed. N1: positive pericolic or perirectal nodes 1-3 (2010: N1a 1 node; N1b 2-3 nodes; N1c tumor deposits in nerve or mesentery, no node). N2: positive 4 or more (2010: N2a 4-6 nodes; N2b 7 or more).
### M
- 2010: M1a single site metastasis; M1b multiple sites.
- 2017 AJCC 8th edition: T and N unchanged; change is in M: M1a: metastasis confined to one organ (liver, lung, ovary, non-regional lymph node) without peritoneal metastases; M1b: metastasis in more than one organ; M1c: metastasis to the peritoneum with or without other organ involvement. Stage IV: any T, any N, M1; stage IVA M1a; IVB M1b; IVC M1c. Stages otherwise unchanged. Appendix: introduction of Tis (LAMN) = low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm confined to the appendix.
- Easy summary: look at N first: node positive means at least stage 3; M positive means stage 4; stage 1 = T1-2 N0 M0; stage 2 = T3-4 N0 M0.
- Limitations of TNM 2017: 1. in cases with prior neoadjuvant treatment, pathological staging after surgery is affected (tumor shrinks, T and N shrink), giving lower stage than truth; 2. prognosis besides T, N, M depends on histologic grade, vascular invasion, tumor molecular markers (DCC, DNA microsatellite instability), which are not included in TNM.
### Investigation of colon cancer (NCCN)
1. CBC, platelets, chemistry profile; 2. CEA; 3. colonoscopy; 4. chest CT (non-contrast); 5. abdomen and pelvic CT with IV and oral contrast; 6. PET-CT not routine, used when CT has doubtful findings. US centers use PET-CT instead of PET alone. In Thailand, choose what is suitable and see how much the Comptroller General's Department allows [as printed]. NCCN recommends consulting an enterostomal therapist in cases that may need an ostomy.

## Colorectal Cancer Screening Tools
- Colonoscopy: the best; accuracy 95%; also biopsy a mass or treat (polypectomy); disadvantages: costly, invasive, perforation or bleeding about 0.03%, sees only mucosa.
- Flexible sigmoidoscopy: too short, misses right-sided cancer, little role in screening.
- CT colonography: accuracy about 90%, sees inside and outside the bowel, not invasive, but may miss lesions under 5 mm or flat lesions; polyp over 1 cm, or over 5 mm and 3 or more in number: proceed to colonoscopy; disadvantage: needs bowel prep like colonoscopy, and cannot treat; if used for screening, repeat every 5 years.
- Double contrast barium enema (DCBE): sensitivity 40%, in combination with FOBT, still misdiagnosed up to 25%; if a suspicious lesion is found it still needs colonoscopy: waste of time.
- FOBT: 1. guaiac fecal occult blood test (gFBOT): collect stool 3 days in a row; peroxidase in hemoglobin turns the guaiac to blue; specificity not very good; not workable now because a positive result needs colonoscopy anyway; before testing avoid red meat and high-peroxidase food (radish, turnip, cauliflower), Vit C, FeSO4, NSAID for at least 48 hr; if used it must be done every year. 2. Fecal immunochemical test (FIT): detects antibody to globin in hemoglobin; advantage: one sample only, no diet restriction; if positive, colonoscopy. 3. Fecal DNA test: expensive; if positive, colonoscopy.
- Rigid sigmoidoscope: too short, should not be used. MRI: keep for rectal cancer.

## Route of Spread & Metastasis
1. Lymphatic: most common first route of spread.
2. Best predictor of LN metastasis is thickness of tumor invasion: Tis: LN positive 0%; T1-2: 5-20%; T3-4: over 50%.
3. Most common distant metastasis: liver; least: bone. 4. Chance of ovarian metastasis 5%.
- Colon cancer: the problem is distant metastasis, so treatment often needs chemo; rectal cancer: much more local recurrence than colon, so treatment needs RT.
- Prognosis of colon cancer: LN involvement is the most important; other bad factors: short symptom duration, poor histology, mucin-secreting, signet ring cell, angiolymphatic invasion, perineural invasion, aneuploid nuclei, many blood transfusions at surgery, complications such as perforation or obstruction; presenting with bleeding is not associated with worse prognosis; factors not related to prognosis: tumor location (contradicting Greenfield who believed age under 40 and rectosigmoid/rectum tumor have bad prognosis).

| Stage 1 T2 N0 M0; Stage 2A T3 N0 M0 | if high-risk cancer (young patient or high-risk histology, poorly differentiated, LVI): adjuvant chemo after surgery |
| Stage 2B-2C T4a-4b N0 M0 | standard cancer resection and adjuvant chemo; for clinical T4b consider neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery |
| Stage 3: any T N1-2 M0 (lymph node metastasis) | standard cancer resection + adjuvant chemo (FOLFOX) |
| Stage 4: any T any N M1 | resection + metastasectomy + chemo, or palliative care; HIPEC for carcinomatosis remains a selected case |
### High-risk stage 2 colon cancer
- Adjuvant chemotherapy helps in stage 3, but in stage 2 benefit is not clear, except in patients with high risk of recurrence:
  1. Histology poorly differentiated or undifferentiated (tumor grade as a prognostic factor is being questioned, but for now count as high risk). 2. Lymphovascular invasion. 3. Perineural invasion. 4. Tumor obstruction or perforation. 5. Fewer than 12 nodes examined. 6. Close, indeterminate or positive margin.

## Preoperative Preparation
- Mechanical bowel preparation (MBP) before elective colon resection is controversial and a matter of surgeon preference; current data do not support that washing stool out of the colon lowers leak rate, infection rate or mortality, since stool is solid and liquid stool left behind carries organisms.
- Still, many surgeons, including the author, wash the stool out because they dislike feeling the stool and the smell in the OR; when the colon is empty, handling tissue and cutting is easier; and when it leaks, infection is easier to control.
- By theory MBP is not necessary. Everyone agrees MBP is needed when intraoperative colonoscopy will be done or the operation is laparoscopic.
- Two guidelines often cited: 1. Canada 2010: no need for MBP in elective right or left colorectal surgery; but for lap or low anterior resection, MBP or not both acceptable; 2. SAGES 2013: MBP in lower rectal surgery, but for colon and upper rectum MBP optional.
- Prep with Nichols & Condon method (3 days, then surgery on day 4) is not popular; now oral Na phosphate 45 cc at 7 am and 5 pm, drink 90 cc/day (not much); a highly osmotic agent, so may cause dehydration; polyethylene glycol (PEG) such as GoLyte, CoLyte is not preferred: need 4 liters; if cannot, 2 liters, patient vomits.
- Antibiotics for prophylaxis: oral and IV forms; must cover gram-negative and anaerobe; the best regimen: give before surgery and again after for 24 hr; in theory can stop right after, but practice believes giving longer is better.
- DVT prophylaxis: popular in USA since patients are mostly elderly; for us debated whether it is worthwhile.
- Stoma planning: advise the patient before surgery, since an ostomy (temporary or permanent) may be needed; talk to the patient and relatives; do not explain so the patient does not understand the necessity.
- Landmarks to prepare: 1. a position visible and easy to care for; 2. mark in standing or sitting position; 3. placed away from costal margin, iliac crest, previous scar, abdominal creases.

## Oncologic Colectomy
- Resectable non-obstructing cancer: colectomy with en bloc removal of regional lymph nodes; extent depends on location of the tumor.
- Lymphadenectomy follows the origin of feeding vessels; at least 12 nodes should be examined to state N stage accurately; sentinel lymph node role is still only research; if a node outside the field of resection is suspected malignant at surgery, excise it too.
- Remember: if after curative resection a positive node remains in the abdomen, count it R2 resection.
- Minimally invasive approaches (lap, lap assist, robot): an option in managing colon cancer; longest follow up so far 3 years shows survival no different from open.
- Memorial Sloan Kettering 2017 (figure): a right colectomy; b extended right colectomy; c transverse colectomy; d right colectomy extended to the splenic flexure; e left colectomy; f sigmoidectomy.
- Curative intent: remove the primary tumor with its lymphovascular pedicle: 1. proximal margin 5-10 cm, distal margin not less than 5 cm; 2. en bloc; 3. tumor site determines vessels to ligate and cut; 4. more than 12 nodes examined for correct N stage; sentinel node still only research.
### Tumor location
1. Cecum and ascending colon: right hemicolectomy, removing colon fed by ileocolic, right colic and right branch of middle colic artery, from terminal ileum 10 cm up to the proximal transverse colon.
2. Hepatic flexure: extended right hemicolectomy, cutting colon up to the left branch of the middle colic artery.
3. Transverse colon: theory: resect only transverse colon, but anastomosis is difficult, so extended right or extended left hemicolectomy is advised depending on tumor location (proximal or distal), including omentum.
4. Splenic flexure and descending colon: left hemicolectomy, cutting the left branch of the middle colic to the left colic artery, from the distal transverse colon to the junction of descending and sigmoid colon.
5. Sigmoid colon: sigmoidectomy or anterior resection: sigmoid colon to upper rectum.
### Sigmoid colectomy
1. Ligate and divide sigmoid branches of the IMA; IMA high ligation = cut IMA from 1 cm from the aorta before it divides into the left colic artery (reduces tension at the anastomosis); low ligation = cut IMA below the left colic artery; no difference in recurrence and survival between the two (figure: high IMA transection in coloanal anastomosis to obtain maximal mobilization).
2. Resect the whole sigmoid to the peritoneal reflection. 3. Take down the splenic flexure to reduce tension. 4. Anastomose descending colon to upper rectum.
- Subtotal colectomy: 1. preserve distal branch of sigmoid artery; 2. anastomose terminal ileum to distal sigmoid colon.
- Total colectomy: 1. resect the sigmoid colon completely, no need to preserve distal sigmoid artery branches; 2. anastomose terminal ileum to upper rectum.
- Anterior resection: resect the rectum through the abdomen into the pelvis:
  1. High anterior resection: cut distal sigmoid and upper rectum; mesorectum cut 5 cm from the lower edge of tumor; no need to remove all mesorectum (partial or adequate mesorectal excision, AME); the rectum is cut without opening the pelvic peritoneum or mobilizing the rectum from the sacral curve; the rest of the colon remains; ligate IMA at its base and IMV at the pancreas.
  2. Low anterior resection: total mesorectal excision (TME), open pelvic peritoneum, mobilize the rectum from the sacral curve, take down the splenic flexure; anastomose colon to extraperitoneal rectum with the lower margin only 2 cm from the tumor.
  3. Extended or ultralow anterior resection: dissect down to the distal rectum, anastomose colon to anus; do proximal diversion, e.g. loop ileostomy, to prevent leak passing the low anastomosis, which if infected gives pelvic sepsis and death.

## Colon Cancer Perforation
- Usually at the tumor site with much necrosis, rather than the cecum splitting. Treatment: look at patient condition, tumor location vs perforation site, and contamination:
  1. Right-sided colon: usually right hemicolectomy with anastomosis, except if the patient is in poor condition or has much contamination: resect without anastomosis, both ends as ostomy.
  2. Left-sided colon: resection then proximal colostomy with distal end closed as Hartmann; but if proximal or right colon is very distended, ischemic, or the cecum is seen about to split: subtotal colectomy.
  3. Right-sided perforation from left-sided obstruction: subtotal colectomy.

## Massive Bleeding
- Treatment uses the same principle as perforated cancer (look from the top).

## Palliative Treatment
- In advanced cases after work-up (CBC, chemistry, CEA, colonoscopy, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, gene KRAS, BRAF, PET/CT, biopsy) finding metastasis (M+) so widespread that cure by surgery is not hoped for, treatment usually uses chemo and targeted therapy.
- Sometimes during chemotherapy we may need to take the patient for colostomy, segmental resection or wide local excision. Indications: 1. obstruction; 2. perforation; 3. bleeding; 4. pain; 5. soiling until care is unbearable.
- Try to avoid colostomy at all; let the patient enjoy defecation to the end of life; SEMS may be the answer for these patients.
- Some patients look unfavorable at first, but after chemo the primary at colon/rectum and metastases shrink until cure by surgery may be possible, or not: nobody knows; the problem is what to do: do not rush to operate; give the drug and watch for trends, except patients with poor body build, liver metastasis over 50-75%, lung involvement, or peritoneal seeding, who must wait for RCT-based studies with more cases.

## Liver Metastasis
- Results are very good if good cases are selected; for the treatment method see the liver chapter; for rectal cancer apply the same principle; if a good case, do both at the same time.

## Lung Metastasis
- Like liver metastasis: if resectable, resect, with the primary able to have R0 resection; before resecting a lung mass do PET scan to exclude extrathoracic lesion.
- General guideline for pulmonary resection: 1. lung masses not over 3, each not over 3 cm; 2. R0 resection possible; 3. patient fit enough with good pulmonary function and reserve.
- In the past lung metastasis was advised as staged operation; now the lung can be done at the same time.

## Bone Metastasis
- Severe pain not controlled by analgesics: RT; fracture: internal fixation + RT.

## Brain Metastasis
- Found 1-2%, usually within 2 years after surgery; rectal cancer goes to brain more than colon cancer. Symptoms of raised intracranial pressure: headache, arm/leg weakness.
- Treatment: solitary, non-vital region: resect if possible; if not resectable: steroid + RT.

## Peritoneal Carcinomatosis
- Peritoneal metastasis means advanced disease with short survival: 1. non-obstructing cancer: chemo; 2. obstruction: choose among resection, colostomy, bypass or stent.
- Paul H. Sugarbaker (Washington Cancer Institute), the father of cytoreductive surgery used for appendix cancer and pseudomyxoma, tried it in 200 patients with colon cancer with peritoneal carcinomatosis; where everyone would have been dead within 5 years, 5-year survival was 40%. Technique: strip the peritoneal surface from the diaphragm downward, greater omentectomy, splenectomy, cholecystectomy, strip hepatoduodenal ligament and floor of the omental bursa; below: rectosigmoid resection, TAH, strip the cul-de-sac; then close, place Tenckhoff catheters, heated intraoperative intraperitoneal chemo left in for 90 minutes, then reopen to wash the chemo out.
- NCCN 2016 regarded cytoreductive surgery as a clinical trial for big centers only; by 2017 accepted as part of treatment. For peritonectomy read Current Surgical Therapy 9th ed. p.246-251 or the appendix chapter (pseudomyxoma peritonei).

## Laparoscopic Colectomy
- Currently an acceptable treatment of colon cancer; safety and complications no more than open; overall survival and recurrence no different from open.
- Advantages: shorter hospital stay, less IV/analgesia, eats sooner, nicer wound. Disadvantages: longer operating time than open.
- Problem: loss of tactile sensation, so a tumor cannot be located by hand; helpers: 1. CT; 2. tattoo India ink in the submucosa at colonoscopy, or intraoperative colonoscopy; 3. hand-assisted.
- Bleeding and bowel injury are slightly more in lap but no statistical significance.
- Even with liver metastasis, lap colectomy is not prohibited. If unsure (little experience, learning curve under 25 cases, very large mass, locally advanced disease), convert to open. Cochrane review in over 4,000 patients: long-term outcome of lap no different from open.
- NCCN 2019 criteria for lap-assisted colectomy: 1. surgeon with much experience in lap colonic disease; 2. no disease in rectum; 3. no prohibitive abdominal adhesions; 4. no locally advanced disease; 5. lesion not large on preop review; 6. no obstruction or perforation from cancer.
- Locally advanced or T4 was once prohibited; now experienced teams with good equipment can do it with conversion rate under 19% and disease-free interval and 5-year survival no different from open; so T4 locally advanced cancer can be operated by laparoscopic surgery.
- Patients who should not have laparoscopic colectomy: 1. hemodynamically unstable; 2. massive colonic obstruction with severe colonic dilatation; 3. tumor size over 7-8 cm; 4. [author's addition] very old patients with many diseases, previous abdominal surgery with much adhesion, very obese: open is better.
- Hand-assisted laparoscopic colorectal surgery (HALS) compared with open: oncologic outcome and postop results: bowel motility returns sooner, shorter hospital stay, no different complications, similar blood given, 5-year survival no different; cost of HALS higher than open.

## Complete Mesocolic Excision (CME)
- As rectal cancer has TME, colon cancer may have CME; in Japan CME has long been done and called D3 lymphadenectomy.
- D1 LND: paracolic nodes only; D2 LND: paracolic + intermediate nodes; D3 LND: D2 + central nodes. Figure: colonic tumor, D1, D2, D3 lines; mesocolon usually divided between lines in standard resection; divided at the D3 line in CME as central vascular ligation (CVL).
- Hohenberger reported in 2009 that colon also has a holy plane (not only rectum): dissect the visceral plane off the parietal plane; between these 2 planes lies tumor embolus; if the visceral peritoneum covering the colon is torn at surgery, tumor spreads, producing intraperitoneal recurrence. He collected patients 2003-2009, doing colectomy with bowel length at least 10 cm + CME (intact visceral peritoneum) + central LND (D3) + central vascular ligation.
- Advantages of CME: lymph node harvest over 30 nodes, lower local recurrence, better survival, while MBR and MTR did not increase (Japanese, who prefer skin-wearing and sashimi: Hohenberger convinced them; reports from Denmark support CME); those who benefit are stage 1-3; Japan recommends CME for all colon cancer T3 and above.
- Control study: CME harvests more nodes than conventional; if the patient is unlucky (a fall), morbidity (MBR) and mortality (MTR) not different; but no RCT yet proving CME increases survival; NCCN 2018 mentions CME (radial or circumferential margin) in passing; believed to matter for the non-peritonized colon; CME is not yet standard like TME, so surgeons must keep following.

## Chemotherapy for CRC
- Given in every node-positive case (stage 3); node-negative (stage 2) disease should have it in high-risk cases: poorly differentiated histology, LVI, localized perforation, obstruction, inadequate margins, T4 lesion, low nodal count (under 12).
- In the past 5FU/LV (LV helps 5FU's potency); now 5FU/LV with oxaliplatin or irinotecan, several regimens: FOLFOX, FOLFIRI; as far as the author sees, mostly FOLFOX for 6 months.
- Biologic therapy: a new, costly group of drugs, given with 2nd line chemo; results disappointing:
  1. Bevacizumab (avastin): anti-VEGF, reduces vessels feeding the cancer; later reports: did not make patients live longer in cancers of colon, breast, kidney, lung, brain; side effects: anaphylaxis, GI perforation when used with chemo, delays wound healing for about 6 months after stopping; the US FDA withdrew it from breast cancer treatment.
  2. Erbitux (cetuximab): blocks the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), which controls cell growth, repair and survival; EGFR is positive in CRC 75-89%, most in head and neck cancer 90-100%.
  3. Panitumumab: a new targeted drug used in the USA; do not go far into it.
- Giving targeted therapy plus chemo as adjuvant: no benefit in stage 3, but gives results in stage 4 (already metastatic).
- Many biologic markers are being studied (microsatellite instability MSI of MMR gene, micro RNAs, KRAS mutation, BRAF mutation) to see who benefits from adjuvant chemo in stage 2 colon cancer; for now: in non-mutated KRAS study BRAF status first to decide whether to give biologic therapy.
- MSI: a molecular biomarker found in sporadic CRC about 15% and in every case of HNPCC; tested by immunohistochemistry of tissue; MSI-H (high level) = absence of expression of MMR protein; MSI-L (low)/MSS (stable).
- In stage 2 colon cancer: if MSI-H: no adjuvant chemo; if MSI-L or MSS with high-risk clinicopathologic factors: consider adjuvant chemo; if low-risk factors: no adjuvant chemo.

## Surveillance (NCCN 2019)
- Most recurrence is found in 3-5 years; if not local recurrence, it spreads to the liver.
- Stage 1 (low recurrence risk): history, examination, CEA like stage 2, 3, 4; colonoscopy at 1 year, 3 years, then every 5 years; CT not needed if no suspicion.
- Stages 2, 3, 4: 1. history and PE every 3-6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for a total of 5 years; 2. CEA (T2 or more) every 3-6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for total 5 years; no LFT needed; 3. chest/abdomen/pelvic CT: stage 2, 3: every 6-12 months for 5 years; stage 4: every 3-6 months for 2 years then every 6-12 months until year 5; 4. colonoscopy: at 1 year after surgery (except when not done before surgery, e.g. obstruction: then within 3-6 months); then at 3 years and every 5 years; 5. PET-CT to monitor recurrence is not routinely recommended.

## Rectal Cancer
- Prominent symptoms: bloody or mucus-bloody stool, abdominal cramps, urge to defecate but feeling incomplete (tenesmus). Treatment principles resemble colon cancer, but the rectum has limits: it lies in the narrow pelvis, so wide resection is difficult and risks incontinence, bladder problems, impotence.
- Spread: upper and middle third go up along the superior rectal vessels to the IMV; lower rectum goes 2 ways: up, or out the sides, even to inguinal nodes.
### Diagnosis and workup
1. PR: size and distance from anal verge, fixation, sphincter or adjacent organ invasion; the finger reaches up to 8 cm, where 20% of cancers can be found.
2. Rigid sigmoidoscope or proctoscope + biopsy: do not forget to inject methylene blue as tattoo; biopsy at the margin without necrosis, orient the specimen; NCCN does not advise using such a scope now (colonoscopy is needed anyway).
3. Colonoscopy: biopsy and exclude synchronous lesion.
4. Endorectal US (ERUS): T&N staging, accurate about 90%: 80-95% accurate in tumor staging; 70-75% in mesorectal node staging; very good at layers of rectal wall; limited to lesions under 14 cm from anus, not applicable for upper rectum or stenosing tumor; very useful in determining extension into anal canal (important for planning sphincter-preserving surgery).
5. Pelvic MRI with contrast (NCCN 2019 advises every case; no bowel prep needed; about 45 minutes): advantages: defines borders, depth of invasion, distance to anal verge, sphincter, peritoneal reflection; assesses pelvic side wall well; helps assess lateral nodes; assesses circumferential resection margin (CRM): distance from tumor to mesorectal fascia; if 1 cm or less, count as CRM involvement; assesses extramural venous invasion (EMVI); MRI with ERUS gives the best locoregional assessment. Suspicious node: over 8 mm, round, irregular border, heterogeneous signal; benign node egg- or bean-shaped with smooth border. Figure: normal rectal anatomy on T2 MRI; mucinous adenocarcinoma (skip).
6. CT chest/abdomen: liver and lung metastasis.
7. PET scan: not routine; keep to confirm advanced cases for whether other treatment than surgery is appropriate.
- NCCN 2019 recommended workup: 1. biopsy and pathological review; 2. colonoscopy; 3. chest/abdominal/pelvic CT; 4. CEA; 5. ERUS or pelvic MRI with contrast (preferred); 6. stomal therapist to teach and mark before surgery; 7. PET-CT not routine.
- TNM of rectal cancer: AJCC 8th ed 2017, changes from 2010 same as colon cancer.
### Management of rectal cancer
- Goals: 1. good local control, low recurrence; 2. prolong survival; 3. good quality of life: 3.1 can defecate (via anus), no colostomy, sphincter preserved with no incontinence; 3.2 can control voiding, no urine bag; 3.3 sexual function preserved (erection/ejaculation).
- Figure (Schwartz edition 11, 2019 p.1300, stage-specific therapy of rectal adenocarcinoma), as steps: staging evaluation: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, endorectal US, +/- MRI, colonoscopy/CT colonography. Stage I (T1-2 N0 M0): high risk or refuses radical resection: transanal excision +/- neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemoradiation; low risk and accepts radical resection: radical resection. Stage II (T3-4 N0 M0) and Stage III (any T, N1-3, M0): neoadjuvant chemoradiation -> restage -> no metastases -> radical resection. Stage IV (any T any N M1): asymptomatic: chemotherapy -> restage: unresectable/multiple metastatic sites: continue chemotherapy; resectable/single metastatic site: consider resection; symptomatic: chemoradiation vs palliative procedure (stent, laser ablation, stoma, resection).
### Early rectal cancer (Tis, T1-2 N0 M0)
- Early cancer: low chance of node or lung spread; main treatment surgery, no need for neoadjuvant treatment.
- Stage 0 Tis N0 M0: treat like colon polyp (non-invasive: in situ, high grade dysplasia): polypectomy with margin 1 cm; if pathology is invasive cancer but T1, chance of N positive about 13%: if favorable histology, clear margins and single specimen, pedunculated or sessile polyp can be observed.
- Stage 1 T1-2 N0 M0: if T1 polypectomy was fragmented, margin not assessable or unfavorable histology: options: transanal local excision or transabdominal radical resection; in cases with only transanal local excision, review pathology after surgery: if pathology reports: 1. T2 lesion; 2. margin not free, or T1 with high risk (poorly differentiated, angiolymphatic or perineural invasion, Kudo SM3): need further treatment [continues on next page].
  - Further treatment: 2.1 adjuvant chemoRT, or 2.2 re-operate with transabdominal radical resection at once; if forced to choose, the author leans toward 2.2; NCCN 2017 also advises operating the same way, then reviewing postop staging: T1-2 N0 M0: observe; T3-4 N0 or any T with N+: adjuvant chemotherapy.
### Locally advanced rectal cancer (cT3-4 cN0 M0, or any T with N+)
- Stage 2: T3-4 N0; any T N1 or more with M0 is stage 3 (node metastasis).
- Everyone now agrees this group should get neoadjuvant treatment (chemo and/or chemoradiation); ESMO guideline 2017 also gives hints for not giving neoadjuvant in T3N0: MRI used to decide: if invasion into the muscle layer under 5 mm, no EMVI, clear mesorectal fascia (MRF), tumor above the pelvic floor, neoadjuvant treatment before surgery is not necessary.
- Advantages of neoadjuvant treatment: 1. downsizing or downstaging; 2. if the lesion is under 6 cm from anal verge, sphincter preservation up to 40%; 3. reduces local recurrence after surgery; 4. controls micrometastasis; 5. if pathological complete response (pCR) is achieved, prognosis is better than non-responders.
- Disadvantage: after surgery the pathological stage is truly understaged; and non-responders waste about 2 months in which a curable cancer may become incurable.
- Forms of neoadjuvant treatment: RT or chemo first, then surgery; or both together. RT before surgery is better than after, since less local recurrence.
- Long course: 2 Gy/day, 5 days a week (rest Sat-Sun) continuous for 5 weeks, total dose about 50 Gy; short course: 5 Gy/day x 5 days, one week only, total about 25 Gy, then surgery after 1 week; local control and long-term survival are not different, but long course gives complete response more often (long course is more popular).
- Concurrent chemoRT (CCRT) is more popular than neoadjuvant chemo alone. Radiosensitizing agents that enhance local control of RT: 5-FU and capecitabine (equally effective); irinotecan (used in metastatic cancer) is still in phase 3 trials; oxaliplatin adds toxicity without added benefit per research.
- CCRT commonly used: infusion 5-FU/LV together with RT. Tumor response is graded by Dworak tumor regression grade (TRG): TRG 0 no response, TRG 4 tumor disappeared, only fibrous tissue.

| Score | Dworak (0-4) | AJCC (0-3) | Mandard (1-5) | MSKCC (1-3) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TRG 0 | minimal tumor response to treatment | no residual tumor cells | - | - |
| TRG 1 | fibrosis in under 25% of tumor | single or small group of cells | no residual tumor cells | no residual tumor cells |
| TRG 2 | fibrosis in 25-50% of tumor | cancer with fibrotic response | rare cancer cells | 86-99% tumor response |
| TRG 3 | fibrosis in over 50% of tumor | minimal tumor response to treatment | fibrosis > residual cancer | 85% or less tumor response |
| TRG 4 | no residual tumor cells | - | residual cancer > fibrosis | - |
| TRG 5 | - | - | minimal tumor response to treatment | - |
- After long course neoadjuvant chemoRT: waiting 6-8 weeks to restage is recommended now; new reports say waiting up to 4 more weeks gives more response, but the downside of waiting over 6-8 weeks is pelvic fibrosis making surgery difficult and adherent; so the usual wait is 6-8 weeks. Then restage; transabdominal radical resection is best if feasible; after surgery adjuvant chemo continues.
- Problem: in patients with concurrent chemoRT who achieve complete clinical response (cCR): tumor disappeared on DRE (no mass, no ulcer), scope shows scar white, telangiectasia, pelvic MRI shows no tumor (dark T2 signal = fibrosis), no suspicious node; after rectal resection the pathology may show complete pathologic response (pCR, found about 10%). What to do:
  1. Operate as planned: choose between TME or local excision; or
  2. Follow up first: wait-and-watch protocol: DRE, CEA every 3 months, scope with pelvic MRI every 3 months in the first year, then every 6 months; CT chest/abdomen/pelvis every 6 months, in the first year; then once a year; recurrence is highest in the first year, mostly mucosal recurrence; if the mass regrows, plan surgery again; those operated late have no worse cure results.
- Data from many reports: patients with cCR have pCR only 60-75%, i.e. 25-40% still have cancer cells, including the opinion of Dr. Ton (a senior): the approach should be like esophageal cancer: even if cCR, operate as before, no re-staging, no change to plan.
- So for rectal cancer the answer: immediate completion oncologic resection.
- Post-op RT: after completely resected rectal cancer with nodes cleared, RT is still needed in: 1. low rectal cancer; 2. T3b or T4; 3. CRM under 1 mm. Post-op chemoRT should be given when: 1. T3, T4 (T2 still debated); 2. N1; 3. N0 with perforation or obstruction.
### Advanced rectal cancer
- M1 = stage 4: treatment is combined: start with combination chemotherapy 2-3 months for palliation: the mass shrinks, symptoms and local complications (pelvic pain, urinary obstruction, pelvic abscess, rectovaginal fistula) decrease; except when after chemo liver and lung metastases disappear until both can be completely resected and the primary resectable, then do radical operation; if unresectable or the patient cannot go to the OR, continue systemic chemo; surgery in unresectable/advanced cases is kept for perforation, uncontrollable bleeding or obstruction.
- Remember: T invasion is the most important factor in choosing treatment; also look at N, M, distance of the mass from the anal verge, and whether the patient can tolerate surgery.
- Choice of treatment: let the patient take part in decision every time; explain operative risks and the chance of needing a colostomy; always prepare a colostomy site.
- Do KUB assessment: in CT with IV contrast do not forget to look at the kidney; ask the urologist to do cystoscopy if bladder invasion is suspected, because en bloc removal of bladder or ureter may be needed; ureteral stents can help; in women do PV every case for invasion or RV fistula; female pelvis is wider than male, so low anastomosis is easier.
### Surgical treatment (details)
1. TME: introduced by Prof. Heald in 1988: excise rectum with fatty tissue and perirectal fascia wrapped around it along the plane between visceral and parietal endopelvic fascia (the holy plane); the standard operation, clearly reduces recurrence.
2. Circumferential resection margin (CRM) should be over 2 mm; if CRM is 1 mm or less, the pathologist reads positive margin (NCCN).
3. Autonomic nerve preservation (figure: inferior hypogastric plexus, nervi erigentes S2-4, hypogastric nerve, superior hypogastric plexus, bladder, seminal vesicle, vesical plexus): be careful from the origin of the IMA (left of the aorta, about T12-L3, forming the hypogastric nerve); when mobilizing the rectum posteriorly the nerve lies behind the presacral (Waldeyer's) fascia; when mobilizing anteriorly off the seminal vesicle or vagina the nerve lies in front of Denonvilliers' fascia; at the lateral ligament beware nerves at the lateral wall too; during TME beware where nerve injury is likely; anteriorly do sharp dissection under vision, keep the plane in front of Denonvilliers' fascia; if Denonvilliers' fascia is taken, the nerve is already cut.
   - Sympathetic injury: 1. urinary bladder contraction (capacity falls); 2. in men retrograde ejaculation (dry ejaculation, no climax); 3. in women dyspareunia, continuous dryness [as printed].
   - Parasympathetic injury: 1. difficult voiding; 2. erectile dysfunction (fortunately mostly temporary, about 1 year); 3. decreased vaginal lubrication, painful intercourse.
4. Proximal margin: not a problem; need 5-10 cm, usually achievable.
5. Distal margin: best 5 cm; upper rectum usually gets a 5 cm margin; if the lesion is low, e.g. middle rectum, TME is needed and the distal margin should be at least 2 cm; if the patient had neoadjuvant chemoRT, a 1 cm distal margin is acceptable; but if the lesion is at the pelvic floor and sphincter saving is not possible, APR is needed.
6. Sphincter preservation: the tumor must be at least 1 cm above the anorectal ring, and the patient must not have incontinence beforehand.
7. Bowel chosen for anastomosis should look well perfused: not swollen, not previously irradiated, cut edge with oozing blood, soft; do a leak test (fill the pelvis with NSS, clamp the bowel, blow air through the anus to see bubbles); if no leak we are happy.
8. Diverting colostomy (or ileostomy) should be done in cases: 8.1 had preop chemoRT; 8.2 very low anastomosis, under about 6 cm from the anal verge.
9. High ligation of IMA: tie the IMA at its origin from the aorta to clear nodes completely and get adequate mobilization of the proximal colon for a tension-free anastomosis; compared with low ligation (below the origin of the left colic artery) results in 5-year survival are no different; no more lymph nodes cleared; leak may increase since blood supply at the anastomosis is reduced (more MBR and MTR); so if the anastomosis is not tense, high ligation seems more than necessary; blood supply to the proximal left colon above the anastomosis comes from collaterals; beware incomplete marginal artery of Drummond, the important SMA-IMA connection at the splenic flexure (Griffiths point), which can cause ischemia at the anastomosis.
10. Number of lymph nodes examined should be at least 12; sentinel lymph node in rectal cancer is still in study (NCCN).
### Types of operation (2 groups)
1. Radical surgery (depends on tumor location): 1.1 upper rectum: anterior resection (distal margin 5 cm achievable, so no need for TME); 1.2 middle rectum: low anterior resection with distal margin 2 cm, CRM 2 mm, TME required; 1.3 lower rectum: 2 options: 1.3.1 ultralow anterior resection + TME + protective loop ileostomy; 1.3.2 APR. Figure: rectum levels (upper 12-16 cm, middle 8-12 cm, distal 4-8 cm, anal canal 0-4 cm) and operations (HAR, LAR, ultralow AR, APR).
2. Local excision: 2.1 transanal approach: 3 ways: transanal, TEM and TAMIS; 2.2 posterior approach: not popular, know the names for friends: Kraske (trans-sacral), York-Mason (trans-sphincteric): for tumors that cannot be reached transanally.
### Transanal approach
- Transanal excision: full-thickness excision; suits Tis, T1N0 and mobile premalignant lesion (non-fixed), within 8 cm of anal verge (at most 10 cm), no lymphadenopathy on pretreatment imaging, favorable histology: under 3 cm in size (at most 4 cm) or under 30% circumference (at most 40%), T1 lesion, SM 1-2, G1-2, no lymphatic or venous invasion, negative margin (clear margin means over 3 mm).
- Method: 1. position so the cancer is at the bottom: prone for anterior tumor; lithotomy for posterior tumor; 2. open the anus with Lone Star retractor or Pratt retractor; 3. stay-suture traction distal to the mass, then cut around about 1 cm away, full thickness until perirectal fat is seen; 4. close the defect transversely with absorbable suture.
- Transendoscopic microsurgery (TEM): full-thickness excision with an operating rectoscope (4 cm wide, 20 cm long) with magnified view; suits T1 mid to upper rectum, e.g. sessile adenoma at the extraperitoneal rectum; not suitable for tumors above the peritoneal reflection (over 20 cm) or in the lower anal canal (distal lesion under 5 cm), where the scope blocks the mass; also polyps larger than 8 cm or occupying over 50% of rectal circumference; disadvantages: the set costs about 3 million baht, and the large scope may stretch the sphincter, so popularity has decreased. Technique: 1. dilate the anal canal with CO2 insufflation about 6 times; 2. use a proctoscope with wide central-lumen and small caliber videoscope, cut tumor through the scope; 3. not suitable for distal tumor or tumor lying near the fascia propria.
- Transanal Minimally Invasive Surgery (TAMIS): uses single port laparoscopic surgery device in the anus; small instruments, does not stretch the sphincter like TEM.
- Before transanal excision, TEM or TAMIS, the patient should be T1N0, by: 1. ERUS (T stage correct 86%, N 77%); 2. endorectal coil MRI (T 66-92%, N 74-87%).
- Because it is excision of the tumor without clearing nodes, these suit only T1 lesions: TE suits Tis, TEM suits T1.
- Indications for local excision (general): 1. early rectal cancer for cure; 2. high-risk case with postop chemoRT added; 3. palliation, e.g. bleeding.

### Summary of transanal approach (NCCN 2019): selection criteria for local excision
- Tumor: 1. location not over 8 cm from the anal verge (if over 8 cm use TEM; Japanese teams cut at 10 cm); 2. posterior location (remember); PR shows a mobile lesion (if fixed, no); 3. exophytic lesion without ulcer, no sphincter invasion; 4. size under 3 cm (over 2.5 cm is already hard), or not over 30% (1/3) of circumference (Japanese cut at under 4 cm or not over 40% of circumference), because for a very large mass the wound closure is tight and the rectum does not open well; 5. ERUS or MRI shows T1N0 lesion (limited to submucosa); 6. histology well (G1) or moderately differentiated (G2), no lymphovascular or perineural invasion; 7. not a mucinous tumor (high recurrence); 8. no N or M on pretreatment imaging.
- Patient: 1. high risk; 2. palliative intent; 3. refuses colostomy.
- Partial-thickness excision = endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD); full thickness = transanal excision, TEM, TAMIS.
- After local excision, review pathology: 1. T1 with favorable histology and free margin: follow up; if margin not free, re-excise; 2. T2 or unfavorable histology (e.g. poorly differentiated): look at the patient: 2.1 good risk, tolerates drugs: consider oncologic resection; 2.2 poor risk: try chemoRT first, then re-evaluate whether to re-resect; 3. T3-4: neoadjuvant chemoRT; even if the tumor disappears, still do oncologic resection, with chemoRT after surgery.
- Remember: local recurrence after local excision is related to positive margin.

### Posterior approach
- For rectal cancer higher than transanal excision can reach; 2 ways:
  1. Kraske trans-sacral resection: patient prone, midline incision, remove the coccyx, dissect to the rectum; for more room, part of the sacral wing may need removal. Figure: coccyx, levator, anus (skip).
  2. York-Mason trans-sphincteric resection: open to the rectum along the edge of the sacrum, divide the sphincter muscle in steps to reach the back of the rectum. Figures (skip).
- Advantage: for posterior rectal cancer, or rectal tumor in a patient whose state is not strong enough for abdominal surgery; also allows pararectal nodes to be sampled, so staging is accurate. Disadvantage: high morbidity, e.g. rectal fistula and incontinence. Currently not popular because staplers help with low anastomosis.
- For radical surgery: some techniques are worth knowing, done open or by lap.

### Abdominoperineal (A-P) resection (APR)
- 2 ways: standard APR (specimen waisted, figure b) and extended or extralevator APR (cylindrical specimen, figure a): removes tissue around the cancer at the level of the levator ani more widely, giving a cylindrical appearance; advantage: reduces specimen perforation and gives adequate circumferential margin; but benefit over standard APR is not yet proven. Figure: conventional vs extralevator APR (robotic).
- Indications for APR: 1. sphincter invasion; 2. anal incontinence; 3. extensive pelvic disease; 4. poorly differentiated histology of lower rectum; 5. adenocarcinoma of the lower rectum (believed to be downward spread of proximal rectal cancer, so it is already advanced); 6. anastomotic recurrence after LAR.
- In women the posterior 2/3 of the vagina should be removed together. Foley catheter should be removed at about day 5 (bladder or nerve contusion may occur during surgery); sexual dysfunction is as common as in LAR.
- Pre or postoperative RT patients may have a non-healing perineal wound (hard to close; sutures or grafts do not take): treatment: gracilis muscle flap to fill the dead space.
- If liver metastasis is found at opening, and is resectable, simultaneous APR + liver resection can be done, very good if feasible.

### Ultralow rectal anastomosis
- Or extended low anterior resection: a very low anastomosis, needing to dissect into the intersphincteric plane for a coloanal anastomosis.
- Criteria for choosing patients: 1. tumor not lower than the anorectal ring; 2. tumor not spread outside the rectal wall (by PR and ERUS); 3. still normal sphincter function (although you may have heard that those who had preop RT have a lax anus); 4. patient with good prognosis expected to live over 1 year, because adjusting to urgency, frequency and incontinence takes about 6-12 months; with training, in the long run frequency may be only 1-2 times a day.
- Contraindications: 1. impaired anal sphincter function; 2. clear sphincter invasion by cancer.
- Because ultralow anastomosis leaves little or no rectum and the rectal reservoir is lost, patients pass stool often, with urgency and clustering: called low anterior resection (LAR) syndrome. Prevention of frequent stools: make a coloanal J pouch.

### Coloanal J pouch
- Reconstruction of choice after proctectomy; fewer bowel movements than straight (end-to-end) anastomosis. J pouch of small size, about 5 cm, suits as a neoreservoir; if larger (e.g. 10 cm), incomplete evacuation tends to occur: constipation, needing enema or laxative.
- If J pouch cannot be made, coloplasty may be done instead (longitudinal colotomy closed transversely; figure).
- Colonic pouch does not affect 5-year survival.
- Low anastomosis has a high chance of leak, so do a proximal diversion (loop ileostomy) temporarily, wait 6-12 weeks to be sure the ultralow anastomosis is intact, then take down the ileostomy.
- Remember: a diverting loop ileostomy makes leak easier to control and lowers the chance of subsequent fibrotic stricture of the neorectum, but it does not prevent leakage, because leakage depends on many factors, patient and surgeon's skill, not on whether an ileostomy was made.

### Pelvic exenteration
- Radical surgery for rectal cancer with extensive pelvic involvement in a good-risk patient who can tolerate the operation, because without such surgery the patient usually lives no more than 2 years; before doing it, exclude extrapelvic involvement.
- Remove all organs in the pelvis: APR + en bloc ureter, bladder, prostate in men, uterus and vagina in women + permanent colostomy + ileal conduit +/- sacrectomy S2-5 if sacral invasion. Figures: male and female exenteration fields; pouches (skip).
- 5-year survival 13-30%.

### Laparoscopic proctectomy with TME
- Compared with open: short- and long-term outcome no different, but laparoscopic surgery finds more positive circumferential margin and incomplete TME; to fix this some advise transanal TME (TaTME: open from below), but patients operated this way are few with short follow-up.
- Summary: to do rectal cancer by laparoscopy, the surgeon must be a master; the teacher advises not to do it in patients with narrow pelvis, big mass, obstruction, perforation or locally advanced disease. Recommended reading: Schwartz 11e 2019 p.1302-1303 on MIS for colorectal cancer.

### Total mesorectal excision (TME)
- Rectal cancer recurs locally after surgery 8-40%; TME reduces local recurrence to 3-10%, but if the excision is incomplete, especially in a narrow pelvis, recurrence is still high.
- TME has no role in upper rectum (5 cm distal margin is already obtained). TME suits mid and low rectum: excise the rectum with mesorectum (where nodes lie); it lowers local recurrence and increases survival.
- Technique: figures: excision line includes mesorectum; the holy plane (Denonvilliers fascia, neurovascular bundle).
  1. Cut anteriorly along parietal pelvic (endopelvic) fascia and posteriorly along visceral pelvic fascia (fascia propria); do not tear the fascia.
  2. Cut from the sacral promontory above down to the pelvic floor; mobilize the rectum posteriorly first, then laterally, then anteriorly. Figures: presacral plane with lighted St. Mark's retractor; hypogastric nerves swept away posteriorly; lateral dissection of mesorectum off the pelvic side wall, transection of the lateral rectal ligament, hypogastric nerve preserved.
  3. Sharp dissection under direct vision, aiming for CRM over 2 mm (under 2 mm local recurrence rises to 16%); the specimen should be bilobed, not cone-shaped; sharp dissection under direct vision reduces nerve injury, especially at the left lateral ligament where the hypogastric (sympathetic) nerve lies; distal margin should exceed 2 cm, or 5 cm if achievable.
4. NCCN advises frozen section of the distal margin in every case.
5. Low anastomosis, e.g. coloanal anastomosis, should have a protective ileostomy.
- Beware: leak risk after TME is higher than after partial mesorectal excision in upper rectal cancer, because the lower blood supply is reduced.
- Obese, short, fatty man with a narrow pelvis: TME is hard; review pelvic MRI first for tumor invading outside the rectal fascia.
- TME technique (teacher's advice): 1. head-down position, pack the bowel so it does not interfere; 2. good light that reaches deep; 3. good surgeon and assistant (the person doing TME gets very tired, even the fingers); 4. entering the pelvis go posterior first, then lateral, then anterior; 5. beware the internal iliac vein; 6. sharp dissection slowly; 7. cut the lateral ligament close to the rectum; 8. Heald's holy plane: the teacher says there is only one plane for TME, which he calls the "only plane"; 9. ligate the mesorectum.
- Quality of TME specimen (figure): 1. poor surgery, little mesorectum; 2. average surgery with incomplete removal of mesorectum; 3. excellent surgery with complete mesorectal excision.
- Still debated: whether lateral (pelvic) node dissection (LND) with TME in extraperitoneal (below peritoneal reflection) rectal cancer should be routine or selective; most think only high-risk cases with positive lateral node or clinically suspected nodes.
- High risk of positive lateral node: 1. female; 2. tumor size 4 cm or more; 3. pT3, pT4; 4. perirectal pN1 positive; 5. unfavorable histology (moderate to poorly differentiated).
- Complications of lateral node dissection: 1. incomplete or retrograde ejaculation (sympathetic injury); 2. incomplete erection and urinary retention (parasympathetic injury). To prevent these, the Japanese teachers advise the option of autonomic nerve preservation (AND). Figure: autonomic plexus, ureter, iliac vessels, floor of pelvis (skip).

## Anastomosis
- Stapled or hand-sewn, results no different, depends on skill.
- Hand-sewn: one or two layers give the same result; suture material as convenient; continuous or interrupted either.
- The author likes hand-sewn 2 layers: inner layer chromic or vicryl 3/0 continuous; outer layer silk 3/0 interrupted.
- Remember: the anastomosis must be without tension and with good blood supply.
### Stapled anastomosis
- After firing the stapler always check whether the anastomosis is complete: air leak test: push air with a syringe via the anus through a sigmoidoscope, keep the anastomosis under saline with a clamp holding the bowel above it: bubbles mean leak; some advise a dye test: blue dye mixed in water 180-240 cc via NGT, squeeze the bowel to see leakage.
- Then check the donuts for completeness. If no leak and the donuts are complete, we sleep well, no pain.
- If leak or incomplete donut, fix: 1. small hole: suture it (if cannot sleep, add a proximal loop ileostomy as an option); 2. large hole: suture if possible; if difficult or cannot, restaple again.

## Anastomotic Leakage
- The submucosa is the strongest layer.
- Postoperative leak occurs about day 4-7, especially in low anastomosis (under 5 cm from the anal verge). By clinical symptoms: contained leakage: abdominal pain, bloating, fever, WBC up, some with turbid fluid through the drain; there is time to investigate; free leakage: high fever, sepsis, rigid abdomen, rapid deterioration, may need to go to the OR at once.
- Plain film, MRI or CT: bowel wall thickening, free air, fluid collection. Figure: MRI features of rectal anastomotic leak (Ma T et al., Dis Colon Rectum 2019): presacral space width, rectal wall thickness, distal sigmoid thickness (leak rate 14.4% in 125 patients; AUC 0.722, sensitivity 66%, specificity 72%).
- Risk factors: 1. higher ASA score; 2. male, narrow pelvis (hard surgery); 3. COPD, smoking, alcohol, DM, HT; 4. obesity BMI over 25 kg/m2; 5. surgery in peritonitis, intraoperative soilage; 6. surgery in colon obstruction; 7. malnutrition, serum albumin under 3.5; 8. steroid use; 9. preop RT or chemotherapy such as bevacizumab; 10. blood transfusion during surgery; 11. cancer larger than 5 cm; 12. poor tissue, anastomosis less than 5 cm from the anal verge, tension; 13. inexperienced surgeon, operation over 4 hours.
- It was once believed neoadjuvant chemoradiation is not a leak risk factor, but current data say that belief is not true: heavy chemo, poor tissue, so it can leak.
- Temporary diverting stoma remains controversial: proximal diversion does not reduce leakage but reduces the severity of its consequences. Support for diversion in patients with risk factors: the Dutch Leak group advises the colon leakage score (a score like APACHE): if over 11/43 points, high leak chance: do diverting ostomy; problem: the Dutch score is not credible to everyone.
- In low anastomosis, e.g. ultralow rectal anastomosis with TME, suspect leak if: 1. POD 2 fever over 38 C with no cause found; 2. POD 3 drain output over 400 cc/day; 3. POD 6 ileus; 4. before POD 7 diarrhea; 5. abdominal pain unexplained. Symptoms: fever, bloating, vomiting, mild pain, wound serous discharge, drain with turbid fluid: PR gently first to see; in low anastomosis the finger may feel the hole; then investigate: 1. film abdomen (free air); 2. CT (beware: do not squeeze the contrast hard: if leak, it spreads): abnormal air or fluid collection around the staple ring.
- Treatment: 2 ways, operate or not, depending on severity (contained vs free), degree of contamination, patient condition; start with IV fluid and IV antibiotics:
  1. Non-operative management for contained leak with criteria: 1.1 no generalized peritonitis; 1.2 no sepsis; 1.3 no free leakage on contrast study; 1.4 low output or controlled fistula; 1.5 if abscess or collection at least 3 cm: percutaneous drainage; 1.6 often a case with proximal diversion already or an extraperitoneal (low pelvic) anastomosis.
  2. If surgery is needed (usually free leak, mostly the hole is hard to repair because the tissue is edematous and fragile): 2.1 if lucky and the tissue is not very inflamed, by theory a leak under 1/4 of the circumference, with no thick fecal peritonitis: repair; but most leaks are over 1/4 of circumference: separate the anastomosis and bring out ostomy + Hartmann; 2.2 if abscess or fistula, adequate drainage; 2.3 diffuse peritonitis or hemodynamic instability: open, take down the anastomosis, bring out an ostomy; the surgeon must think and decide fast, to survive.
  - Options: 1. open drainage for generalized peritonitis with much contamination; 2. laparoscopic drainage for someone skilled, little contamination, sure to wash out all pus; 3. percutaneous catheter drainage, anterior abdominal, transgluteal, transrectal, or presacral drainage like extraperitoneal rectal injury.
- Novel techniques: 1. endoluminal vacuum therapy for perianastomotic abscess cavity, especially rectal leak: reported 86% closure of leak; 2. stenting with fully covered stent: reported 19/22 closed.

## Anastomotic Stricture
- By balloon dilation: fill balloon up to 2 cm via colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy; a lumen under 5 mm can be dilated. Figure: stenosis entrance and exit and balloon dilation (skip).

## KUB Complications
- In colorectal and anal surgery KUB complications occur; the one asked often is ureteric injury; for detail read KUB injury in the Trauma 2018 book already given.
### Injury to ureter
- Intraoperative injury is often in cases with much adhesion, e.g. complicated diverticulitis, intestinal endometriosis, recurrent cancer, previous radiation.
- Ureter is often injured at 3 sites: near the origin of the IMA; where the ureter crosses the iliac vessels; and at the lateral ligament where the ureter enters the bladder.
- If suspected (much adhesion, not sure whether the ureter has been cut), give IV methylene blue: blue seen leaking into the abdomen proves the ureter is cut.
- After surgery, drain output like clear fluid (urine) in large amounts, smelling of urine, with high creatinine, shows the ureter is cut; for diagnosis, retrograde pyelography shows the injury site.
- Management depends on the site: 1. sharp cut found during surgery, cut across, no segment loss, can join without tension: primary repair, ureteroureterostomy with a double J stent for 6 weeks, then remove the stent through cystoscopy; 2. if cut but within 5 cm of the bladder or below the pelvic brim: consider ureter length: if it reaches the bladder, ureteroneocystostomy (reimplantation); if reimplant is not possible because the ureter is too short, use a bladder flap as a tube (Boari flap) or psoas hitch (figures B, C); if still not possible because the ureter has extensive injury, join it to the other ureter: transureteroureterostomy (figure D); 3. late case: often hydronephrosis: do not rush: PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy) first, then wait 6-8 weeks for inflammation to subside, then repair.
- Figure: techniques of ureteral repair: A end-to-end ureteroureterostomy; B vesico-psoas hitch; C Boari flap; D transureteroureterostomy (principles: tension-free, watertight, stented, spatulated, mucosa-to-mucosa, absorbable suture, drain).

## Presacral Bleeding
- Bleeding from the presacral venous plexus under the presacral fascia covering the sacrum in front. The plexus consists of lateral and median sacral veins connected to the basivertebral vein (a venous channel in the sacral bone, via the foramina of S3, 4, 5).
- Found about 5%, especially in TME when dissecting posteriorly in the wrong plane (blunt dissection), large mass, or previous RT.
- Bleeding from the presacral plexus is still tolerable, but bleeding from the basivertebral vein is serious: the vein retracts into bone and bleeds profusely.
- Technique to stop bleeding:
  1. Try packing first for about 10-15 min (meanwhile the anesthetist resuscitates); if after taking the pack off the bleeding stops, fine.
  2. If packing fails: use helpers such as a clip, suture, or ligation or take muscle tissue to plug it; suturing is hard because the vein is thin and lies on the sacrum.
  3. Fingertip pressure for a while, or electrocautery or spray coagulation.
  4. A thumbtack (hemorrhagic occlude pin with applicator) driven into the sacrum; disadvantage: chronic sacral pain, and if close to the rectal anastomosis it may leak. Figure: tack and applicator (skip).
  5. If the thumbtack still does not work, plug with rectus muscle or nearby tissue.
  6. If steps 1-5 still fail: use packing: in APR, push gauze in a plastic bag through the perineal wound; the pack stays about 2-4 days then is pulled out gradually through the perineum; if the abdomen is open, pack swabs down and leave in the abdomen for about 48-72 hr then re-open to remove the pack.
  7. Some use a Sengstaken-Blakemore tube or silicone breast implant sizer to compress; opinions differ.
- Do not do: internal iliac artery ligation (no benefit); nor internal iliac vein ligation, because presacral venous pressure rises and bleeds even more.

## Lower Limb Compartment Syndrome
- Rare but serious, especially in long operations in lithotomy and Trendelenburg position: reduced vascular perfusion of the legs, with compartment ischemia and reperfusion injury; the longer the position, the higher the risk. In APR do not forget to look at the patient's legs.

## Prophylactic Bilateral Oophorectomy
- Synchronous ovarian metastases from CRC 1-7%, from sigmoid and rectal cancer, usually premenopausal, by lymphatic and hematogenous spread more than direct spread.
- Prophylactic bilateral oophorectomy in every case does not affect survival; if done, should be postmenopausal; discuss with the patient; premenopausal patients with normal-looking ovaries should not have routine oophorectomy.
- Indications: 1. gross direct extension; 2. abnormal ovary; 3. postmenopause or post RT; 4. HNPCC (high risk of ovarian cancer).

## Surgical Techniques That Have Not Proved Benefit
1. Turnbull's no-touch technique: not proven; handle the cancer gently, no squeezing or scrubbing.
2. High IMA ligation: helps bring the colon down for a low anastomosis without much tension; no proven survival benefit.
3. Local recurrence reduction techniques: proximal and distal bowel ligation, povidone-iodine wash of the lumen, intralesional chemo injection: none proven.
4. Omentoplasty to cover a leak: no proven benefit.
5. Extended abdominal-pelvic lymph node dissection (duodenum to levator ani, around IVC and aorta): no survival gain.
6. Routine drain: does not reduce leak rate.
7. Routine oophorectomy: covered above.
8. Coloshield, silicone tube, SEMS or rectal tube to protect the anastomosis: benefit not conclusive.

## Surveillance (NCCN 2019)
- Look for recurrence or metachronous disease; think cost-effectiveness and necessity.
1. History and PE every 3-6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months to 5 years.
2. CEA every 3-6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months to 5 years; if CEA rises, investigate further.
3. Colonoscopy 1 year after surgery (if not done before surgery, about 6 months after); 5% have another lesion; if normal, repeat at 3 years then every 5 years.
4. Only for transanal excision: proctoscopy (with EUS or MRI with contrast) every 3-6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months to 5 years.
5. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis every 6-12 months for the first 5 years.

## Recurrence of Rectal Cancer
- Found 3-35%; risk depends on staging, margin, surgical technique. 60-80% appear in the first 2 years, 90% within 4 years.
- After recurrence 5-year survival falls to 20-30%; chance of cure only 7%. Recurrence is more at lower rectum than upper rectum.
- Suspicious symptoms: pain, mass, bleeding; recurrence at the surgical wound is rare (under 1%).
- If CEA rises: examine, colonoscopy, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with contrast; if nothing found, PET/CT and high-resolution phased-array pelvic MRI with reconstruction (read by an expert, since recurrence and dense fibrosis can be hard to separate); if no site is found, repeat CT in 3 months or consider PET/CT; if pelvic anastomotic recurrence or metachronous metastases look resectable, treat. Figure: presacral recurrence after APR on CT and PET/CT (skip).
- Treatment: multimodality: 1. first RT + chemo, then en bloc surgery (intraoperative RT if facility); 2. postoperative chemoRT; 3. surgery: recurrent cases adhere to artery, vein, ureter, so it is very difficult.
- Contraindications for surgery: 1. extrapelvic disease (except resectable liver or lung); 2. predicted R2 or circumferential margin, or extensive pelvic side wall involvement; 3. neurologic invasion, e.g. sciatic pain, even unilateral; 4. DVT with swollen leg; 5. bilateral hydronephrosis from bilateral ureteric obstruction (mass must be huge, unless trigone involvement); 6. S2 involvement or higher, bone or nerve; 7. tumor extension through the greater sciatic notch; 8. tumor encasement of common or external iliac vessels; 9. patient not ready: ASA 4 or 5; 10. team not ready (ortho, neurosurgery, radiology, blood bank, ICU).
- The operation usually ends as APR; chance of sphincter-saving is small. Need en bloc: if stuck to the sacrum: sacrectomy; if stuck to bladder neck and prostate: pelvic exenteration.
- To keep recurrence low: 1. transanal excision should be sharp dissection under vision; blunt clamp spreading lets cancer cells spill into a contaminated field; 2. do not rush sphincter-saving if it looks incurable; 3. if TME is not mastered, do not operate mid or low rectum.

## Future Trend of CRC
- Sentinel lymph node study; KRAS and BRAF mutation testing and MSI testing.

## Retrorectal Tumor
- Anatomy: the retrorectal (presacral) space between the upper 2/3 of the rectum and the sacrum, above the levator ani; sides are the lateral ligaments, behind is the presacral fascia (Waldeyer's); it contains embryologic remnants: neuroendoderm, notochord, hindgut. Figure (skip).
- Congenital > acquired; malignant > benign as printed (cancer more in children; in adults more in women than men); cysts outnumber solid tumors, but cancer is found more in solid (60%) than cystic (10%).
- Most common retrorectal tumor: chordoma (from primitive notochord); next neurogenic tumors from peripheral nerve sheath: schwannoma (most common benign), neurofibroma, ganglioneuroma; most common malignant of this group: malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor.
- Cystic group: epidermoid, dermoid, enterogenous, teratoma, meningocele.
- Symptoms: from compression of pelvic structures: back pain, pelvic pain; urinary symptoms most common; PR palpates a mass posteriorly.
- MRI: most sensitive and specific for extension and side wall invasion; chordoma shows multilobulated appearance + calcification + sacral destruction; CT best for cortical bone destruction, MRI better for bone marrow involvement. Figure: MRI chordoma (skip).
- Biopsy: formerly forbidden (bleeding, infection, even sepsis, tumor contamination). Now allowed if imaging shows a solid lesion: benign: excision; cancer: en bloc; Ewing, osteogenic sarcoma or desmoid: neoadjuvant treatment before surgery helps en bloc resection; in unresectable cases tissue helps choose chemo or RT. Do not biopsy cystic lesions: tissue does not change treatment (a parasacrococcygeal approach is needed anyway); for meningocele, biopsy may cause meningitis and death.
- Biopsy route: transperitoneal or parasacral (CT-guided; Figure: ideal zone of needle path, CT of tract); not transrectal or transvaginal (infection risk, and en bloc resection would then need part of rectum and vagina plus the needle tract).
- Treatment: en bloc excision with margin 2 cm or more; a very large mass: 2-staged, abdomen first then posterior sacral approach; beware rupturing the mass, which recurs.
- Sacrum: try not to cut above S3 (incontinence); if the tumor extends higher than S3, sacrectomy is needed, often ending with permanent colostomy; explain before surgery. RT and chemo are adjuvant.
- Figure (guideline, Shackelford 2019 p.2115), as steps: retrorectal mass -> imaging. Purely cystic: meningocele: ligate dura; developmental cyst: low: posterior approach excising cyst +/- coccyx; high: anterior-only or combined approach. Solid or heterogeneous: biopsy: benign: CNS involvement? yes: laminectomy, spare nerve roots and dural connection (structures below S4 may be sacrificed bilaterally when required); no: low: posterior approach excising completely; high: anterior or combined. Primary malignancy: metastatic disease? no: neoadjuvant therapy? no: en bloc oncologic resection; yes: restage, resectable? yes: en bloc resection; no: chemo +/- palliative resection. Metastatic: resectable? no: chemo +/- palliative resection.

## Rectal Leiomyosarcoma
- Smooth muscle tumor at age 50-60, mostly stomach and small bowel; colon and rectum rare; polypoid or submucosal lesion under normal mucosa.
- IHC: SMA and desmin positive; CD117 and CD34 negative.
- Symptoms: bleeding, discomfort, change in bowel habit. Prognosis depends on pathologic grade.
- Treatment: surgery like rectal adenocarcinoma; limitation: radioresistant.

## Anal Cancer
- Anal cancer is less familiar than colon and rectal cancer; this gives the idea.
- Divided into: 1. anal canal cancer; 2. perianal or anal margin cancer.
- Anal canal: anatomically from the anorectal ring to the anal verge, about 4 cm (longer in men); by surgical margin: from the puborectalis sling to the anal verge; by histology 3 parts: below the dentate line squamous epithelium; at the dentate line and up 1-2 cm transitional zone; uppermost columnar epithelium.
- Perianal or anal margin: from the anoderm-anal verge junction (squamous mucocutaneous junction with perianal skin) outward 5-6 cm; cancers here resemble skin cancer.
- Simply: anal canal cancer lies proximal to the dentate line, tumor often not fully visible, usually poorly differentiated, more aggressive; anal margin cancer lies distal, tumor fully visible, usually well differentiated, less aggressive.
- Separating anal canal from rectal cancer: look at the epicenter; if 2 cm or more proximal to the dentate line, it is rectal cancer.
- HPV-associated anal lesions: condyloma acuminatum, Buschke-Lowenstein tumor, anal intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN), SCC; HPV is believed to inhibit tumor suppressor gene proteins.
- Biopsy: a wound or mass that may be cancer; pruritus ani without mass: no early biopsy, treat with hygiene, no scratching or wiping; non-cancer itch is usually a symmetrical lesion around the anus; biopsy when focal, unilateral with persistent pruritus.
- TNM: T stage by size (not thickness as in CRC), same for canal and margin; 8th ed (2017) same as 7th: Tis carcinoma in situ; T1 under 2 cm; T2 2-5 cm; T3 over 5 cm; T4 invading adjacent organ such as vagina, urethra, bladder. Figure: pT1, pT2, pT3 (skip).
- N stage changed in edition 8: anal margin and perianal skin tumors (within 5 cm of the anal margin) are now classified with anal canal carcinoma. T categories unchanged. N0 no regional node metastasis; N1 regional node(s); N1a inguinal, mesorectal and/or internal iliac nodes; N1b external iliac nodes; N1c external iliac and inguinal, mesorectal and/or internal iliac nodes. M categories unchanged (M0 not spread, M1 distant).
- Stage I T1 N0 M0; IIA T2 N0 M0; IIB T3 N0 M0; IIIA T1-2 N1 M0; IIIB T4 N0 M0; IIIC T3-4 N1 M0; IV any T any N M1.

## Anal Canal Cancer
- Residents should know 3 types: 1. SCC; 2. adenocarcinoma; 3. malignant melanoma.
- Lymphatic spread: above the dentate line: superior rectal nodes then IMA group; at the dentate line: internal pudendal, hypogastric and obturator nodes then up to IMA nodes; below the dentate line: superficial inguinal nodes. Figure: lymphatic drainage (skip).
### Anal canal SCC
- SCC in the anal canal is more aggressive than anal margin SCC.
- WHO divides it by histology (large cell, transitional cell, basaloid, cloacogenic); know only that treatment is the same for all; except Paget's (adenocarcinoma type).
- Found often in women; now also in men: more HIV-positive men who have sex with men, so HPV infection becomes anal intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN) and then rapidly invasive SCC.
- Usually diagnosed late (advanced), since the lesion is in the canal and bleeding is thought to be hemorrhoids.
- Clinical features: bleeding most common; then itching, tenesmus, discharge; a finger in the anus can feel an indurated mass; pelvic pain or anovaginal fistula means sphincter involvement.
- Distant metastasis: liver and lung, bone rarely. Lymph node spread: proximal > distal.
- Diagnosis and workup: 1. PE and PR: size, consistency, fixation, pararectal and groin nodes; suspicious node: FNA; PV in women; 2. anoscopy and biopsy; 3. colonoscopy to rule out proximal associated lesion; 4. ERUS: depth of invasion and node metastasis; 5. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or MRI; 6. do not forget HIV test; if positive check CD4; 7. PET/CT not routine.
- Treatment:
  1. Nigro's chemoradiation: treatment of choice for non-metastatic cancer; no need for surgery or colostomy; survival as good as radical surgery such as APR, and quality of life better.
     - 1.1 Start with RT 55 Gy to the anus, pelvis and nodes, groin nodes included except T3 or T4 or size over 5 cm, where the RT dose rises to 66-70 Gy.
     - 1.2 Infusion 5-FU with mitomycin C (doses: look up).
     - Result: complete response T1-T2 90%, T3-T4 50%. Be careful in HIV+ patients: formerly feared that chemoRT would give severe hematologic/skin toxicity up to 50% and local recurrence 50% in 5 years; now this belief is untrue: when antiretroviral therapy brings CD4 up to normal, chemoRT can be given.
  2. Surgery is secondary to chemoRT:
     - 2.1 Local resection: only for early or well differentiated cancer, invasion not beyond the submucosa (T1), mobile, not fixed, size not over 2 cm, groin nodes negative; if after Nigro's chemoRT some cancer remains, do local excision.
     - 2.2 APR: formerly the treatment of choice, now too aggressive (permanent colostomy). Keep APR for: 1. chemoRT failure: persistent disease for more than 3-6 months: complete the chemoRT course, biopsy, still tumor cells; or cannot tolerate chemoRT; 2. sphincter invasion or incontinence already.
  3. Palpable groin node: FNA the node; if cancer, 2 options: 3.1 chemoRT with RT including the groin (results look better than surgery); 3.2 groin dissection followed by post-op RT (second choice).
  4. Groin node negative: prophylactic node dissection is not advised; formerly believed 10-30% harbor spread (others say 10-30% is not much, but Goldberg and Dr. Santhat say nodes under 5 cm may be positive up to 44%; excision is reasonable); to avoid unnecessary groin dissection a sentinel node trial is ongoing as in breast cancer; wait for conclusions.
- Summary: the one best for anal canal SCC is chemoRT; RT alone or chemo alone does not work; drugs 5-FU + mitomycin C; surgery only for specific indications.
### Adenocarcinoma of the anus
- Primary adenocarcinoma of the anus is rare, usually adenocarcinoma associated with fistula in ano; most believed to be spread of rectal adenocarcinoma down to the anus.
- WHO 3 types: 1. rectal type (most common); 2. anal gland type (few); 3. within anorectal fistula, e.g. in Crohn or FIA. Figure: anal adenocarcinoma spreading from rectum (skip).
- Treatment: like rectal cancer: 1. APR with wide local excision of the perineal part + chemoRT is the treatment of choice; 2. wide local excision alone only for a small, well differentiated tumor still in the submucosa (T1); 3. chemoRT does not work well, so a heavy operation is needed; some give chemoRT after to reduce local recurrence.
### Malignant melanoma
- Melanoma occurs in skin first, eye second, anorectal mucosa third.
- Primary GI melanoma: anus 92%, rectum 8%; found above and below the dentate line; if found in the small bowel, suspect metastasis, not a primary.
- F : M = 2 : 1, age 63. At diagnosis usually spread to nodes, lung, liver: prognosis poor, 5-year survival under 10%.
- Symptoms: painless bleeding 54%, mass felt on PR 34%, mass in the anus about 4 cm; pain only 10%; others itching, change in bowel habit, tenesmus, weight loss.
- Diagnosis: 1. proctoscope: pigmented polypoid lesion sometimes looks like thrombosed hemorrhoid; melanoma has ulcer and raised edge; 40% are amelanotic (no pigment), looking like a polyp; 2. biopsy: if the pathologist is not sure, order IHC: S-100, HMB-45, Melan-A; 3. ERUS: depth of invasion and nodes; deeper invasion is worse, spreads fast.
- Metastasis: 1. perirectal, perianal, mesenteric nodes (first); 2. submucosally along the rectum; 3. hematogenous to liver, lung.
- Treatment: 1. wide local excision (WLE) is the treatment of choice (formerly APR for all, but survival is no better than WLE because of metastasis; keeping the anus is better; one best answer is WLE); 2. APR only if WLE impossible, circumferential lesion, or significant sphincter involvement; 3. groin dissection: 20% have positive groin node at first: do groin dissection only if groin node positive; for thickness over 1 cm the role of SLN mapping is not settled as in skin melanoma; 4. adjuvant chemoRT: not useful.

## Perianal Cancer
- Residents should know 6: 1. SCC; 2. BCC; 3. Bowen's disease or AIN; 4. Paget; 5. verrucous; 6. Kaposi.
### Anal margin SCC
- Less common than anal canal SCC; but the most common malignant tumor of the anus; age 62-70; M = F. In women associated gynecologic cancer may exist: look for it.
- Gross: rolled and everted edges with central ulceration (late diagnosis, 30% misdiagnosed, spreads to lymph nodes). Histology: well differentiated keratinizing (differs from anal canal SCC, non-keratinizing).
- Clinical: diagnosed late, mass often over 5 cm; 50% were thought to be something else after symptoms over 24 months; usual presentation: lump, bleeding, pain, discharge, itching; beware a chronic non-healing wound near the anus: biopsy to rule out SCC.
- Spread: to inguinal nodes. Workup same as anal canal SCC.
- Treatment:
  1. WLE: for small T1N0, mobile, invasion not to the dentate line, well differentiated SCC: excise about 1 cm around, frozen section to control margin; positive margin: excise more; if groin nodes positive, groin dissection.
  2. Chemoradiation for cases not suitable for WLE: treat like anal canal SCC; if foci remain after chemoRT, then do WLE. Indications: 2.1 very large mass; 2.2 invasion above the dentate line; 2.3 sphincter invasion.
  3. APR: for failure of the above or fecal incontinence already.
  4. Groin node: if positive, groin dissection; negative nodes: no prophylactic dissection for T2, T3 lesions; Dr. Santhat gives prophylactic RT to the groin when size over 2 cm or T2 and up.
  5. CIS or microinvasion: local excision without wide margin: cure rate 100%.
  6. Mohs surgery: like skin cancer SCC, suits cases likely to fail treatment, e.g. perineural invasion, or morphea or sclerotic pattern on pathology.
- Summary: one best is still WLE. Recurrence: repeat WLE.
### Basal cell carcinoma
- Rare; M > F, age about 60. Bleeding and itching; lesion 1-2 cm, superficial, long-standing, low invasion potential, fairly mobile, rarely metastasizes; 33% also have BCC elsewhere on the skin, so examine the whole body.
- Treatment: 1. WLE with margin 5-10 mm like skin BCC; if the resection leaves much tissue loss, or high recurrence, or morphea/sclerotic pattern, consider Mohs surgery; APR rarely used; 2. other treatments as in skin BCC: RT, curettage, topical 5-FU; 3. recurrence: re-WLE.
### Anal intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN)
- Squamous dysplasia of anal mucosa at the dentate line; formerly Bowen's disease: SCC in situ, cancer cells still above the basement membrane; precursor of invasive SCC; more common in HIV patients; HPV is the carcinogen like cervical cancer.
- Now AIN is classified by thickness of dysplasia into 2 groups:
  1. High grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) = AIN grade 2, 3 or Bowen's disease: grade 2 thickness over 1/3 but not over 2/3; grade 3 over 2/3 of anal mucosa; HSIL likely from HPV 16, 18.
  2. Low grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) = AIN grade 1 (thickness under 1/3): likely HPV 6, 11; but relation to HPV not clear; in HIV it is found more than HPV.
- May have associated malignancy of colon and vulva (5%), especially in young patients; not clear, like Paget.
- Clinical: itching, scratching until burning or spotted bleeding; long-standing: brownish-red plaque-like patches; 25% are misdiagnosed as skin disease (biopsied as skin, and when the pathologist reads AIN, send to a specialist).
- Gross: discrete, non-elevated erythematous, scaly or crusted plaque; with ulceration suspect invasive cancer. Figure (skip).
- Histology: Bowenoid cells, multinucleated giant cells with vacuolization (halo effect), a feature of dysplasia; classified by Bethesda cytology mixed with AIN histology:

| Bethesda (cytology) | AIN (histology) | WHO terminology |
|---|---|---|
| ASC-US, ASC-H | atypia | - |
| LSIL | AIN I | mild dysplasia |
| HSIL | AIN II, AIN III, CIS | moderate dysplasia, severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ |
| Cancer | cancer | cancer |
- Diagnosis: anal cytology (PAP smear), high resolution anoscopy, biopsy; start with PAP smear; if abnormal, anoscopy + biopsy.
- Biopsy: if a lesion on perianal skin, anoderm or vulva is seen, skin biopsy right away; if unsure where to biopsy, paint acetic acid on the lesion (turns white) or Lugol's solution (lesion stays unstained, while normal glycogen-containing cells turn dark brown or black).
- When dysplasia is found, management follows a guideline (figure): cytology normal: repeat in 12 months (HIV+) or 2-3 years (HIV-); ASC-US, LSIL, HSIL or ASC-H: anoscopy with biopsy: no lesion seen: back to repeat cytology; AIN I: follow-up in 6 months or treat if minimal potential for morbidity; AIN II or III: treat.
- Low grade AIN (AIN 1): little malignant potential and may regress; observe and follow up with anal PAP smear every 6-12 months.
- Treatment options of high grade AIN (AIN 2, 3):
  1. Imiquimod (Aldara) 5% cream applied every night on Mon, Wed, Fri for 16 weeks: topical immune modulator with antiviral and antitumor activity; also treats BCC and SCC.
  2. Topical 5% 5-FU for 15 weeks.
  3. Cautery ablation: visualize the margin with acetic acid painting first; do not burn deep, since the wound will not heal. Advantage: convenient (every OR has electrocautery, unlike laser or cryo available only in some places). Disadvantage: no tissue diagnosis, so invasive SCC (found together in about 2%) may be overlooked; burning the anal canal circumferentially gives anal stricture.
  4. Excision (WLE): now the trend is back to surgery as the main treatment: WLE with 5 mm margin under frozen section control, then follow up with anoscopy every 6 months. Complications of WLE: anal stricture, ectropion, fecal incontinence.
- Incidental finding of AIN after hemorrhoidectomy: low grade: follow up; high grade: go back to the OR for WLE until a free margin is obtained.

### Perianal Paget's disease
- Adenocarcinoma in situ from apocrine glands, mostly from the duct of a sweat gland. Extramammary Paget occurs at axilla, thigh, buttock and anogenital region (labia, perineum, penis, scrotum, groin, pubic area).
- Found in women about as often as men, age about 60; not related to HPV.
- Clinical: old people 60-70 years; 1/3 of cases diagnosed late; very itchy, some burning pain and bleeding.
- Gross: slowly enlarging erythematous eczema with sharply demarcated perianal skin rash and scaling from scratching; thick, red, well-bordered plaque: think of Paget.
- Diagnosis: 1. biopsy at the thick area: typical Paget cells: large round cells with pale cytoplasm and large nucleus lying at the edge, above the basal layer of the epidermis; IHC separates it from Paget of colorectal origin; 2. visceral malignancy, especially GI, is associated in up to 50%, so evaluate the GI tract + KUB completely: colonoscopy, upper endoscopy, PR for prostate.
- Differential: causes of pruritus ani, Bowen, SCC.
- Treatment: 1. no invasive cancer: WLE with at least 1 cm margin; for a free margin do mapping + intraoperative frozen section; 2. recurrence: re-excision; 3. invasive cancer: poor prognosis like adenocarcinoma, so APR; 4. poor-risk patient who would die on the table: refer for chemoRT.

### Verrucous carcinoma
- Many names without universal consensus: Buschke-Lowenstein tumor; some believe it is an aggressive form of condyloma acuminata, called giant condyloma acuminata; size is not defined (anal condyloma generally is not over 1-1.5 cm).
- Associated with HPV types 6 and 11; found in men about as often as women.
- Typical: mass over 8 cm, slow growing, painful; wart-like cauliflower but soft; around perianal skin, anal canal and distal rectum; malignant transformation 56%; a giant condyloma takes about 5 years to become cancer. Figure (skip).
- Grossly sometimes hard to separate from SCC: biopsy if suspicious.
- Extension: erosion or pressure necrosis to deep tissue with infection, bleeding, multiple sinus tracts and fistula; metastasis uncommon; slow growth but frequent local destruction.
- Histology: like condyloma acuminata.
- Treatment: few cases and recurrence up to 66%, so treatment is debated: 1. WLE with 1 cm margin under frozen section control: most accepted, treatment of choice; 2. with SCC: chemoRT helps the mass regress; 3. APR for sphincter involvement or incontinence; 4. unfit patient: imiquimod cream for about 6 weeks first, then CO2 laser ablation of residual mass under local anesthesia, then imiquimod another 6 weeks to prevent recurrence; 5. podophyllin: 60% responds but high recurrence; do not use if already cancer.

### Kaposi's sarcoma
- Very rare at perianal skin; think of AIDS; radiosensitive cancer. Main treatment RT; if generalized disease, RT + chemo. Figure (skip).

## Summary of Anal Cancer Treatment
1. If you know nothing, answer WLE + mapping or frozen section control: right about 90%.
2. APR for adenocarcinoma, or anal cancer with sphincter invasion with incontinence, or sarcomas (leiomyosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, anaplastic sarcoma).
3. Chemoradiation for anal canal SCC.
4. No prophylactic groin dissection.
5. SLN role awaits future conclusions.

## Screening of Anal Cancer Precursors
- Consider in high-risk groups: 1. homosexual men; 2. HIV women who use the anus like these men; 3. women with cervical or vulvar cancer.
- Technique: 1. cytology: screening test of choice: brush the anal canal with NSS, smear on glass slide like a PAP smear; if abnormal cells go to 2; 2. anoscopic assessment: paint the anal mucosa with 3-5% acetic acid or Lugol's solution; at a suspicious area, the lesion lacks glycogen and does not stain, biopsy there; or if on scoping you see vascular punctation (high grade dysplasia), white plaque (leukoplakia), papillomatosis or irregular mucosa, biopsy at once.

## Anal Condyloma
- In texts also condyloma acuminata; common name "cock's comb" warts. Caused by HPV, a DNA virus: HPV 6 and 11 with warts; HPV 16 and 18 with AIN and SCC.
- HPV causes warts only; no proctitis. Painful proctitis has other causes: gonorrhea, chlamydia, lymphogranuloma venereum subtypes, syphilis, herpes simplex.
- Classic: small ones itch slightly; large ones are unsanitary, bleed, moist, with soiling all the time.
- Sometimes spontaneous regression alternating with recurrence: warts often disappear when the body is strong (exercise, etc.) but come back when stressed or immunocompromised (AIDS, cancer).
- Recurrence also occurs because HPV hides inside the anal canal: in HIV patients warts hide in the canal up to 60%; in non-HIV about 15%; on the thighs, groin, and vagina in women.
- Some lesions are too small to see: paint 3% acetic acid: warts with much keratin turn white (aceto-white change).
- Diagnosis: examine the perianal skin closely with DRE and anoscopy; in women also PV; biopsy not necessary except if suspicious for cancer (large or fast growing).
- Follow up often since treatment takes time and may be needed several times. Do not forget to test HIV. Beware giant condyloma: cancer found together in up to 50%: biopsy.
- Treatment:
  1. Small size: topical: 1.1 podophyllin or bichloracetic acid: cheap, easy; recurrence up to 60%; cannot be used in the anal canal; prolonged use may mislead the pathologist into reading dysplasia; 1.2 5% imiquimod (Aldara) cream: immunomodulator, apply 3 times a week until warts regress; recurrence 20-30% after stopping; more expensive; may burn skin, so not for the canal.
  2. Large or numerous lesions: 2.1 electrocoagulation: very effective; block (spinal), widen the anus, then burn or cut with cautery in pieces; if many, do part at a time; follow up every 2 weeks, treat small remnants or new ones; in non-HIV usually 2 sessions; beware HPV DNA in the smoke; 2.2 laser; 2.3 cryotherapy: weaker than cautery, cannot be used in the canal, but needs no anesthesia; 2.4 surgical excision for lesions over 1 cm.
  - When burning with cautery or laser, beware HPV in the plume; send tissue to the pathologist; if cancer, WLE.
  - HPV vaccine is accepted for cervix and vagina, but for anal condyloma still a trial.

## Obstructive Defecation Syndrome
- A group of symptoms of abnormal defecation: straining, difficulty, incomplete evacuation; some use laxatives, finger to support the vagina or digitate stool out, or prolonged toilet sitting.
- Constipation by Rome III: difficult defecation for over 6 months with at least 2 of the following for at least 3 months: 1. fewer than 3 bowel movements per week; 2. straining over 25% of defecations; 3. lumpy or hard stool over 25%; 4. feeling of incomplete evacuation over 25%; 5. digital maneuver over 25% of defecations.
- In 2017 Rome IV came out, adding tests, manometry and EMG, to classify defecation disorders better.
- The Cleveland Clinic Florida Constipation Score also exists to grade severity.
- Causes: 1. functional abnormality of the anorectum; 2. anatomical abnormality: rectal prolapse, rectal intussusception, rectocele, solitary rectal ulcer syndrome.
- Mechanism of defecation depends on muscle squeezing, coordinated function of anorectum and nervous system.
- Bristol stool chart: type 1 separate hard lumps: severe constipation; type 2 lumpy sausage: mild constipation; type 3 sausage with cracks: normal; type 4 smooth soft sausage or snake: normal; type 5 soft blobs with clear-cut edges: lacking fibre; type 6 mushy with ragged edges: mild diarrhea; type 7 liquid, no solid pieces: severe diarrhea.
- Approach to chronic constipation: 1. history and exam, exclude medical causes; 2. colonoscopy to rule out tumor; 3. then laxatives with high fiber diet; if no improvement, investigate: colonic transit study, defecography, anal EMG, and treat by cause: 3.1 colonic inertia: subtotal colectomy + ileorectal anastomosis; 3.2 obstructed defecation: if rectal prolapse, prolapse repair; if none, biofeedback / sacral nerve stimulation.

## Colonic Inertia
- The colon works less than normal, tired or not working, from many causes (diet, drugs such as analgesics, hypothyroidism), severe functional constipation without outlet obstruction.
- Study shows delayed transit and EMG finds no colonic motor activity. Colonic transit scan at 24, 48, 72 hr: normal: by 72 hr the hot spot has passed out through the anus; colonic inertia: at 72 hr the hot spot is still at the splenic flexure. Figure (skip).
- Treatment: 1. non-operative: diet, drugs, biofeedback, enema: often not very effective; for poor-risk patients; 2. surgery: standard treatment, megacolon or not: subtotal colectomy with ileosigmoid anastomosis (best results).
- Figure (algorithm, intractable constipation): colonic transit time: normal: therapeutic trial; abnormal defecation, straining: tests of pelvic floor function and defecation proctography; abnormal: psychiatric evaluation and tests of pelvic floor function/defecation proctography: anatomical rectal defect: rectal surgery; normal: therapeutic trial, if no response total colectomy and ileorectostomy; pelvic floor dysfunction: biofeedback.

## Rectal Prolapse
- 2 kinds: internal prolapse (intussusception) and external prolapse: part or the entire thickness of the rectum protrudes through the anal canal, seen at the anal orifice. Some classify into 3 by adding rectoanal intussusception with leading point inside the anorectal ring.
- Complete prolapse = visible outside the anus on straining; occult or internal prolapse = still inside the anus, not protruding.
- Etiology: still unknown, mostly associated with anatomic abnormalities (known from exams):
  1. Rectal intussusception at 6-8 cm above the anal verge, near the cul-de-sac.
  2. Sliding hernia of the deep cul-de-sac (pouch of Douglas, rectovesical pouch) pushing down into the pelvis, so rectum and sigmoid slide down and out.
  3. Lax ligaments holding the rectum, levator ani or anal sphincter.
  4. Anal obstruction with straining more than usual: constipated patients, or prolapsed hemorrhoid, prolapse easily.
  5. Sacrum fairly straight, not curved, so rectum and anal canal form a straight tube.
  6. Redundancy of sigmoid and rectum.
  7. Diseases making pelvic muscle paralysis: spinal cord injury, cauda equina lesion, injury to the anus (e.g. after FIA surgery).
  8. Abnormal pelvic floor descent (descending perineum syndrome) causing prolapse of pelvic organs: cystocele, rectocele, uterine prolapse.
- Women : men = 6 : 1; women are often 70 years old, but men can be any age.
- 33% of women with prolapse never had children; 15% have uterine prolapse through the vagina; having many children is not a predisposing factor.
- Also found together with rectocele, cystocele, vaginal prolapse.
- Adults usually have rectal prolapse (full thickness); in children what protrudes is often only the rectal mucosa (mucosal prolapse). Figure: mucosal prolapse (skip).
- Symptoms: mass in the anus, bleeding, mucus discharge; while coughing or straining the mass protrudes; long-standing, the pudendal or perineal nerve is stretched until it loses function, causing anal and/or urinary incontinence. Do not examine the patient in prone jackknife position, since that cannot show how far it protrudes.
- Differential diagnosis: prolapsed hemorrhoid: rectal prolapse shows concentric rings, mucosal folds of rectal mucosa, circumferential full-thickness bowel; prolapsed hemorrhoid has grooves between purple hemorrhoid heads (radiating sulci), petal-like. Figure: A rectal prolapse, B prolapsed hemorrhoid.
- Investigation: 1. colonoscopy or BE to rule out a mass leading point and check redundancy of the rectosigmoid; 2. if severe constipation and total colonic inertia suspected: colonic transit study; 3. if rectal intussusception suspected with no visible protrusion but a feeling of straining, incomplete evacuation or bloody stool: scope may find solitary rectal ulcer syndrome (SRUS); these patients should have cinedefecography; 4. assess anal sphincter in every case; 5. MR defecography (dynamic MRI): the best investigation for diagnosis and pelvic floor descent (pelvic organ prolapse).
- Small, asymptomatic prolapse with few symptoms and no life disturbance: no surgery; advise avoiding constipation, treat symptoms such as itching; operate in symptomatic cases.
- Management: 1. acute prolapse without strangulation: manual reduction first, may need sedation; 2. surgery is the main treatment; 3. medical treatment such as laxatives; 4. Kegel exercise (periodic voluntary contraction of pelvic muscle) strengthens pelvic muscles to reduce prolapse.
- Surgical treatment: principle: cut away a redundant bowel or plicate it, with fixation of the rectum to the sacrum; 2 approaches: transabdominal and transperineal; choose by patient condition: strong, low risk: abdomen (open or lap); high risk: perineal approach (results lose to abdominal).
1. Transabdominal approach: formerly prosthesis (Teflon, Marlex, Gore-Tex mesh) to fix the rectum to the sacrum: anterior sling = Ripstein operation; posterior sling with Ivalon polyvinyl sponge = Wells operation; no longer popular since much strangulation, mesh erosion, chronic pain, recurrence up to 25%. Figure: Ripstein and Wells (skip).
   - Popular now: resection with fixation: sigmoidectomy with rectopexy (Frykman-Goldberg operation); and ventral mesh rectopexy.
   - Frykman-Goldberg: open or lap; very good results with low recurrence. Technique: mobilize the posterior rectum down to the coccyx without cutting the lateral ligament (to reduce nerve injury), pull the rectum up and suture it to the sacral promontory, do sigmoidectomy like LAR to remove redundant sigmoid, anastomose handsewn or stapled; do not ligate the IMA (if recurrence, re-operation Altemeier would be difficult because lower blood supply is reduced; leak possible); then suture the lateral stalks to the presacral fascia at S2-S3, 2-3 stitches each side. For good-risk patients (strong, tolerates anesthesia): recurrence under 10%, little obstructed defecation, fixes redundancy of rectosigmoid. Disadvantages: needs abdominal anesthesia, anastomotic leak risk, pelvic dissection (impotence risk), not for young men; may give LAR syndrome (frequent soft stool, incomplete) which takes 6-12 months to settle. Figure (skip).
   - Laparoscopic resection + rectopexy (since 1992): fairly good; shorter hospital stay but 50 min longer than open. Recurrence: open abdominal resection + rectopexy under 5-10% with follow-up over 5 years; lap rectopexy 4.8% at 3 years (short follow-up); lap resection + rectopexy 2.5% with follow-up over 5 years (the author would choose this for one best).
   - Laparoscopic ventral rectopexy: mobilize only the anterior rectum without lateral or posterior dissection; prolene mesh sutured: upper end fixed to the sacral promontory, lower end fixed to the leading point of the prolapse at the anterior rectal wall about 6-8 cm above the anus, with the mesh also sutured to the posterior vagina to strengthen the rectovaginal septum and prevent rectocele; then close peritoneum over the mesh to prevent erosion. Can prevent rectal intussusception; recurrence only 2.4%, close to Frykman-Goldberg; advantage: little dissection, no bowel resection, little incontinence; problem: mesh erosion about 2% (18 of 2,203 cases needed mesh removal, sometimes with bowel resection); wait for long-term RCT. Figure (skip).
2. Transperineal approach: know 3 ways: Altemeier, Delorme and Thiersch; compared with abdominal: lower mortality (no heavy anesthesia, spinal block or even local), disadvantage: higher recurrence.
   - Selection criteria: 1. high-risk patient with many comorbidities; 2. elderly in nursing home, Alzheimer; 3. young men whose bodies are still wanted: abdominal rectosigmoid resection while mobilizing the sigmoid and upper rectum may cut autonomic nerves (erection and ejaculation lost): so young men should choose the perineal way.
   - Altemeier operation (transperineal rectosigmoidectomy): perineal full-thickness rectosigmoidectomy: pull the rectum down as much as possible, cut it, suture anastomosis about 1.5 cm above the dentate line; if incontinence, add levatoplasty. Among perineal approaches this has better results: recurrence 24% (lower than Delorme 31%); suits men (no impotence); disadvantages: spinal block, anastomotic leak (from cutting too much colon so the anastomosis is tense). Selection criteria: 1. rectal prolapse protruding over 6 cm; 2. cannot be reduced, gangrene (black) or ulceration of the prolapse; 3. with incontinence. Figure (skip).
   - Delorme operation (mucosal proctectomy with muscle plication): strip only the rectal mucosa, starting about 1 cm below the dentate line, lifting the mucosa as high as possible, cut the mucosa out, then suture the muscular layers proximal and distal together (plication). Advantages: can be done under local; suits poor-risk patients with prolapse under 6 cm (if you can poke the back, spinal block is more comfortable than local); less anastomotic complication and impotence. Disadvantages: higher recurrence than Altemeier because only mucosa is removed and the pelvic floor problem is not fixed, the sigmoid is not removed so it is still redundant. Figure (skip).
   - Anal encirclement (Thiersch operation): silicone rubber or mesh placed in the subcutaneous tissue around the anus, 2 cm from the anal verge. Very simple but not good: recurrence up to 75%; if too tight, patient cannot pass stool, may have necrosis or ulcer; easy under local, suits the highest-risk patients, e.g. on tube in ICU with prolapse. Summary: no need to use it.
### Summary of choosing operation
1. Incarceration: manual reduction (sugar or table-sugar sprinkled on the prolapse is said to reduce swelling; or swab soaked in water pushed in bit by bit), then prepare for elective repair; if cannot reduce, Altemeier.
2. Good risk: transabdominal approach. 3. Poor risk: transperineal: 3.1 if spinal block tolerated: Altemeier; 3.2 if back cannot be poked: inject local then Delorme.
4. With incontinence: good risk: Frykman-Goldberg; poor risk + incontinence: Altemeier + levatoplasty.
5. Strangulated: Altemeier (transperineal rectosigmoidectomy). 6. With ulceration: Altemeier.
7. Good risk and uterine prolapse together: abdominal Frykman-Goldberg with TAH + BSO, or lap ventral rectopexy with sacrocolpopexy.
8. Young male afraid of impotence: transperineal approach; even young women should be advised the perineal way (Cameron 2011 p.168).
9. Total colonic inertia: subtotal colectomy + rectopexy.
10. Recurrence: consider what was done first and comorbidities: 10.1 previous abdominal operation: good risk: re-enter the abdomen and repeat or change to a new procedure; avoid the perineal approach after transabdominal resection + rectopexy (proximal and distal rectal blood supply both tied; perineal approach may cause rectal ischemia); poor risk: Delorme; 10.2 previous transperineal: next also transperineal to avoid disturbing the remaining proximal blood supply; if not possible, lap ventral rectopexy.
- Figure (guideline for recurrent rectal prolapse, Cameron 12e 2017 p.198), as steps: recurrent rectal prolapse -> anorectal physiology/colonoscopy -> initial operative procedure: perineal rectosigmoidectomy: significant comorbidities: repeat perineal rectosigmoidectomy; none: rectopexy without resection. Delorme: significant comorbidities: repeat Delorme or perineal rectosigmoidectomy; none: rectopexy with or without resection. Rectopexy with resection: significant comorbidities: Delorme; none: repeat rectopexy without resection. Rectopexy without resection: significant comorbidities: perineal rectosigmoidectomy; none: repeat rectopexy with or without resection.

## Rectal Intussusception
- Internal rectal prolapse: sigmoid and upper rectum slide down into the rectum or anal canal temporarily but do not come out past the anal verge (hidden inside); believed from abnormal straining for a long time, causing outlet obstruction with feeling of difficult or incomplete evacuation.
- With heavy straining day and night, the hidden prolapse may come out as external rectal prolapse in about 2%, and cause solitary rectal ulcer syndrome (SRUS).
- Symptoms: 50% none; 50% have symptoms: difficult, incomplete evacuation; suspect if scope finds SRUS.
- Diagnosis: cinedefecography: barium into the rectum, patient sits and strains while the doctor watches under fluoroscopy; if the upper rectum slides down to the lower rectum, the disease is real. Figure (skip).
- Treatment: 1. like rectal prolapse, start conservative: high fiber, water, laxatives, train defecation by biofeedback so evacuation is easy without much straining. 2. If conservative fails, or the intussusception descends below the puborectalis sling, or is associated with SRUS, rectocele or fecal incontinence, surgery is needed:
  - 2.1 Abdominal approach: reduction + rectopexy or laparoscopic rectopexy; fairly good results, obstructive defecation improves in 80% (no more laxatives).
  - 2.2 Transanal: stapled transanal rectal resection (STARR procedure): less invasive than the abdominal approach: cut out the internal prolapse in the rectum, correct the rectocele (if present), and re-establish continuity of the rectal wall; the author calls STARR the treatment of choice when the patient has little discomfort: symptoms of constipation decrease, defecation improves, quality of life better. Delorme gives acceptable results. Figure: STARR (skip).

## Solitary Rectal Ulcer Syndrome (SRUS)
- A syndrome of abnormal severe defecatory dysfunction; real cause unclear; some believe from bad habit of straining too long until the rectal mucosa swells, or trauma from repeated straining and ischemia of the anterior rectal wall; in the past patients were said to like poking the anus or using Fleet enemas because they could not evacuate.
- Anal straining may come from anal outlet obstruction from rectal prolapse or spastic pelvic floor syndrome.
- Diagnosis is difficult because symptoms and signs resemble other proctologic diseases: need high index of suspicion from both surgeon and pathologist.
- Key: symptoms of pain, difficult incomplete evacuation, mucus-bloody stool in a young woman who often sits and strains; scope finds an anterior rectal ulcer 4-12 cm from the anal verge; some cases have no ulcer but a polypoid lesion protruding from the rectum with typical histology.
- Differential: 1. CRC (scope and biopsy every case); 2. endometriosis; 3. IBD (Crohn, UC); 4. ischemic colitis; 5. stercoral ulcer in fecal impaction.
- Investigation: 1. proctoscopy, sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy: typical ulcer; biopsy the ulcer or mass; 2. histology; 3. after diagnosis, imaging: defecography or dynamic MRI to find associated spastic pelvic disorder, rectal intussusceptions, internal or external mucosal prolapse: treat together.
- Ulcer: single or multiple; wound or nodular polypoid lesion; biopsy to rule out malignancy; classic ulcer at the anterior rectal wall about 4-12 cm from the anal verge. Figure: endoscopy (skip).
- Typical histology: 1. fibromuscular obliteration in the lamina propria; 2. thickened, disarranged muscularis mucosae muscle; 3. in some cases mucosal gland displacement into the submucosa, surrounded by colonic mucosa: glandular crypt abnormality called colitis cystica profunda (CCP).
- Management: since rare, no standard formula; nonsurgical treatment is the answer.
- Conservative treatment: behavior modification: 1. dietary management: high fiber diet plus intermittent bulk-forming agent as needed; 2. defecation training: reduce heavy straining; teach how to sit and relax: gentle squeeze, no loud noise; 3. reassurance, including antianxiety drugs; 4. biofeedback: retraining defecation to fix uncoordinated rectal-anal function from paradoxical contraction of puborectalis and external sphincter, so that they relax and reduce further trauma to the anterior rectal mucosa; 5. some use topical anti-inflammatory drugs (ASA, mesalamine); 6. if the floor has much spasm, botox injection may help.
- Surgical treatment: generally not very effective except SRUS with rectal prolapse: surgery for the prolapse helps heal the ulcer up to 80%; if no prolapse, LAR is feasible. Indications: 1. complete rectal prolapse; 2. stricture causing outlet obstruction; 3. bleeding needing frequent transfusion. Local ulcer excision is not useful (does not fix the cause); APR is too much; colostomy has a place for patients who cannot pass stool, as a temporary solution.
- Summary: SRUS: answer conservative first.

## Colitis Cystica Profunda (CCP)
1. Benign lesion, not precancerous. 2. Associated with rectal prolapse and SRUS. 3. Not associated with IBD. 4. Histology: mucus-filled glands displaced from mucosa into the submucosa. 5. At scope, at the SRUS site, the CCP part is a nodule or mass, reddish, perhaps with bloody mucus on the surface. 6. Treatment same as SRUS: avoid straining, high fiber diet, laxatives.

## Rectocele
- Herniation of the anterior rectal wall pushing into the vagina, from a lax rectovaginal septum; found after trauma, obstetric injury or chronic straining; more in women with many children, postmenopausal low estrogen, than in women with no children. Figure (skip).
- Symptoms: difficulty evacuating: stool does not come out well because some stool enters the pouch; some feel pain, need to strain hard; if under 1 cm usually no symptoms; if over 2 cm, often incomplete evacuation; some must use a finger around the anus or insert a finger into the vagina and push the bulge back toward the anus to defecate; PR anteriorly finds a lax rectovaginal septum.
- Investigation: 1. defecography: size of rectocele and whether contrast is retained after evacuation; 2. colonoscopy: causes of difficult defecation, since a tumor may coexist; 3. dynamic MRI: size and outlet obstruction; in the USA advised in every case before surgery; 4. with incontinence, EAUS.
- 80% of rectocele are under 2 cm; if the patient has no symptoms from it, most say do not treat.
- Treatment: 1. non-operative first: high fiber, bulk forming, laxatives; pelvic dyssynergia needs defecation retraining by biofeedback so pelvic floor muscles relax and evacuation is easier. 2. Surgical: indications: 2.1 size over 3 cm on sagittal defecography; 2.2 after defecogram the film shows much contrast retained in the rectocele; 2.3 chronic recurrent obstructed defecation; 2.4 needs to press the perineum or posterior vaginal wall with a finger for complete evacuation.
  - Because rectocele arises from laxity of endopelvic fascia supporting the rectovaginal septum, so the anterior rectal wall bulges into the posterior vaginal wall, the principle is to strengthen the rectovaginal septum.
  - Technique: transvaginal repair, transanal repair (colorectal surgeons prefer), or transperineal repair: suture the lax rectovaginal septum together, or place mesh; in a patient with obstructed defecation and rectal intussusception too, do STARR; the rest is for the gynecologists (the author says choose freely).
  - Figure: transvaginal repair with mesh over the rectum anchored to sacrospinous ligament (A-D, skip).

## Fecal Incontinence
- Or anal incontinence: unable, from age 4 and up, to hold solid stool; some also cannot hold liquid stool and flatus; symptoms for over 1 month.
- Incidence 10-13 per 1,000 in people over 65.
- Mechanism of continence: when stool or flatus reaches the anal canal at the dentate line, the receptor senses whether it is flatus, stool or liquid; if not time to defecate, the pudendal nerve causes puborectalis contraction (anorectal angle narrows) and external sphincter contraction (anal canal narrows); if the stool is not much or not urgent, we remain continent.
- Etiology:
  1. Anatomical: childbirth is the most common cause of incontinence in healthy women (each delivery has occult sphincter injury 10-35%); after FIA surgery; rectovaginal fistula; rectal prolapse; anorectal trauma; congenital anomaly (imperforate anus); rectal tumor (stretching pudendal nerve); descending perineum syndrome.
  2. Neurologic: 2.1 CNS: brain tumor, mental retardation, stroke, dementia, congenital (meningocele); 2.2 spinal cord: multiple sclerosis, tabes dorsalis; 2.3 peripheral nerve: DM, cauda equina lesion, polyneuropathy, pudendal neuropathy, toxic or traumatic neuropathy.
  3. Muscular: 3.1 skeletal muscle disease: myopathy, dystrophy, myasthenia gravis; 3.2 smooth muscle dysfunction: scleroderma, rectal ischemia, fecal impaction, abnormal rectal compliance.
  4. Miscellaneous: inflammation (Crohn, post RT); IBS; severe diarrhea from infection or short bowel or long laxative use; hypothyroidism; sedative drugs; aging with abnormal pelvic floor.
  5. Idiopathic.
- When the 3rd sacral nerve is cut on both sides incontinence occurs; one side alone does not.
- Trauma from childbirth, pelvic fracture, complex FIA surgery risks sphincter injury.
- Classification: 1. true incontinence: cannot hold solid stool, usually external or internal sphincter abnormality; 2. partial incontinence: cannot hold liquid stool and flatus; 3. overflow incontinence: solid stool impacted in rectum until rectal pressure exceeds the anal sphincter, so liquid stool seeps around the solid stool.
- Approach: 1. history, examination, bowel history, frequency, urgency, drugs, Bristol stool scale, PR to find the cause; 2. severity: Cleveland Clinic fecal incontinence score: at worst cannot hold flatus or stool, or must wear a pad all the time; 3. investigation and plan treatment.
- If unsure, run saline 100 cc into the rectum: a normal person can hold it.
- Differentiate from anal soiling or pruritus, mucosal prolapse and rectal prolapse.
- Investigation: to separate whether sphincter is torn, nervous system paralyzed, or muscles weak: 1. nerve and muscle tests: EMG, anorectal manometry, PNTML (pudendal nerve terminal motor latency); 2. sphincter: EAUS shows where the tear is; MRI if EAUS is unclear; 3. cause: colonoscopy to exclude neoplasm or inflammation (Crohn); defecography for rectal intussusception.
- Not everything must be investigated: current gold standard for sphincter muscle injury is EAUS; anal physiologic tests are used only about 10%. Figure: EAUS left normal, right defect anterior (skip).
- Guideline (Sabiston), as steps: clinical evaluation with DRE -> treat underlying disease and manage bowel disturbances; treat local anorectal problems -> persistent symptoms -> anal manometry (normal pressures; weak pressures: consider anal imaging, EMG if appropriate), rectal sensation (reduced), rectal balloon expulsion (abnormal: evacuation disorder) -> pelvic floor retraining -> persistent symptoms: consider sphincteroplasty or sacral nerve stimulation if appropriate.

### Management of fecal incontinence
1. Non-operative treatment (mild incontinence):
   - 1.1 Diet: high fiber, maybe bulk-forming agent; avoid foods causing loose stool or fecal impaction (to prevent overflow).
   - 1.2 Bowel management: train one defecation a day; may need enema to wash stool out.
   - 1.3 If too frequent stools: antidiarrheal such as loperamide occasionally.
   - 1.4 Biofeedback or pelvic floor retraining: suits neurogenic causes that still have some anal sphincter contraction; even with partial external sphincter defect, if rectal sensation remains, biofeedback works. Method: a balloon with pressure sensor in the rectum; inflate it to increase pressure and have the patient squeeze and relax external sphincter and pelvic muscles; after 3-5 weeks of training incontinence decreases; average improvement 65-85%; painless, safe, cheap compared with surgery; also used to supplement after surgery. Figure: manometry before and after squeeze training: stop straining, stronger squeeze, longer duration (skip).
   - 1.5 Anal plug: a device blocking the anus, opened when stool comes; problem: patients often cannot tolerate it.
   - 1.6 Injectable bulking agent (silicone, collagen, dextranomer-hyaluronic acid) into the intersphincteric space to strengthen the internal anal sphincter.
2. Operative treatment:
   - 2.1 If pelvic floor abnormality: levatoplasty; if from rectal prolapse, treat the prolapse; leave it to the proctologists to read up.
   - 2.2 If the sphincter was torn after childbirth: wait about 3-6 months for inflammation to subside, then sphincteroplasty: curved incision parallel to the perianal sphincter edge, dissect the sphincter anteriorly and laterally from the vagina, preserve muscle fibers as much as possible, then overlapping repair or plication, tight enough that one finger fits into the anus; after repair do EAUS to see if a defect remains (a remaining defect may give incontinence again, especially at older age, when the repair fails). Elderly patients with sphincter tear after delivery usually do not do well since pelvic floor weakness coexists. Figure: overlapping sphincteroplasty (skip). After sphincteroplasty, if stool is too hard to pass, the patient can be dilated.
   - 2.3 If sphincter function is lost or total loss and the anus is unusable: repair will not work: construct a neosphincter using gracilis or gluteus muscle wrapped around the anal canal (dynamic graciloplasty with pulse generator and electrodes). Figure (skip).
   - 2.4 Patient completely deteriorated, bed-ridden, stool soiling, hard to care for: sigmoid loop colostomy is easier to care for.
   - 2.5 Artificial bowel sphincter: device implanted around the anal canal: inflatable cuff with a control pump in the scrotum or labia; when the patient wants to defecate, press the pump to deflate the cuff, opening a channel. Figure (skip).
   - 2.6 New: magnetic beads ring (Fenix fecal incontinence system) around the anus, like the Linx system in GERD; used in patients failing all treatments; in the first year fecal incontinence reduced about 50%; long-term results awaited. Figure: implanted around the anal canal to maintain closure; expands to allow stool passage, then reapproximates (skip).
- Summary of treatment: 1. elderly from fecal impaction: high fiber with laxative; 2. diarrhea or loose stool: antidiarrheal such as loperamide; 3. incomplete sphincter injury and partial nerve loss: biofeedback; 4. sphincter injury only, nerve control still good: repair, plasty or plication; 5. muscle and nerve controlling the sphincter lost completely: neosphincter or artificial sphincter.
- If you know nothing (exam, incontinence): answer biofeedback: probability right about 75%.
- Figure: less or minimally invasive procedures: A bulking agent injection; B sacral nerve electrical stimulator; C radiofrequency probe producing thermal lesions; invasive surgical procedures: D gracilis muscle with electrical stimulator; E reservoir, inflatable cuff, pump; F magnetic anal sphincter device.

## Hemorrhoid
- Cause: truly unknown; formerly those with hemorrhoids were associated with heavy straining, long sitters, or anyone whose anal canal muscles had weakened with age; suspensory ligament of the anal canal wall also lax; at present we believe Thomson's theory: hemorrhoids arise from sliding anal lining (sliding anal cushion theory).
- Pathophysiology: normally the anal canal has anal cushions as a lining pad, consisting of mucosa, submucosa, fibrovascular tissue and blood vessels; the cushions are at 3, 7 and 11 o'clock, helping close the anal canal tightly, reinforcing the squeezing muscle; normally above the dentate line; the cushions help hold liquid stool or flatus so it does not leak, especially during coughing, sneezing, and sleep. Figure: conjoined longitudinal muscle, hemorrhoidal venous plexus, muscularis mucosae, Treitz muscle (skip).
- The cushion is held by smooth muscle called Treitz's muscle and part of the conjoined suspensory ligament (Parks' fibers); if muscle and Parks' fibers relax or tear with age or heavy straining, the cushion slides down (sliding anal cushion theory), producing congestion of the hemorrhoidal plexus, the hemorrhoid heads; mucosa and vessels are stretched and may rupture; a cushion with symptoms is called hemorrhoid.
- Above the dentate line = internal hemorrhoid; if it slips below the dentate line under squamous epithelium = external hemorrhoid; both = mixed or combined hemorrhoid.
- Straining or constipation makes the cushion slide until it protrudes out of the anus.
- Internal hemorrhoid: painless, usually bleeding with defecation, bright red fresh blood dripping, no clot (because connection between venous and arterial blood vessels), unlike cancer where blood is mixed with stool or mucus.
- External hemorrhoid: aching, because skin around the anus is stretched, mucosa prolapse makes it moist, itchy, needs scratching, soiling; some very painful from thrombosis or strangulation.
- Bleeding is usually dripping with defecation; beware patients over 50 years: constipation, stool mixed with blood, mucus, bowel habit change: think of cancer.
- Most hemorrhoids do not hurt, except thrombosed external hemorrhoid.
- Examination: PR and anoscope; position: left lateral decubitus, flex hip and knee (some use prone jackknife); if family history of cancer, add colonoscopy.
- Grading of internal hemorrhoid (Goligher): 1. bleeding without prolapse; 2. prolapse with spontaneous reduction; 3. prolapse with manual reduction; 4. irreducible prolapse. Figure (skip).
- Grade 1, 2, 3: usually OPD treatment with minor procedures such as rubber band; grade 3, 4: especially grade 4, surgery; grade 3 half-half, advised to operate gradually.
- Table of treatment: first degree: bleeding, no prolapse: dietary modification; second degree: prolapse with spontaneous reduction: rubber band ligation; bleeding or seepage: coagulation, dietary modification; third degree: prolapse requiring digital reduction: surgical hemorrhoidectomy; bleeding, seepage: rubber band ligation, dietary modification; fourth degree: prolapsed, cannot be reduced: surgical hemorrhoidectomy; strangulated: urgent hemorrhoidectomy, dietary modification.
- Principles of treatment:
  1. Internal hemorrhoid: if seen by proctoscope but no symptoms, no treatment; treat only if bleeding or prolapse.
  2. Method depends on grading, complications and location of the hemorrhoid heads.
  3. Every case needs dietary and lifestyle modification: high fiber diet, plenty of water, bulk-forming agent to soften stool; while straining, do not read, play phone, or flirt in the toilet.
  4. Drugs: 2 groups: suppository inserted into the anus or cream applied at the hemorrhoid head; local drugs usually contain steroid and antibiotic to reduce irritation and pain, making the anus slippery, no pain in the short term; phlebotonic flavonoid (daflon): believed to reduce inflammation and increase venous return, strengthen vessels, reduce bleeding, pain and itching, but compared with placebo studies show no difference.
  5. Some need intervention: 5.1 OPD treatment: aim to remove some hemorrhoid tissue and create scar fixation so the anal cushion sticks to the anal wall and does not slide down: rubber band, sclerosant injection, coagulation or burn; 5.2 surgical methods: many techniques (written briefly).
  6. External hemorrhoid: if no symptoms, no treatment unless large and annoying or complicated (thrombosed external hemorrhoid): remember: for external hemorrhoid, injecting sclerosant or using rubber band is forbidden, because very painful.

### Rubber band ligation (RBL)
- The most effective OPD treatment and the most popular; no anesthesia, no bowel prep; suits internal hemorrhoid grade 1-2; grade 3 acceptable but surgery gives better results; the pedicle of the hemorrhoid must lie above the dentate line 1-1.5 cm. Figure (skip).
- With the band no pain; can band 1-3 heads per session; safe to band one head every 4 weeks; put in an anoscope then Barron ligation around the pedicle with double rubber band; the hemorrhoid head turns necrotic and usually sloughs within 1 week; success 65-85%.
- Complications: 1. pain: from banding too low, below the dentate line where pain sensation exists; tolerable: give analgesic and sitz bath; if unbearable, remove the band; 2. delayed bleeding: from band slipping off or infection, usually after 1 week, mostly stops on its own, a few need suture or cautery; 3. local infection: beware immunocompromised patients such as HIV with CD4 under 200 or on chemo: severe infection, even necrotizing infection with pelvic sepsis, death; suspect severe infection when the triad: delayed anal pain, urinary retention, fever.
- Not to do RBL in: patients on Coumadin or heparin; if on ASA or Plavix stop 5-7 days before; if cannot stop, do not band; do sclerotherapy instead.

### Sclerosing therapy
- Put in an anoscope, inject 1-2 cc sclerosant into the submucosa about 3 mm above the pedicle of the hemorrhoid, to cause fibrosis that strangulates the vessels; do not inject into the hemorrhoid head itself (drug may escape into the circulation); inject at the submucosal layer, not deeper (inflammation until stricture) or shallower (drug only in mucosa: mucosal ischemia and sloughing into a wound). Figure (skip).
- Sclerosants commonly used: 1% aethoxysclerol, sodium morrhuate, 5% phenol 1-2 cc; one hemorrhoid per session, inject not more than 3 heads; repeat every 3-4 weeks until the hemorrhoid has gone.
- Suits 1st and 2nd degree bleeding internal hemorrhoid where the head is small and not suitable for banding.
- Do not inject during fecal impaction or an external hemorrhoid (pain, anal fissure, urinary retention); even though early success is 75%, long-term results are not very good.
### Coagulation technique
- Bipolar, laser or infrared heat to burn the hemorrhoid pedicle, causing fibrosis; the tissue turns white and usually disappears in 4 weeks; can repeat every 3 weeks; suits internal hemorrhoid grade 1 and 2, like sclerosing therapy. Disadvantages: high recurrence, instrument hard to find and expensive. Figure: infrared coagulation (skip).
### Cryosurgery
- Burn the hemorrhoid head with liquid nitrogen so that it shrinks; problem: depth is hard to control, slow healing, much watery discharge, tiresome; judged not to work.
### Lord's sphincter dilation
- Insert 2 fingers into the anus, stretch, then gradually increase to 4 fingers (up to 8 fingers with both hands); when the anus is stretched, anal pressure falls and defecation is comfortable; problem: anal incontinence, especially in the elderly; not popular; forbidden answer.

### Surgical treatment of hemorrhoid
- Indications: 1. failure of conservative treatment (e.g. rubber band ligation); 2. hemorrhoid grade 3 and 4; 3. combined internal and external hemorrhoid; 4. coexisting disease needing surgery, e.g. FIA, fissure; 5. thrombosed external hemorrhoid; 6. prolapsed strangulated hemorrhoid; 7. patient chooses to operate after informed discussion.
- Anesthesia: anus is very sensitive; if the block is inadequate the patient has pain and the operator cannot cut: use spinal or local 0.5% xylocaine with adrenaline 1:200,000.
- Many techniques, open and closed, chosen as convenient; a resident should know each one's advantages and disadvantages.
- For circumferential hemorrhoids, choose to cut the 3 largest heads first, then gradually trim small ones the next day.
- Open hemorrhoidectomy (Milligan-Morgan): ligate the hemorrhoid at its base, then cut off the hemorrhoid head protruding from the anus (excision and ligation); the skin wound is left open to heal by itself; can do 2-3 heads, leaving skin bridges about 1 cm between sites. Advantages: reduces anal stricture, short operation. Disadvantage: wound heals slowly, painful for long, soiling, because the wound is open, about 4 weeks to close. Figure (skip).
- Closed hemorrhoidectomy (Ferguson): excision and ligation, then the wound is closed with continuous absorbable suture from the inside outward along the length; developed in 1952; use the Hill-Ferguson retractor. Advantages: heals faster than open. Disadvantages: more postoperative pain because the wound is tight; if too much anoderm is cut, sutures sewn too tightly strangulate (prevent by leaving 3 columns and preserving at least 1 cm of anoderm). Fansler retractor (size over 3.5 cm) is used for closed technique by some: larger scope allows good excision without cutting too much; most of the wound heals by primary intention and there is no anal stricture; disadvantage: may cut too little, leaving skin tags.
- Whitehead hemorrhoidectomy: cut the hemorrhoidal vessels in the submucosa along the circumference of the anus at the dentate line, remove all, then suture the mucosa to an anodermal flap at the lower side at the dentate line; no longer popular since mucosa slides out (ectropion) with continuous soiling, hard to treat, may cause anal stricture; ectropion with soiling is called Whitehead's deformity.
- Stapled hemorrhoidopexy: hemorrhoidal circular stapler inserted into the anus; purse-string suture at the mucosa 3-4 cm above the dentate line; tighten the thread on the shaft, rotate the device closed, fire: removes a ring of suprahemorrhoidal rectal mucosa, staples the wound, pulling the hemorrhoid tissue back into the anal canal without handwork; short operation. The staple line lies 2-3 cm above the dentate line so not much pain; it lifts and pushes the cushion back to the original position, anus not narrowed. Suits grade 3 and grade 4 or mixed circumferential hemorrhoids; do not use with external hemorrhoid that cannot be reduced into the anus; the instrument is expensive. Include only mucosa and submucosa, not sphincter muscle and vagina in the staple line, otherwise fecal incontinence, retroperitoneal sepsis and rectovaginal fistula. Contraindications: abscess, gangrene, anal stenosis, rectal prolapse. Figure (skip).
- Hemorrhoidal artery ligation (HAL) = transanal hemorrhoidal dearterialization (THD): Doppler guide finds the distal superior hemorrhoidal artery branches at 6-8 circumferential points in the distal rectum, each vessel ligated with a suture placed per-anally; frequently modified to include mucopexy for associated prolapse; reduces blood flow to the hemorrhoid heads so they shrink; used with grade 2, 3; the author says results are good enough to substitute for excision but he has not seen it done in Thailand.

### Postoperative complications of hemorrhoidectomy
1. Urinary retention: the most common, about 15%; painful, patient lies flat, hard to strain; decrease it with adequate analgesia, do not overload IV fluid; if it truly will not pass, catheterize (UTI may follow).
2. Postoperative bleeding: 2.1 immediate (within first day): usually technical error, poor ligation of the pedicle, the tie slips and bleeds, dripping; treatment: most do not stop by themselves, so return to OR, give good anesthesia, open the anus, resuture; 2.2 delayed: usually after about 1 week from the thread breaking or infection; bleeding at defecation, not very active; mostly conservative and stops on its own; if not, treat as above: anesthesia, prone/lithotomy, anoscope, suture or cauterize.
3. Postoperative pain: 3.1 early: causes: 3.1.1 anal packing (feels like stool stuck at the anus); 3.1.2 plaster pulling the buttocks together; 3.1.3 internal sphincter spasm; 3.1.4 closed hemorrhoidectomy with anoderm and anal verge sutured too tight; 3.1.5 wound hematoma; 3.2 late: 3.2.1 intersphincteric abscess; 3.2.2 sphincter injury.
   - Treatment: 1. adequate analgesia; 2. avoid anal packing or plaster pulling the buttocks; 3. if closed technique with much tightness, change to open; 4. warm sitz bath relieves anal spasm; 5. metronidazole after surgery: a study reports it reduces pain, believed to reduce subclinical infection (most do not accept it; the author does not either).
4. Fecal impaction: usually from severe pain until the patient will not defecate, or from analgesics (morphine group) that reduce bowel movement; prevent by adequate analgesia and laxative before and after surgery; if the patient refuses to defecate, may need to wash stool out or manually evacuate.
5. Pelvic sepsis (necrotizing infection): found after RBL and stapled hemorrhoidopexy, rare but must beware, especially in compromised hosts such as HIV; symptoms: high fever, severe pain, cannot defecate, fast heart rate, usually within 12 hr after surgery; if suspected CT abdomen/pelvis often shows retroperitoneal air. Treatment: 5.1 hurry IV antibiotic; 5.2 return to OR, rectal examination under anesthesia with anoscope to rule out necrotizing infection; debride necrotic tissue, adequate drainage and colostomy to reduce fecal contamination.
- Postoperative stricture and ectropion are not detailed ("the level of a master must not have them").

### Non-thrombosed external hemorrhoid
- Treatment is only diet modification; indications to excise: very large, poor hygiene, or much pain.

### Thrombosed external hemorrhoid
- External hemorrhoid with a clot inside the hemorrhoid vein: severe pain, strains with hard stool, a tense bluish tender mass at the anal margin. Figure (skip).
- Conservative or excision depends on the duration (pain curve: peak at 2-3 days, then falls over 1-2 weeks; excision window is the first 72 hr; after about 4 days watching):
  1. Within 72 hours: elliptical excision of the external hemorrhoid, or urgent hemorrhoidectomy; thrombectomy alone may not be enough since re-thrombosis can occur.
  2. Over 72 hours: pain has usually peaked, the clot slowly dissolves, leaving only a skin tag; treat by symptoms; surgery is not advised since the surgical wound troubles the patient more than doing nothing; consider excision only for recurrence cases.
  - Practical advice (Dr. Chulalongkorn, agreed by the author): within 3 days: 1. a single small thrombosed lump: evacuate the thrombus (but beware: most textbooks say not enough, excise it all); 2. more than 2 lumps: excise; 3. combined thrombosed external and internal hemorrhoid: do hemorrhoidectomy at once; 4. if over 3 days and the hemorrhoid is gradually shrinking: conservative.

### Incarcerated internal hemorrhoid
- Prolapses out of the anus but no mucosal necrosis yet: push it back in and then treat according to grade; if it cannot be pushed in or already has mucosal necrosis, do hemorrhoidectomy; when cutting do not take too much mucosa, suture so the anus does not narrow.

### Prolapse strangulated internal hemorrhoid
- Usually grade 2, 3 hemorrhoid; one night the hemorrhoid heads protrude at the anus and cannot be pushed in; the anus squeezes until the heads become gangrenous: a black circumferential mass with radiating folds around the anus; very painful, cannot be reduced; hurry to do urgent hemorrhoidectomy, removing the dead tissue; pain disappears quickly; no need to bring the patient back again; conservative treatment (sitz bath) does not work. Figure (skip).

### Hemorrhoid in portal hypertension
- When a patient presents with anorectal bleeding: take history, examine, scope from below and above, give blood, correct coagulopathy.
- If bleeding comes from the anorectum, separate hemorrhoid bleeding from anorectal varices bleeding: anorectal varices on anoscopy are bluish or gray tortuous, saccular submucosal veins, extending upward, some down to the distal rectum.
- Treatment: 1. bleeding hemorrhoid: conservative as much as possible; if it will not stop, suture the bleeding point; if not controllable, hemorrhoidectomy; 2. bleeding anorectal varices: packing, suture ligation, sclerotherapy as chosen; most use silk figure-of-8 suture with drugs to reduce portal pressure; RBL is not advised (rebleeding often); if uncontrollable, refer for intervention (embolization or TIPS).

### Hemorrhoid in IBD
1. Ulcerative colitis: hemorrhoidectomy as usual.
2. Crohn: if not necessary, and the disease is not quiet, do not do hemorrhoidectomy at all, since the wound heals very poorly.

### Hemorrhoid in HIV, leukemia, lymphoma
- If not necessary, avoid hemorrhoidectomy: high infection risk and poor wound healing; if it must be done, improve the patient first, including treating fever and correcting WBC and platelet counts.
- Refractory to conservative treatment in asymptomatic HIV (seropositive only, or CD4 over 200): RBL or hemorrhoidectomy can be done, with antibiotics.

### Hemorrhoid in pregnancy
- Common; most settle with conservative treatment, symptoms often better after delivery; operate only if necessary, e.g. strangulation or thrombosis.

### Interesting topics
- Sitz bath: can be done right after surgery; helps pain and keeps the wound clean; soak twice a day and after each defecation; for a short time until the pain goes, then wash the anus gently with a bidet-like spray instead of a sitz bath. Remember: 1. after each stool wash with water, do not use toilet paper to wipe; 2. patients with anal pain generally do not like warm water (the author says warm water melts the stitches faster, so wounds open and bleed); bidet-type water spray twice a day is fine; some surgeons do not advise sitz bath at all.
- Antibiotic: no report supports giving it routinely after hemorrhoidectomy; in theory, answer: not routine; give only in those with low host defense, e.g. diabetes, AIDS, hematologic malignancy, bacterial endocarditis, valvular heart disease, or those who will have heart valve replacement.

## Anal Fissure
- A longitudinal tear at the anal margin below the dentate line down to the anal verge.
- Typical fissure: 80-90% at posterior midline; 10% at anterior midline.
- 1% atypical (lateral): found in TB, Crohn, HIV, herpes, GC, chlamydia, syphilis, cancer, leukemia; when a fissure is found outside the midline, investigate. Figure: location distribution (posterior 90%, anterior 10%, lateral under 1%: Crohn, UC, syphilis, TB, leukemia).
- Why the posterior midline: 1. anodermal blood flow at the posterior midline is the poorest; 2. muscle supporting the stool mass is less, strain is less supported; 3. higher tension.
- Hypotheses of pathogenesis (real cause unknown): 1. hard stool, defecation straining, the posterior being the usual site; the problem is that some people with soft stool also get fissure; 2. anal hypertonic contraction: high resting anal pressure from internal anal sphincter spasm is the most important factor: when the anus squeezes too tightly, blood supply to the anal mucosa decreases, so a tear becomes an ischemic ulcer, healing slowly; patients feel very painful, anus squeezing tight all the time, while the external anal sphincter is normal.
- Two kinds: acute and chronic.
### Acute anal fissure
- Early, shallow wound like a paper cut; with good bowel care it should heal within 6 weeks. Figure (skip).
### Chronic anal fissure
- Hard stool tears again and again; the more pain, the more internal sphincter spasm, so the wound heals even more slowly; usually lasts over 6 weeks; however treated the wound may not heal at all; some become infected, turning into a pus pocket then a fistula (dorsal subcutaneous fistula, with the tract shallow, not passing the internal and external sphincter).
- Features of chronic fissure: 1. sentinel pile (external skin tag) at the lower end; 2. hypertrophied anal papilla at the upper end; 3. fibrotic wound edge, induration; 4. if the ulcer is deep, circular internal sphincter fibers visible at the base; a finger in the anus feels very tight. Figure (skip).
- Symptoms: pain during defecation, as if cut by a knife through the heart; may have drops of blood with stool; some have pain for hours; after straining you know it is real hell.
- History: if a posterior midline fissure is seen, treat first; trying to put in a proctoscope for a deeper look just hurts more; if the patient has fever, beware anal pain from anorectal abscess; a patient with fever and anal pain who cannot defecate: examination under anesthesia (EUA) if unsure.
- Chronic fissure hurts less than acute; at least do PR: wound edge feels hard, sphincter squeezes tight.
### Treatment of acute anal fissure
1. Prevent the wound from tearing more: high fiber diet, plenty of water, stool softener.
2. Reduce pain with oral and topical drugs; sitz bath helps relax sphincter spasm.
- Both together: 80% heal within 2-4 weeks. 20% do not heal and need helpers, e.g. topical nitroglycerin, topical calcium channel blocker, or newer ones: arginine (nitric oxide donor), topical bethanechol (muscarinic agonist), applied 4-6 weeks; relaxing the sphincter helps heal 50-70%. Those still unhealed are treated as chronic fissure: poor-risk patients: botox injection; good risk: if able to tolerate surgery, refer for partial lateral internal sphincterotomy.
- Figure (algorithm): acute fissure -> stool-bulking agents, warm sitz baths -> healed (80%) / nonhealed (20%) -> 0.2% nitroglycerin or 2% nifedipine 4-6 weeks -> healed (70%) / nonhealed (30%): repeat treatment with alternate medication, or chronic fissure: botulinum toxin A injection, open LIS (98% healing), closed LIS.
### Treatment of chronic anal fissure
- Initially treat per theory without surgery: 45-60%, not over 80%, improve in 4-8 weeks, but those not healing may recur because the internal sphincter spasm problem is not fixed.
- So treatment of chronic fissure focuses on reducing sphincter spasm completely and increasing blood supply to the wound so it heals; surgery achieves the aim and gives better results than drugs, so a chronic fissure patient may go straight to surgery without trying drugs first.
- Surgical treatment: for chronic fissure and recurrent cases:
  1. Partial lateral internal sphincterotomy (LIS): cut the internal sphincter about 30%, only the lower half about 1 cm, from the upper edge to the upper edge of the wound, not past the dentate line, without needing to excise the fissure; open or closed method; cut one lateral side only, not both (incontinence); some inject local, some do a block, no matter; but do not cut the sphincter at the midline at the fissure site, which produces keyhole deformity, a partial incontinence of both flatus and stool, needing anoplasty. Result: pain relief 2 days; healing about 95% in 3-4 weeks. Figure (skip).
  - If recurrence or non-healing, the cause is usually incomplete internal sphincterotomy: cut too short, not reaching the dentate line, or cutting the subcutaneous external sphincter mistaking it for internal.
  2. Anal dilatation: stretch the internal sphincter with 2 index fingers, then 2 middle fingers about 4 fingers; incontinence about 70%; nowadays not used; if met in an exam, answer wrong.
  3. Fissurectomy and anoplasty: cut the fissure with its hard edges, then close the wound with a V-Y skin flap from the anal margin; suits fissure with stricture, or elderly who may have incontinence if LIS done (accept sentinel pile).
- Summary: partial lateral internal sphincterotomy is the standard operation.
### Nonsurgical treatment
- Although LIS gives >95% healing alone, drugs should still be offered first in initial treatment, including high-risk patients; refer to the OR only when drugs fail; the aim of drugs is to reduce internal sphincter spasm; residents should know about 3.
- Botulinum toxin A: inhibits acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction causing muscle paralysis, injected into the internal sphincter at 2 o'clock on the left and 10 o'clock on the right, 20 units each site; sphincter relaxes within 7 days before the drug wears off, and the fissure heals; heals 80% with no incontinence. Disadvantages: expensive; recurrence over 50% after 1 year, and results lose to surgery.
- Topical nitroglycerin: nitric oxide donor: 0.2% glyceryl trinitrite applied at the anus morning and evening for about 2 months relaxes the sphincter; healing about 60%; disadvantages: recurrence 60%, headache, orthostatic hypotension.
- Topical calcium channel blocker: 0.2% nifedipine or diltiazem: blocks calcium entry so smooth muscle of the internal sphincter does not contract; apply morning and evening for 2 months; healing about 60%, fewer side effects, so better to use than nitroglycerin.
- Update treatment (post-op incontinence from LIS worries surgeons, so new methods; from Shackelford 2019 p.1867-1869): 1. botox injection + fissurectomy: 102 cases reported 2015, ulcer healing 95%, but ASCRS doubts about dose and number of injections, so not standard; 2. pneumatic balloon dilation: ulcer healing about 83%, loses to LIS, but with follow-up 2 years no incontinence.
- Post-partum anal fissure: common in the last trimester of pregnancy from constipation; treat with bulking agents, stool softener, sitz bath; healing about 90%.
- HIV with anal fissure: in advanced HIV with CD4 under 200; causes: herpes simplex and CMV; very painful; posterior midline like fissure, but overhanging edges, proximal location above the dentate line, deep penetration through the sphincter into the postanal space with collection; treat with antiviral plus non-surgical fissure care; surgery heals poorly.
- Summary: chronic fissure: 1. best results: LIS: disadvantages incontinence and pain; 2. sphincter-relaxing drugs suit initial treatment or poor-risk; fewer incontinence but not widely available in Thailand, results lose to surgery, long, frequent recurrence; 3. if not healing however treated, biopsy for another cause, especially lateral fissure.

## Anorectal Infection
- Residents must know about anorectal abscess (fistulous abscess), which presents in 2 phases: 1. acute: anorectal abscess; 2. chronic: fistula in ano.

### Anorectal abscess
- Normally the mucosa has 10-15 anal glands around the anal canal in the intersphincteric space; their ducts open at the crypts of Morgagni at the dentate line; when a duct is blocked, inflammation of the anal gland follows: cryptoglandular infection; the infection spreads to form an intersphincteric abscess, a pocket of pus between internal and external sphincter, which spreads along tissue planes. Figure (skip).
- Pus tracking downward gives perianal abscess (most common); breaking laterally through the external sphincter into the ischiorectal space gives ischiorectal (ischioanal) abscess; going back into the deep postanal space gives a deep postanal abscess; going upward above the levator ani gives supralevator abscess. Figure: ischiorectal, intersphincteric, perianal, supralevator abscess (skip).
- Symptoms: fever, pain, swelling, redness, heat at the anus, cannot sit; a deep abscess may have no fluctuation; if very painful use EUA, to find a bulging mass; suspect, try a finger in the anus.
- 4 types by space:
  1. Perianal abscess: 40-50%: infection of the subcutaneous tissue around the anus, outside visible swelling, red, tender; can be drained externally: incise at the most tender place, close to the anus as possible; if FIA follows it will not be hard to treat. Figure: cruciate incision (skip).
  2. Intersphincteric abscess: 2-5%: pocket between internal and external sphincter; no external swelling; patient complains of anal pain; PR finds a mass or tender spot in the anal canal; drain from inside the anus through mucosa and the internal anal sphincter (internal sphincterotomy to the abscess).
  3. Ischiorectal abscess (horseshoe): 20-25%: from anal gland infection at the posterior midline, pus passing through the deep posterior anal space to the ischiorectal space of one or both sides; at drainage, suggested modified Hanley's technique: radial incision at the posterior midline between coccyx and anus with only internal sphincterotomy (do not cut the external sphincter, to prevent incontinence) and removal of the infected anal gland, plus counterincision at both ischiorectal spaces to drain pus from the ischiorectal and deep posterior anal spaces; pass a Penrose drain through each counter drain and leave it about 1 week so the pus drains fully; then deal with the FIA that follows. Figures (skip).
  4. Supralevator abscess: pus rising above the levator ani because the abscess origin is high.
  - Supralevator abscess (continued): found about 2%; the pus is above the levator ani because the abscess origin is high; the external skin looks normal but the patient has high fever, much pain, sepsis; hard to diagnose, sometimes endoanal US is needed.
  - Drainage depends on where the abscess started (per Dr. Santhat): 1. if from the intersphincteric space and spread upward: drain through the rectum; 2. if from the ischioanal space and spread upward: drain downward through the skin. Figure: drainage of supralevator abscess (skip).
- Summary of treatment of anorectal abscess:
  1. The key is to drain the pus, taking care not to injure the sphincter and nerve (incontinence would ruin the doctor).
  2. Make the incision as close to the anus as possible, since if FIA follows the tract is short and easy to treat (FIA follows 30-50%); open wide enough to drain; for a small abscess use a radial incision from the anus; for a large one a circumferential curved incision around the anus; no need to curette the cavity (bleeds, ends with tight packing, much pain; also may scoop out the sphincter or injure the pudendal nerve, causing incontinence); a finger can help break loculation gently, wash with saline, light packing, or put a penrose drain in the cavity; at each dressing change or washing, if the wound closes quickly, leave a drain.
  3. Antibiotic: generally not necessary; give only if: 3.1 extensive cellulitis or systemic sepsis; 3.2 immunocompromised: DM, SLE, lymphoma, leukemia, HIV, hematologic malignancy (e.g. acute leukemia on chemo), morbid obesity, congenital heart disease, heart surgery with prosthetic devices (valve replacement), and those on immunosuppressants.
  4. If anal abscess comes with a simple FIA: do fistulotomy at the same time; with a complex FIA: only drain the pus first.
  5. Immunocompromised hosts, especially hematologic malignancy with neutrophil under 500: 11% get serious perianal infection, progressing fast; suspect anorectal sepsis with fever and perianal pain as the clinical hallmark; hurry for EUA; if an indurated area is found or pus is aspirated, drain quickly with aggressive debridement; send pus for culture; if leukemic infiltration is suspected do not forget to biopsy the wall.
  - If after drainage the patient still has fever and pain, it is usually inadequate drainage: return to the OR under anesthesia, open more drain or debride.

## Fournier's Gangrene
- Anorectal sepsis from strep, clostridium, gram-negative organisms; mortality 40-50%; men more than women; in immunocompromised hosts, cirrhosis, alcohol drinkers, diabetes, cancer.
- Symptoms: systemic sepsis symptoms: high fever, chills, severe pain at anal skin, scrotum or labia; the outside may not look very inflamed since necrosis is usually in the subcutaneous layer; some have local gangrene, blisters, black skin, crepitus.
- Treatment: 1. rush resuscitation and treat septic shock; 2. broad-spectrum antibiotics; 3. most important: aggressive debridement, removing dead or infected tissue until healthy tissue (about 21% of patients end up with orchidectomy); 4. some may need diverting colostomy to prevent fecal contamination, preferably loop transverse colostomy, to keep away from a possible spread of infection from below, with indications: 4.1 extensive perineal debridement with a broad wound that is hard to care for; 4.2 gross sphincter infection; 4.3 perforation of colon or rectum; 5. hyperbaric therapy: a new toy, not as important as item 3.

## Fistula in Ano (FIA)
- Occurs after the acute phase of an anorectal abscess when the pus does not dry up and keeps draining: a chronic fistulous abscess with an internal opening at the dentate line (the position of the anal gland opening), and an external opening on the skin at the anal margin, which may rupture by itself or be an I&D scar; outside shows a lump with pressure causing pus drainage at intervals (intermittent drainage). Figure: lump at perianal skin (skip).

### Classification of FIA
- Parks classification (must remember): classifies FIA anatomically by the depth of the tract that passes the external sphincter into 4 types (figure: type I 45%, type II 30%, type III 20%, type IV 5%).
1. Type 1 intersphincteric fistula: the most common (70% as written; figure 45%): tract runs from the internal opening at the dentate line through the internal sphincter into the intersphincteric space, not passing the external sphincter; drains at perianal skin (anal margin), external opening close to the anal verge. Remember: type 1 does not pass the external sphincter, so cutting does not injure the sphincter muscle, so no incontinence, so type 1 does not need a seton.
2. Type 2 transsphincteric fistula (25%): tract from the internal opening through the internal sphincter into the intersphincteric space, then through the external sphincter into the ischioanal fossa, out to the perianal skin. 2 subtypes:
   - 2.1 Low transsphincteric fistula: the tract passes through the subcutaneous external sphincter (most are straight); in type 2.1 it is classed as simple FIA like type 1, easy to treat.
   - 2.2 High transsphincteric fistula: the tract passes through the deep external sphincter with an internal opening at the posterior midline and external opening at the perianal skin; in the high type it may run into the ischiorectal space and exit laterally, as horseshoe fistula, which carries a high risk of recurrence after surgery.
3. Type 3 suprasphincteric fistula (3%): tract starts at the internal opening at the posterior midline, curves upward in the plane between the internal sphincter and the deep external sphincter and levator ani, then loops over the external sphincter and curves down to open at the skin.
4. Type 4 extrasphincteric fistula: extremely rare: internal opening located high above the dentate line, tract runs high outside the external sphincter then curves down to the skin; usually from trauma, or from probing producing a false tract, or Crohn eroding.
- For practical use, classify by how easy treatment is: simple vs complex fistula (guideline):
  1. Simple fistula: easy to cut, tight, intersphincteric (type 1) or low transsphincteric involving sphincter under 30%.
  2. Complex fistula: hard to treat, high chance of incontinence, and often not healing.
- Criteria of complex fistula: 1. tract passing through the external sphincter more than 30-50%: 1.1 high transsphincteric; 1.2 suprasphincteric; 1.3 extrasphincteric; 2. anterior fistula in women (the sphincter muscle at the front is rather thin); 3. multiple tracts, so many openings it confuses; 4. recurrent FIA; 5. already with incontinence, e.g. previous sphincter injury; 6. previous RT; 7. underlying Crohn's disease, AIDS.

### Tract identification
- Must know where the tract lies and how it runs so the cut plan can be set; emphasize finding the internal opening:
  1. PR: a superficial fistula may palpate the tract as a firm cord; with a proctoscope pus may be seen coming from the internal opening; if the opening is clear, a probe can be passed through the tract; if the opening is unclear do not force the wire, which makes a false tract.
  2. Dye injection: methylene blue or hydrogen peroxide through the external opening, watch where it exits at the internal opening.
  3. Endoanal US: popular in this era; examine and read by the doctor who operates; if the doctor's skill is low or the tract is far from the anus it may mislead; use in complex fistula.
  4. MRI: from recent data identifies the tract better than endoanal US, so use MRI in complex FIA.
  5. Goodsall's rule: current reports say accuracy is low, about 50% but acceptable: remember the tract runs curved to the posterior, and anteriorly within 3 cm the tract runs straight; practically the author uses 1, 2, 5 since he does not have endoanal US and MRI. Figure: Goodsall's rule (transverse line, anal line, posterior curved tracts, anterior straight tracts, exception 3 cm).
  6. During surgery, when beginning to cut the tract, try pulling the tract sideways to see where the internal opening dimples.

### Treatment options of FIA
- FIA is an indication for surgery. The best surgical method has high cure, low recurrence, no incontinence, is easy, and heals fast.
- Must find the internal opening, know the relation of the tract to the external sphincter, and remove the infected anal gland; do not cut the external sphincter too much (incontinence).
- Remember: normally the internal opening of FIA is at the dentate line; if the opening is higher than the dentate line, especially in the rectum, suspect IBD, neoplasm, trauma or infectious disease.
- Textbook advice: biopsy the fistula tract cut or curetted and send to pathology to rule out, especially chronic ones (over 10 years), repeated ones, or a nodule at the external opening.
- Fistulotomy: put a probe through the fistula tract, then cut with cautery, opening the tract along the length from the external opening seen as a lump at the skin to the internal opening; gently curette granulation tissue, then leave the wound to heal by secondary intention; suits simple fistula; some sphincter muscle is cut, so incontinence is possible. Fistulectomy: cuts deeper, bigger wound, higher chance of sphincter injury. Figure (skip).
- Complex fistula needs helpers to prevent incontinence:
  1. Seton: used to avoid cutting the sphincter muscle all at once in complex fistula: silk or nylon No. 1, vascular loop, or a small penrose threaded through the tract around the external sphincter, tied to stimulate fibrosis of the external sphincter, then tightened about every week until it cuts through all the muscle by itself (the author likes a vascular loop); as the muscle cuts gradually, fibrosis follows, so when the muscle is cut through and the silk falls, the muscle ends are held by fibrosis and do not separate; called cutting seton; it takes months to cut through; patients with a thread poking at the anus are fed up. Figure: tight (cutting) seton, commonly used in high transsphincteric fistula, divides muscle slowly to produce a gradual fistulotomy (skip).
     - Another purpose of a seton is to drain a large FIA, or as bridging therapy awaiting fistulotomy: called drainage seton.
     - In exams: use cutting seton when the tract involves external sphincter more than 30-50%; using a seton reduces major incontinence to only 5%; depending on where the seton cuts and fibrosis occurs, assess whether a staged fistulotomy is needed.
  2. Endorectal advancement flap closure (ERAFs): curette the external opening first, cut the internal opening from the inside, close it with a flap from the rectal wall consisting of mucosa, submucosa and superficial smooth muscle above the internal opening, slide the flap down to cover the internal opening so fecal organisms cannot run into the tract. Figure (skip). Fairly hard; advantage: in theory no sphincter cut, so no incontinence, but reports say incontinence can still occur 12-30%, probably during flap dissection; also less pain, no wound to wash, no sitz bath, since the wound is inside the rectum. Said to suit high transsphincteric fistula in Crohn without active proctitis, and high anterior fistula in women.
  3. FIA with anal stricture: island flap from perianal skin to widen the anus and pull the upper edge of the flap to cover the internal opening: dermal island flap anoplasty (the author has done it; his teacher Dr. Chatchai Thatsiyanon taught it; OK).
  4. Core out fistula and close the internal opening: fistulectomy only (cut out the tract) then suture sphincter muscle to close the hole; the author has not done it; recurrence is high; said to suit women with anterior fistula, elderly FIA, those with previous incontinence problems, or tract passing too much of the sphincter.
  5. Fibrin glue injection: suits long fistulous tracts (remember, since fibrin glue plugs a lot): curette the tract then inject fibrin glue into the internal opening of the fistula; fibrin stimulates fibroblast growth, which stimulates collagen synthesis; recurrence is high, expensive, but still better than surgery in not hurting, not destroying sphincter muscle, and repeatable. Figure (skip).
  6. Fibrin plug: a fiber from pig intestine plugging the internal opening; disadvantages: very expensive, high recurrence; the author sees it as not working in Thailand. Figure (skip).
  7. Ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract (LIFT): first reported by Phillip 1993 in British J Surg; popularized by Dr. Rojanasakul (Thai), reported in J Med Assoc Thai 2007 with very good results, wound heals, no incontinence; residents must know the concept: LIFT can be used with FIA of almost every type except intersphincteric.
     - Idea: why surgical wounds of FIA do not heal: 1. stool passes through the internal opening down into the fistulous tract, causing chronic infection, usually E. coli and B. fragilis; 2. the intersphincteric space between internal and external sphincter is squeezed whenever the muscle contracts, so it becomes a closed septic nidus: if infection or pus is there, drainage is poor; this space is where FIA occurs most often.
     - LIFT: separate the fistulous tract without any sphincterotomy: open the wound at the anal verge, dissect into the intersphincteric space until the fistula tract is found; ligate both the proximal end (toward the internal opening) and the distal end with Vicryl Plus 3 sutures, the proximal end tied as close to the internal sphincter as possible and tight so stool cannot pass down the internal opening; then divide the intersphincteric tract to solve the closed septic space problem. Figure: steps 1-8: identify the internal opening by injecting sterile water through the external opening; circular incision at the intersphincteric groove above the expected tract; identify the tract by meticulous dissection with scissors and cautery; expose the intersphincteric plane with a special fistula retractor; hook the tract with a small right-angled clamp; suture ligation of the tract at the internal sphincter; suture ligation of the defect in the external sphincter.
     - LIFT has a small wound, heals fast, can be repeated, and cuts no sphincter muscle, so it is a promising operation of the future while waiting for supporting data.
     - Now: LIFT is a standard treatment in the Surgical Clinics of North America February 2010 (Anorectal abscess and fistula p.45-68), which cites the work of a Thai surgeon: even with only 58% healing on follow-up about 35 weeks; the Thai series (67 patients, Rojanasakul) healed 89.5%, only 10.5% failed, re-operated and all healed; and no incontinence problems.
     - Attempts in Thailand by others look less successful, believed due to: 1. wrong intersphincteric tract or dissection in the wrong plane; 2. unsecured ligation; 3. inadequate curettage; 4. sphincter injury.
     - Cameron 2017 p.274 also describes LIFT as a standard sphincter-preserving procedure like seton; Schwartz 2019 and Shackelford 2019 cite it too. The author for easy cases does fistulotomy, for a bit harder ones a seton.
   - Bio-LIFT: modification (Ellis, Univ. of South Alabama, DCR 2010) for complex FIA: place biological mesh into the intersphincteric space as a barrier against re-fistulization; bioprosthetic graft chosen because it tolerates contamination and has no foreign body reaction; 31 patients 2005-2008 healing 94% (29/31); not an RCT; BioLIFT reports since 2014 are similarly good.
   - Video-assisted anal fistula treatment (VAAFT): new; suits straight tracts: a small fistuloscope (about 4 mm) is passed through the skin opening into the tract, granulation tissue burned with electrode, then the internal opening is sutured closed.

## Proctalgia Fugax
- Sudden, fleeting rectal pain lasting not over 2 minutes, believed from spasm of the internal sphincter; diagnosis by excluding anal fissure, thrombosed external hemorrhoid, or abscess.
- Treatment: explain it is not dangerous; reassurance, and drugs to reduce sphincter spasm.

## Rectovaginal Fistula (RVF)
- Fistula between vagina and rectum or anal canal above the dentate line. Figure (skip).
- Etiology: 1. congenital, e.g. imperforate anus with fistula; 2. acquired: 2.1 trauma: obstetric injury most common; 2.2 postop: after vaginal or anorectal surgery, benign and malignant; 2.3 infection: TB, AIDS, cryptoglandular sepsis; 2.4 inflammation: UC, Crohn, diverticulitis; 2.5 neoplasm: CRC, gynecologic cancer; 2.6 post RT; 2.7 drugs, e.g. ergotamine, indomethacin.
- Classification: 1. by position (low, middle, high):

| Type | Rectal opening | Vaginal opening | Causes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | close to dentate line | near posterior vaginal fourchette | obstetric injury, foreign body |
| Middle | higher than low type, below sphincter complex | between fourchette and cervix | obstetric injury; postop rectal cancer; RT; undrained abscess |
| High | higher than middle type, above sphincter complex | near cervix or posterior fornix | postop rectal cancer; IBD; RT |
2. Simple vs complex:

| Type | Vaginal opening | Diameter | Etiology |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple | low or middle portion of rectovaginal septum | under 2.5 cm | obstetric trauma; cryptoglandular infection |
| Complex | upper portion of rectovaginal septum | over 2.5 cm | IBD or diverticular; malignancy; post RT; recurrence |
- Remember: low type: the most common cause is obstetric injury.
- Symptoms: 1. small low fistula: not much; some have only recurrent UTI or recurrent vaginitis; 2. large fistula: stool or flatus comes through the vagina continuously, foul vaginal discharge, very distressing; if the fistula is above the anal sphincter, stool passes continuously (life is ruined).
- Diagnosis, 4 aims: 1. is there a real fistula: PR and PV with proctoscope and vaginal speculum; simply a methylene blue test: methylene blue through the anus and a tampon in the vagina; hard cases: contrast injection through the vagina (vaginography); 2. sphincter defect: besides PR, EAUS or MRI; 3. cause: high type with history of cervical or endometrial cancer, or prior RT: biopsy to rule out cancer or radiation-induced fistula; if cancer, CT or MRI, colonoscopy; 4. size and position: bimanual: finger in the vagina and index finger in the rectum, feel the fistula; do not forget the gynecologist to evaluate the pelvic organs.
- Management:
  1. Conservative, especially simple or low and mid type from obstetric injury: may close within 3 months; or from a cryptoglandular abscess if drained well; spontaneous healing in at least 50%.
  2. Surgical: wait at least 3-6 months for inflammation to subside as much as possible, no infection or active IBD, nutritional status improved; if from cancer, do it sooner after full mechanical bowel preparation and antibiotic.
     - 2.1 Low or mid type: transanal repair preferred by proctologists: endorectal advancement flap enough; if sphincter injury coexists, overlapping sphincteroplasty. Figure: probe in fistula; incise rectal mucosa and internal sphincter; flap of mucosa and internal sphincter muscle; attenuated rectovaginal septum; flap sutured; excess flap excised (skip). Transvaginal repair: operating in a low pressure zone, incontinence and recurrence higher than transanal. Transperineal repair: dissect rectum and anus from the vagina and repair, possibly interposing tissue for strength; disadvantage: complex repair with risk of rectal and vaginal injury. Figures (skip).
     - 2.2 High type: transabdominal approach: if tissue is fairly healthy, explore laparoscopically, cut out the diseased tissue, close the defect, and to prevent recurrence interpose healthy tissue (usually omentum) between rectum and vagina; for those from RT, IBD (not active Crohn; UC can be done at once), infection such as diverticulitis, cancer: LAR + coloanal anastomosis with loop ileostomy; especially RT and Crohn, remember tissue healing is poor, so always make a proximal diverting ostomy; the very poor high-risk patient: only a diverting loop colostomy.
  3. Fibrin glue: minimally invasive, no significant complication; use in a long tract RVF; remember fibrin glue fails in a too short tract since glue cannot stay long.
  4. Infliximab: for RVF from Crohn.
  5. Hard cases: studies of interposition flaps, stem cell injection, closing with biologic mesh: wait for results.

## Rectourethral Fistula (RUF)
- Very rare; most are iatrogenic from surgery, suprapubic prostatectomy most, then perineal injury, rectal surgery, Crohn, cancer, post RT.
- Currently the most common cause: prostate cancer treated by RT or surgery (as underlined in the book).
- Symptoms: urine through the anus, or stool through the urethra, or pneumaturia.
- Investigation: retrograde urethrogram together with anoscope and/or flexible sigmoidoscope/colonoscope: to identify the rectal opening, assess anal sphincter, and see whether active rectal disease (IBD or radiation proctitis) exists; retrograde urethrogram shows position, size, extent, and whether urethral stricture or bladder neck contracture coexist. Figure (skip).
- Treatment: 1. conservative: diversion (fecal and urinary) and wait about 3 months for inflammation to subside; a small fistula may close; if the fistula persists, surgery to close the defects in both urethra and rectum; 2. a large defect, recurrent fistula, post RT or after ablation usually will not close by itself: fecal diversion first, then definitive repair; for RT cases with poor tissue, interpose gracilis muscle to close the fistula. Figure: gracilis flap closure (skip).

## Pilonidal Disease
- Also called hair-nest or sacrococcygeal sinus: disease from ingrowing hair follicle then foreign body reaction in the intergluteal cleft at the sacrococcygeal area; M : F = 4 : 1, age 20-30 years, hairy, well-built.
- Cause: unknown; formerly believed congenital, now acquired more; the most supported theory is ingrowing hair under the skin acting as a foreign body, resulting in infection. Figure (skip).
- Symptoms: often infection or abscess about 3-5 cm above the anus toward the back, with a draining sinus at the midline (pits), if recurrent: chronic sinus drainage.
- Treatment: incidental finding without symptoms: no treatment.
  1. If treatment is needed: first principle: good hygiene and hair control (shaving), which may reduce the need for surgery; if acute abscess: I&D: place the incision about 1 cm lateral to the midline (not at the midline, since the wound heals poorly); open along the length of the cavity through the subcutaneous layer, curette hair and necrotic tissue out, wash once or twice a day; the key point is to remove or burn away the hair at the wound edge so ingrowing hair does not enter the wound again; 60% of patients heal with only this. Figure: incision line lateral to midline (skip).
  2. Chronic or recurrent ingrowing sinus with persistent discharge and hair coming out of the sinus: needs surgery, with no need for preoperative antibiotic; options: unroofing the tract, curettage, marsupialization, then secondary intention; or complete sinus excision with primary closure, placing the incision lateral to the sinus, not at the midline; in extensive or complex disease primary closure has high recurrence: close with a flap: sliding flap = Karydakis procedure; rotating flap = Fishbein procedure; others: Limberg, V-Y plasty, Z-plasty. Figures: Limberg flap; modified Karydakis flap (lateral advancing flap: flap about 2 cm wide and 0.5 cm deep, ellipse should include all sinuses).
  3. Chronic sinus drainage of 20-25 years untreated, with a non-healing wound, induration, raised edge, easy bleeding: biopsy since it may turn to SCC (0.1%); treat SCC with WLE closed with a flap.

## Melanosis Coli
- Benign condition found in people using laxatives for over 4 months, especially anthracene group (senna, cascara, aloe); found at any colon site but right > left.
- Does not increase risk, and no relation with CRC or malignant melanoma.
- No symptoms; found during scope for CRC screening in people over 50: mucosa brownish-purple or black; biopsy of the black area finds no melanin pigment, only pigment-laden macrophages in the lamina propria. Figure: resection specimen showing black colon (skip).
- Treatment: stop the laxative; the pigment usually disappears within 6 months.

## Pruritus Ani
- Some call it anusitis: unpleasant cutaneous sensation, itching, and burning at the perianal skin.
- Found in 5% of the population; M : F = 4 : 1; age 50-70; itching often worse at night or when the skin is moist.
- Two groups: 1. primary or idiopathic: diagnosed by excluding secondary causes; believed from fecal contamination causing local irritation, with poor hygiene around the anus; 2. secondary: many causes: 2.1 infection (fungus, parasites, bacteria); 2.2 anorectal diseases such as FIA, fecal incontinence, hidradenitis suppurativa; 2.3 skin disease such as contact dermatitis; 2.4 cancer at the anus presenting with itching; 2.5 systemic disease: diabetes, hyperthyroidism, hyperbilirubinemia, chronic renal failure, iron deficiency anemia, leukemia and lymphoma.
- Diagnosis: history, physical examination, proctoscopy; if unclear do anal swab; if cancer suspected, skin biopsy with endoscopic evaluation.
- Cancers that itch: perianal and anal canal cancers, Bowen's disease (SCC in situ), Paget's disease (cutaneous adenocarcinoma in situ): they itch a lot and do not go away.
- Treatment: direct: treat the cause together with adequate perianal hygiene to keep the skin dry; no tight underwear; avoid scented skin lotion or soap, avoid injecting perfumed water into the anus (neurotic habit); topical steroid is still debated; if used, only for refractory cases for a short time to relieve itching.
