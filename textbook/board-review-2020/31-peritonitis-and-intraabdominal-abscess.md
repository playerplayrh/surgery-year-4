# Peritonitis and Intraabdominal Abscess 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · printed pages 683-695 (PDF pages 1780-1792)

## Introduction
- Peritonitis is an important topic, but standard textbooks such as Schwartz removed it and folded it into the surgical infection chapter with very little detail; the author brings it back and rewrites it, because surgery residents will surely meet it in the Board exam.

## Anatomy
- **Peritoneal cavity**: potential space from the diaphragm down to the pelvis covering the abdominal organs; area about 2 m², about equal to the skin surface.
- Divided into spaces by nearby organs, e.g. subphrenic, subhepatic, paracolic and pelvic spaces.
- Peritoneum: serous membrane of a monocellular layer of mesothelial cells.
- 2 layers: parietal (outer) and visceral (inner).
- **Nerve supply**
  1. **Somatic nerve** T7-T12 supplies the parietal peritoneum: irritation gives well-localized pain; abdominal muscles with motor supply from the same levels give a spinal reflex — abdominal muscle tensing, felt as guarding & rigidity.
  2. **Splanchnic nerve** T7-T12 supplies the visceral peritoneum: irritation gives poorly localized pain.
- Inflammation of an abdominal organ first gives visceral pain; when it spreads to the parietal peritoneum, pain moves to the site of the inflamed organ — clearly seen in appendicitis.

## Histology
- A single layer of mesothelial cells covers visceral & parietal peritoneum, with a network of blood vessels & lymphatic vessels.
- Peritoneal healing differs from tissue healing: tissue heals with fibroblasts, whereas peritoneum heals by migration of mesothelial cells from the wound edges and rapid proliferation of mature mesothelial cells, closing the peritoneal defect completely within 5-8 days.
- Healing time of peritoneum does not depend on the initial defect — small or large, healing time is the same (จำ).
- Clinical & experimental studies of peritoneal healing in abdominal incisions: suturing the peritoneum has the disadvantage of increasing intraperitoneum adhesion.

## Physiology
- Normal peritoneal cavity has 100 cc. of isotonic fluid.
- Peritoneal fluid absorption: 30-50 cc./hour.
- Severe inflammation → fluid shift via this route up to 300-500 cc./hour = 3rd space fluid loss, e.g. in severe acute pancreatitis, a large loss.
- **Intraperitoneal blood** is absorbed via lymphatics; blood cannot be absorbed through the peritoneal membrane.
- Bacterial peritonitis with uncontrolled sepsis: the first system to fail is the respiratory system.
- Treatment of septic shock: steroid is no longer given.
- **Factors increase spreading peritonitis** (very basic, but asked in the past)
  1. Polymicrobial flora
  2. Spillage of GI content
  3. Presence of a large volume in the peritoneal cavity → slower bacterial clearance
  4. Presence of blood, bile, urine, chyme → promote faster bacterial growth
- The body walls off infection with fibrin and omentum to decrease spreading peritonitis — not increase spreading.
- **Factors increasing mortality in peritonitis** (guessable)
  1. Duration — the longer, the worse
  2. Very old patients
  3. Many medical comorbids
  4. WBC < 5,000 or > 25,000

## Primary Peritonitis
- Also called spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP): bacterial infection of ascitic fluid without an intraabdominal source.
- Monobacterial infection: in children usually dipplococci (as printed); in adults usually E.coli; overall in every age group E.coli is number 1.
- **Causes** (สาเหตุ): UTI, URI or pneumonia, pericarditis — bacteria outside the abdomen spread hematogenously into the peritoneal cavity. The pathogenesis now favoured is bacterial translocation from the GI tract: reduced host defences plus impaired bactericidal activity in ascites mean translocated bacteria from the mesenteric lymphatics and bloodstream cannot be cleared → peritonitis.
- SBP is now more common in adults than children; common underlying disease in children: nephrotic syndrome; in adults: cirrhosis and SLE.
- **Current data on SBP**
  1. Adults > children
  2. Not related to toxemia in pregnancy
  3. In children, 2 age peaks:
     - 3.1 First year of life — but age < 30 days should not be diagnosed as SBP, as it is very rare
     - 3.2 Age 4-5 years
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): children more acute than adults — fever, generalized abdominal pain, distension, vomiting, some drowsy. About 30% of SBP have very few symptoms — only fatigue and anorexia. Diarrhea is common in children, uncommon in adults. Diagnosing peritonitis is not hard; the hard part is separating primary from secondary peritonitis.
- **Criteria for diagnosis SBP**
  1. Clinical presentations
  2. Presence of ascites
  3. Paracentesis: neutrophil > 250/mm³
  4. No evidence of an obvious intraabdominal source such as perforation
- **Abdominal paracentesis**: most useful for diagnosing SBP; fluid study also helps rule out secondary peritonitis (mixed organisms).
  1. Low pH < 7.35; lactate > 32 ng./ml.
  2. Neutrophil count > 250 cells/mm³ — highest sensitivity and specificity
  3. Gram stain shows a single organism — highly specific but low sensitivity
- Caution (ระวังด้วยว่า): gram stain or culture is positive in only about one third, so no bacteria does not rule out SBP.
- A single organism, especially gram positive cocci, fits SBP.
- **Treatment**: non operative management
  1. Start IV antibiotic such as ceftriaxone or another 3rd generation cephalosporin covering both aerobes and anaerobes, then adjust to culture; duration about 5-10 days
  2. Repeat tap at about 48-72 hr. to check response
  3. PAD has no role — whoever does it is wrong
- **Problems of SBP**
  1. High mortality — up to 50%
  2. Frequent recurrence: 50% in 6 m. and 70% in 1 yr. Some recommended long-term quinolone prophylaxis — this belief is now wrong (จำ); if quinolones are given, short-term prophylaxis is accepted by the experts.
- Greenfield 2017 p.969 and Cameron 2017 p.416 recommend prophylactic antibiotics, oral or IV, when there is:
  1. GI hemorrhage
  2. Low protein in ascitic fluid < 1.0-1.5 g/L, and
  3. Any one of: serum creatinine > 1.2, blood BUN > 25, serum sodium < 130, CPS score > 9, serum bilirubin > 3

## Secondary Peritonitis
- Also called septic peritonitis; the most common peritonitis.
- Intraabdominal source, e.g. ruptured appendix, PU perforation.
- Bacteria are mixed organisms, aerobes and anaerobes; common: B.fragilis, E.coli and Enterococcus.
- Pathogenesis (primary and secondary response) is not written — too basic.
- **Treatment**
  1. Treat the source of infection — ruptured appendix or PU perforation must be operated; if it can wait, e.g. sigmoid diverticulitis, give heavy antibiotics until fever settles and WBC returns to normal
  2. Metabolic and hemodynamic support

## Tertiary Peritonitis
- Also called persistent or recurrent peritonitis: chronic intraabdominal infection in patients on long antibiotics and with low host defence; may present with abscess or phlegmon, with or without fistula.
- Organisms from peritoneal taps are often odd ones never heard of.
- **Pathogenesis**: still poorly understood.
- **Treatment**: antibiotics covering the organisms found, but resistance is common.
- **Mortality**: > 50%.

## CAPD Peritonitis
- Also called peritoneal dialysis catheter related infection.
- **Incidence**: 1.3 episodes per year.
- **Organism**: most common staph. epidermidis, then staph. aureus — not strep.
- Single organism, like SBP — not mixed infection.
- 1-6% of patients develop a sclerosing process long-term → bowel obstruction.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): earliest sign is turbid fluid, then fever and abdominal pain.
- **Diagnosis**
  1. Cloudy dialysate effluent
  2. Clinical signs of peritonitis
  3. Fluid shows neutrophil > 100/cc³ (as printed)
  4. Gm. stain (as printed) or culture shows gm +ve bacteria
- **Observations** (ข้อสังเกต)
  1. Blood culture is rarely +ve
  2. A single organism may be pseudomonas in 3%; pseudo usually cannot be cured with antibiotics
  3. Mixed organisms or anaerobes → secondary peritonitis, possibly bowel perforation, usually needing laparotomy
- **Treatment**
  1. Start antibiotics: for minor organisms, first generation cephalosporin such as cefazolin is enough; for very ill patients or more virulent organisms, vancomycin. Together with heparin, add to the dialysate to wash the abdomen first — more effective than IV antibiotics. Most patients improve without needing CAPD catheter removal.
  2. **Indications for catheter removal**
     - 2.1 Persistent peritonitis > 4 days without improvement
     - 2.2 Fluid grows pseudomonas, fungus or TB
     - 2.3 Fecal peritonitis
     - 2.4 Severe skin infection at the catheter site
- Note (หมายเหตุ): HIV infection is not an indication for catheter removal; gm -ve infection by itself is not an indication — only gm -ve from pseudo.

## TB Peritonitis
- More common now because of many HIV cases.
- TB reaches the peritoneal cavity by 4 routes:
  1. Hematogenous spread from a pulmonary focus
  2. Transmural spread from bowel disease
  3. Fallopian tube in TB salpingitis
  4. KUB from TB nephritis
- Often the primary focus is not found — discovered only at autopsy.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ)
  1. Insidious onset: anorexia, fatigue, weight loss, diffuse abdominal pain for more than 2-3 weeks
  2. Ascites almost always present (SAAG < 1.1 g/dL)
- **Diagnosis**
  1. **Paracentesis** — problem is low sensitivity. Classic fluid for TB:
     - 1.1 +ve AFB in 80%
     - 1.2 Lymphocyte predominate
     - 1.3 High protein content
     - 1.4 Low glucose, usually < 30 mg./dl.
  2. **CT scan** — most useful test, sensitivity 94%: increased vascularity of the peritoneum, thickened bowel wall, serosal studding like sago pearls (เม็ดสาคู) on bowel serosa and peritoneal surface, omental cake, swollen mesentery, mesenteric and retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, ascites.
  3. If ascitic fluid and CT do not settle whether it is TB → tissue diagnosis by needle biopsy, laparoscopy or open peritoneal biopsy, taken at the sago-like spots.
- **Treatment**
  1. Anti TB 3-4 drugs for 6 months
  2. **Indications for surgery**
     - 2.1 For diagnosis
     - 2.2 Complications such as gut obstruction or fistula
- Note (หมายเหตุ): avoid drains or gut exteriorization — fluid leaks and oozes a lot.

## Chemical Peritonitis
- From bowel perforation with digestive juices, pancreatic juice, bile or urine irritating the peritoneum.

## Fungal Peritonitis
- Usually immunocompromised hosts: cancer patients, or patients on long-term steroid or antibiotics.
- Organism mostly candida.
- **Diagnosis**: culture of peritoneal fluid grows fungus.
- **Treatment**: IV antifungal such as amphotericin B, miconazole.

## Periodic Peritonitis
- Know a little — it has been examined.
- Peritonitis seen around the Mediterranean in Jews, Arabs, Turks.
- Familial pattern, AD.
- **Symptoms** (อาการ): fever, chest pain, abdominal pain, joint pain that come and go; sometimes resolves by itself.
- Patients are often operated because of a rigid abdomen thought to be ruptured appendix; exploration finds inflamed peritoneum and ascites but no bacteria or intraperitoneal (as printed "intraperineal") pathology.
- In such cases do an incidental appendectomy to avoid confusion if abdominal pain recurs later.
- **Treatment**
  1. Start with NSAID and analgesics
  2. Best drug: colchicine (used for gout) to prevent recurrent attacks; colchicine relieves pain in every case, so colchicine is a therapeutic diagnosis

## Drug Related Peritonitis
- 3 drugs can cause abdominal pain with rebound tenderness:
  1. INH
  2. Erythromycin
  3. Inderal
- These drugs may give signs of peritoneal irritation; just remember the drug names — the mechanism or pathogenesis can be ignored (as printed, "ช่างคุณแม่ของมัน").
- These patients are often operated: thick peritoneum pulling small bowel into adhesions.
- **Treatment** (การรักษา): lysis of bowel adhesions to the abdominal wall.

## Intraabdominal Abscesses
- Localized pus in the peritoneal cavity.
- Causes of pus:
  1. Intraabdominal infection, e.g. appendicitid (as printed), diverticulitis, PU perforation
  2. Secondary after surgery — previously in the USA common after pancreatic surgery — or after abdominal trauma
- Suggestive feature: fever on post-op. day 5-6, intermittent spiking fever.
- CT is the single best diagnostic modality.
- **Percutaneous abscess drainage (PAD)**
  - Success about 70% on the first attempt; a second PAD raises success to 82%.
  - **PAD failure usually from**
    1. Draining peripancreatic infection
    2. PAD done > 2 times already
    3. Pus from fungus or yeast — patient is very ill
  - **Indications for catheter removal**
    1. Daily drainage < 10 cc.
    2. Purulent changes to serous drainage
    3. Afebrile, normal WBC count
    4. Radiographic criteria: US or CT shows cavity collapse → can remove
  - **Contraindications for PAD**
    1. Coagulopathy (จำ)
    2. Adjacent to vital structures — puncture could be disastrous
    3. Ongoing uncontrolled GI content contamination, e.g. still leaking
  - **Indications for open abscess drainage**
    1. Failure PAD
    2. Primary disease can't be controlled
    3. Cancer perforation
    4. Interloop abscess
    5. Fungal abscess
- **Subphrenic abscess**
  - Suspect after liver resection, splenectomy, or liver/spleen trauma, about 5-7 days post-op: high spiking fever, distension, pain in the upper abdomen or lower chest, but few abdominal signs. CBC: leukocytosis with left shift; film chest: atelectasis or fluid in the lower lung field.
  - In the past, senior teachers from the old days (as printed, joke about teaching students and residents on bedside rounds) taught: when there is "Pus somewhere" in the abdomen, number one to think of is subphrenic abscess; take history, examine, percuss and press — the most tender spot is where the abscess probably is.
  - Imaging if plain film is sent:
    1. Film chest: pleural effusion or plate-like atelectasis in the lower lung
    2. Plain abdomen: raised diaphragm; may see air fluid level
  - Now CT is available, so young doctors send everyone into the scanner without much thinking (as printed).
  - **Treatment** (การรักษา)
    1. Percutaneous abscess drainage (PAD) under CT guidance first
    2. If PAD fails, or in a hospital with no one to do it → open drainage, anterior or posterior approach; for technique read Schwartz edition 4 — written by Uncle Schwartz himself; old but easy to read and understand
- Figure: Sites of upper abdominal abscess and drainage — subphrenic abscess above the liver (right) and around the spleen (left), subhepatic abscess below the liver; drains brought out through the flank.
- **Pelvic abscess**
  - Pus can collect around the Cul de sac without serious illness, because it may drain spontaneously into the rectum and pus may come out per anus. PR often needs examination under anesthesia: a soft bulging tender mass is felt; then do ERUS and try drainage through the rectum or vagina first.
  - CT example in the book: ruptured appendicitis with pus pooled in the pelvis.
  - If the primary disease can be treated by PAD, avoid laparotomy.
- **Retroperitoneal abscess**
  - **Causes** (สาเหตุ): mostly renal infection, then retrocecal appendicitis, pancreatitis, diverticulitis.
  - **Symptoms** (อาการ): pain at the frank (as printed), back or pelvis; fever, raised WBC.
  - **Treatment** (การรักษา): IV antibiotic + CT-guided drainage first; if not controlled, open drainage. Now expert laparoscopists also do laparoscopic drainage of pelvic abscess with success.

## Percutaneous Drainage
- First-line treatment, usually guided by US or CT (CT examples in the book: drain under the liver; drain in a pelvic abscess).
