# Gastrointestinal Lymphoma 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 1077-1091 (PDF pages 1081-1095)

- Lymphoma is a neoplasm of lymphoid tissue, which is found in almost every part of the body; residents should know a few things.

## Differential diagnosis of lymphadenopathy
1. Infection. 2. Autoimmune disease. 3. Malignancy: primary or secondary.
- Suspicion of malignancy: 1. age over 40 yr; 2. size over 2 cm and hard in consistency; 3. history of smoking, fever, night sweat, weight loss.
- Suspicion of benign: 1. age under 40 yr, usually nonspecific or infectious node; 2. size under 1 cm, not significant; 3. newly enlarging node.
- Decision by size: 1. under 1 cm: advise observation; if not growing, nothing needs to be done; 2. over 2 cm: advise FNA or open biopsy; 3. 1-2 cm: advise observation and reexamination in 1 month; if the mass does not shrink, do FNA or open biopsy; in this group, if there are no signs of inflammation (pain, swelling, redness, warmth), giving antibiotic while observing is not necessary.

## Lymphoma
- Site of involvement: 1. cervical 75%; 2. axilla 15%; 3. inguinal 9%.
- Divided into 2 big groups: 1. Hodgkin lymphoma (read in the spleen chapter; no need to know much); 2. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (know as much as written; if you can remember all, life is happy).

## Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL)
- A tumor arising from a lymph node basin.
- 70% are nodal; the other 30% are extranodal.
- Extranodal NHL: usually within solid organs: 1. GI 50%; 2. non GI 50%.

### World Health Organization (WHO) system
1. Diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL): found most, number 1, 55%; may arise from the start or follow low grade MALT lymphoma. Figure: histology (skip).
2. Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosal associated lymphoid tissue, i.e. MALT: found as number 2, about 40% (a prominent type; pay attention).
3. Those not found often, e.g. Burkitt's lymphoma, about 3%: a very aggressive lymphoma associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV); luckily this type is found around Africa.
- Residents only need to know this much; the rest is for hematologists.

## GI Lymphoma
- Is 20% of NHL; found from the oral cavity to the rectum.
- Stomach is found 50%, the most; next small bowel, colon, pancreas; except the Board exam in India: they found small bowel more than stomach.
- Symptoms: rather vague; need a high index of suspicion; suspect in patients with abdominal pain, anorexia, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, vomiting; 50% have anemia from chronic blood loss.
- Physical examination normal in 55%; average 2-3 years to diagnose.
- B symptoms (fever, night sweat, weight loss) found in under 12%.
- Criteria for diagnosing GI lymphoma (Dawson's criteria):
  1. Disease grossly confined to the affected viscus, without liver or splenic involvement, except invasion from the GI lymphoma itself.
  2. Absence of palpable peripheral lymphadenopathy.
  3. Absence of mediastinal lymphadenopathy on chest x-ray.
  4. Normal bone marrow biopsy.
  5. Normal peripheral blood smear.
- Diagnosis relies on tissue diagnosis, tissue from endoscopy: NHL looks like submucosal giant folds or grows like a polyp; if seen like this use EUS then a needle to sample for examination. Figure: endoscopic photos (skip).
- If pathology reads NHL, the medical doctors do upper airway examination, bone marrow biopsy, send CT chest and abdomen to see whether metastasis has occurred.
- Staging: TNM and Ann Arbor are popular; we do not need to know (except to be a close friend of a hematologist).

## Gastric Lymphoma
- Found about 2% of gastric cancers; 50% of GI lymphoma is gastric lymphoma.
- Most are NHL; commonly: 1. B cell lymphoma is number 1, arising from MALT; 2. i.e. MALT itself; 3. T cell lymphoma is found less in the stomach; most are in the jejunum; for treatment read in small bowel lymphoma.
- M : F = 3 : 1; age 60-70 years; but nowadays young people are found more because of AIDS.
- Found throughout the stomach, but most at the antrum.
- Common symptoms: bleeding, epigastric pain, dyspepsia.
- Diagnosis:
  1. Upper endoscopy and biopsy: positive result about 90%; do not forget H. pylori too; the most common endoscopic appearance is nodular with enlarged gastric folds, some are submucosal lesion; if gross looks like an ulcer or diffuse infiltration, it is bad because it spreads fast.
  2. EUS: very important for telling intramural infiltration of the tumor in MALT lymphoma: 2.1 a superficial lesion in the innermost layer, with no perigastric lymph node enlargement: classified as low grade MALT; 2.2 high grade: tumor infiltrates to the muscle layer or serosa, or has perigastric node enlargement.
  3. CT chest, abdomen, pelvic and bone marrow biopsy: if spread is found, it counts as high grade MALT. Figure: low grade vs high grade MALT CT (skip).

## Gastric MALT Lymphoma
- Found most at the stomach; while salivary gland, small bowel and colon, bladder and respiratory tract may also have MALT lymphoma.
- Mechanism of disease: believed to result from chronic inflammation:
  1. 90% are H. pylori infection, causing activation of B cells, until they aggregate in the lamina propria, and finally become MALT lymphoma.
  2. 10% occur after autoimmune disease.
- Figure (pathogenesis): chronic H. pylori infection -> autoreactive B cells -> early MALT lymphoma (H. pylori dependent) -> late MALT lymphoma -> transformed MALT lymphoma (H. pylori independent); aggressive MALT lymphoma with t(11;18) pathway; genetic markers listed (trisomy 3, p15/16 hypermethylation, t(1;14)/t(1;2) BCL-10, p53 mutation, LOH, p16 deletion, t(8;14)).
- Usually diagnosed from EGD in patients with nonspecific gastric symptoms, e.g. dyspepsia, seeing reddish mucosa with ulcer or erosion, multifocal lesions at the antrum; take biopsy right away.
- Treatment depends on: 1. H. pylori status, mostly positive; 2. whether low grade or high grade MALT.
- Principle of treatment: 1. low grade: treat with antibiotic; 2. high grade: treat with chemo RT.
- Low grade MALT lymphoma features: 1. EUS: superficial lesion; 2. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis: lung, liver, spleen normal, no node enlargement; 3. diffuse infiltration of small and medium sized lymphocytes (if large blast cells, it is high grade); 4. bone marrow biopsy normal.

### Treatment of low grade MALT
1. Start with H. pylori eradication therapy: 80% of patients have regression.
2. Endoscopic surveillance with biopsy 2 months after treating; since complete response takes 2-3 months, then serial endoscopy every 6 months for 2 years.
3. In relapse, or if MALT still persists after treatment, send for RT; but if it turns into high grade, give chemo RT.
- High grade MALT lymphoma features: 1. EUS finds tumor involving the muscle layer; 2. CT shows liver, spleen and node enlargement indicating spread; 3. lymphocyte infiltration is large blast cells forming clusters or sheets; 4. symptoms like gastric cancer, may have bleeding, obstruction; 5. about 50% have fever, night sweat, weight loss.

### Treatment of high grade MALT
1. Chemo RT in every case; before 2012 the CHOP regimen alone gave very good results; nowadays anti CD 20 (rituximab) is used with CHOP, found to give better results than CHOP alone; how to give and how long: see the oncology fellow.
2. Surgical resection: whether radical subtotal gastrectomy with D2 dissection or palliative resection, used when: 2.1 bulky tumor, to prevent bleeding/perforation; 2.2 residual disease after chemo RT; 2.3 complications such as bleeding, perforation, obstruction.
3. If H. pylori positive, give eradication together. Figure: CT of perforated gastric MALT lymphoma (skip).

### Summary: differences between low grade and high grade MALT lymphoma

| | Low grade | High grade |
|---|---|---|
| Infiltration (EUS) | mucosa or submucosa | muscularis propria |
| LN enlargement | negative | positive |
| Hepatosplenomegaly | no | yes |
| Bleeding or obstruction | no | yes |
| B symptom | no | 50% |
| Size of lymphocyte | small or medium | large blast cell |

- For the treatment and follow up guideline of low and high grade MALT lymphoma, follow the Schwartz algorithm (figure, as steps):
  - Low-grade (indolent) MALT:
    1. Confined to gastric wall, no t(11;18) translocation: H. pylori eradication therapy, re-evaluate at 12 months. Lymphoma regression: close follow-up. Lymphoma persists: stage I: XRT*; stage II: chemo** + XRT*.
    2. Lymph node involvement, t(11;18): H. pylori eradication therapy, re-evaluate at 3-6 months; same follow-up as above.
    3. Stage III or IV: H. pylori eradication therapy and chemo** +/- XRT*.
    - *XRT: external beam radiation therapy, approximately 30 Gy with 10 Gy boost. **Chemo regimens include chlorambucil, fludarabine, and cyclophosphamide, vincristine, prednisone (COP) +/- rituximab.
  - High-grade (aggressive): stage I, II, III: chemo* + XRT**; stage IV: chemo* +/- XRT**. No residual disease: follow-up. Residual disease: further chemotherapy or surgery.
    - *Chemo regimen usually cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone (CHOP) +/- rituximab. **XRT: external beam radiation, approximately 30 Gy with 10 Gy boost.

## B Cell Lymphoma
- Found 55% of gastric lymphoma.
- Treatment is like high grade MALT.

## Perforated Gastric Lymphoma
- Perforation can occur but usually from full thickness involvement followed by necrosis of the tumor, more from the effect of chemo.
- Treatment: needs surgery; which method depends on the patient's condition: if not toxic and there is a fever team at the hospital, it is advised to do radical gastrectomy since perforation can recur; but if the patient is toxic, closure with a Graham patch is enough.

## Small Bowel Lymphoma
- Incidence:
  1. Found as number 2 in the GI lymphoma group, but number 3 among small bowel tumors (20%).
  2. Bimodal distribution: found in age under 10 years in one period and over 50 years in another.
- Location: found most often at the terminal ileum, because GALT is concentrated there; 90% are diagnosed at operation (a surgeon who finds an odd mass should resect and send pathology, so the report returns as lymphoma).
- Most are diffuse large B cell lymphoma.
- 30-50% of patients come as emergencies, e.g. obstruction from intussusception, or perforation. Figure: CT and specimen (skip).
- Treatment (differs from gastric lymphoma):
  1. Localized disease: if operable, resect everything: segmental resection of the involved intestine and adjacent mesentery, with proximal and distal margin of 10 cm each side.
  2. Diffuse lesions: the main treatment is chemo.
- After surgery chemo is given for certain; avoid RT because of many complications.
- Prognosis: poor and bad compared with gastric lymphoma.

## Appendiceal Lymphoma
- Rare: 1-3% of GI lymphoma.
- Symptoms: like appendicitis, so diagnosis is usually from the pathology sent. Figure: specimen (skip).
- CT that raises suspicion: appendix with diffuse mural soft tissue thickening, diameter over 2.5 cm in the middle, large and bulging, with soft tissue thickening around; some have an appendicolith. Figure: CT of NHL of appendix (skip).
- Treatment: usually known as lymphoma after appendectomy since it was thought to be appendicitis:
  1. Localized and confined to the appendix only: appendectomy alone is enough, with no need for adjuvant chemotherapy.
  2. If extension into the cecum or mesentery: go back in and do right hemicolectomy, followed by postop chemo.

## Colorectal Lymphoma
- Rare: under 10% of GI lymphoma.
- Most common at the cecum, believed to spread from the terminal ileum.
- Symptoms: bleeding and obstruction; hard to separate from adenocarcinoma. Figure: colonoscopy (skip).
- Treatment: 1. localized lesion: surgical resection followed by chemo; 2. advanced disease: chemotherapy.

## Hepatic Lymphoma
- A very rare tumor, divided into 2 groups: 1. primary hepatic lymphoma, very rare; 2. secondary hepatic lymphoma, found more, usually with enlarged nodes and spleen. Figure: gross liver and CT (skip).
- Treatment: surgery is the main treatment, followed by adjuvant chemotherapy.

## Pancreatic Lymphoma
- Clinical presentation resembles pancreatic adenocarcinoma: patient comes with vague abdominal pain, fatigue, weight loss and B symptoms.
- CT: shows a pancreatic mass, usually larger than that found in adenocarcinoma, with large peripancreatic nodes, many, especially nodes at the level below the renal vein, a finding rarely seen in pancreatic adenocarcinoma; however, a definitive diagnosis cannot rely on imaging features. Figure: CT soft mass with peripancreatic lymphadenopathy.
- Biopsy: if lymphoma is suspected, tissue diagnosis must be obtained via: 1. EUS-guided (preferred); 2. percutaneous; 3. laparoscopy.
- Treatment: 1. chemotherapy (CHOP) + anti CD 20 (rituximab) is the standard treatment; 2. endoscopic stenting for cases with obstructive jaundice.
- Remember: surgery has very little role in this disease; the indications for opening are few: uncertain diagnosis where percutaneous or endoscopic biopsy gave unsure pathology from too little tissue; or a mass large enough to cause GOO, which needs a palliative bypass gastrojejunostomy.

## Post-Transplantation Lymphoma
- 95% are B cell lymphoma.
- Risk factors: 1. receiving immunosuppression; 2. receiving anti T cell therapy; 3. EBV virus infection, found often after transplant.
- Treatment: 1. reduce the dose of immunosuppressive drug; 2. give chemotherapy.
- Currently anti CD 20 is a targeted therapy against B cells, used to help patients die more slowly.
- Prognosis: very rapid; no matter how well treated, over 50% die.

## Breast Lymphoma
- Very rare; patients present in 2 age groups: 1. age under 40: usually bilateral, most are Burkitt's lymphoma; 2. age 40 or over: usually B cell lymphoma. Figure: patient photo and histology (skip).
- Some cases palpate a mass only at the axilla, with the breast being occult.
- Treatment depends on stage: 1. local disease: lumpectomy or mastectomy depending on stage; 2. recurrent or progressive locoregional disease: chemo RT.
- 5-year survival 74% and 10-year survival 51%.

## Mediastinal Lymphoma
- Children: found as number 2, after neurogenic tumor.
- Adults: found as number 4, while neurogenic tumor is number 1.
- Found at the middle compartment too, but the posterior compartment is very rare.
- 50% are NHL and the other 50% are HL (Hodgkin's lymphoma).
- Symptoms: fever, night sweat, weight loss, extrathoracic lymphadenopathy.
- Lab: WBC up, ESR up, C-reactive protein up.
- Treatment: 1. early stage: chemo + RT, cure rate 90%; 2. advanced stage: chemo + RT, cure rate 60%.

## Thyroid Lymphoma
- Found under 1% of thyroid malignancy; it is NHL B cell type.
- Associated with patients with chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis or Hashimoto thyroiditis, with lymphocyte transformation into lymphoma. Figure: patient, CT, specimen, histology (skip).
- Symptoms and examination: resemble anaplastic thyroid cancer: a thyroid mass that suddenly grows fast and is painless; some have difficulty breathing.
- Diagnosis: with FNAB.
- Treatment: 1. chemo is the main one, RT is a helper, often used together; 2. lobectomy +/- LND is used when there is an airway obstruction problem, usually in cases where chemo RT does not work.
- 5-year survival about 50%.

## CNS Lymphoma
- Two forms: primary in the CNS itself, or secondary from systemic disease.
- Currently found mostly in patients after transplant and AIDS patients.
- Symptoms: from increased intracranial pressure.
- CT brain: hyperdense lesion with enhancement. Figure: primary brain lymphoma CT (skip).
- Diagnosis: by stereotactic needle biopsy.
- Treatment: 1. the top set: steroid followed by whole-brain RT and chemotherapy; 2. some give intrathecal methotrexate as a supplement; 3. remember that for CNS lymphoma, surgical excision is not indicated.
