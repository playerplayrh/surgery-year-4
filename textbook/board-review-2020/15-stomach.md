# Surgical Diseases of the Stomach 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 818-913 (PDF pages 822-917)

## Introduction (book p.818)
- The stomach digests food and has nutrition and endocrine functions.
- Since H. pylori was discovered, the role of surgery and the ways of operating have changed; even Schwartz admits most residents have never seen vagotomy done.
- The 2020 update covers fields still not clear (surgery for peptic ulcer disease, operative complications); gastric cancer data come from NCCN 2019, SAGES 2018, Shackelford 2019 and reports from Japan (JGCA 2018) and Korea, which show treatment trends now and in the future.
- (The rest of the introduction is the author's advice to residents on training and reading; not study content.)

## Basic considerations: anatomy (p.818-820)
- Four parts from top to bottom: cardia (junction with the esophagus, at the LES), fundus, body, antrum. (Figure: esophagus, cardia, fundus, body, lesser curvature, greater curvature, pylorus, pyloric antrum, duodenum, angle of His, incisura, zone of transition.)
- Antrum: starts from the incisura angularis, the lowest part of the lesser curve of the body; the distal antrum narrows gradually into the pylorus; along the greater curve the antrum is the distal third, while fundus and body make up the proximal 2/3.
- The upper side of the body is the lesser curve; the lower side is the greater curve.
- Greater omentum connects the stomach to the transverse colon; the gastrohepatic ligament connects the stomach to the liver.

### Histology (p.819)
1. Mucosa: columnar epithelium.
2. Submucosa.
3. Muscularis propria: unlike the rest of the gut, the stomach has 3 layers: 3.1 innermost oblique muscle fibers; 3.2 middle circular; 3.3 outer longitudinal.
4. Serosa.
- (Figure labels: submucosal plexus of Meissner, myenteric plexus of Auerbach.)

### Blood supply (p.820)
1. Celiac trunk has 3 branches: 1.1 left gastric artery: the largest (remember); 1.2 splenic artery; 1.3 hepatic artery.
2. Greater curvature gets blood from: 2.1 right gastroepiploic artery (the second largest after the left gastric artery), which joins the left gastroepiploic artery, a branch of the splenic artery, to form the gastroepiploic arcade; 2.2 short gastric arteries: important, supplying the proximal part; so if a subtotal gastrectomy is done and the short gastric arteries are cut, it converts to a total gastrectomy.
3. Lesser curvature gets blood from: 3.1 right gastric artery; 3.2 left gastric artery.
- Right gastroepiploic artery is a very important branch in supplying the stomach used for gastric pull-up to join the esophagus in an esophageal cancer patient.
- Venous drainage: on the lesser curve both right and left gastric veins drain into the portal vein and into the IVC (as printed); on the greater curve, blood drains via the right gastroepiploic vein into the SMV and via the left gastroepiploic vein into the splenic vein.

### Nerves (p.821)
- Vagus nerve has a left trunk and a right trunk (figure: hepatic branch of left vagus; celiac branch of right vagus; nerve of Latarjet; pyloric branch).
  - Left trunk lies anterior and gives branches to supply the stomach, liver, biliary tree, pancreas and duodenum.
  - Right trunk lies posterior and gives branches to supply the stomach, small bowel and large bowel (up to the splenic flexure); beware the criminal nerve of Grassi arising from the right trunk: if it is not cut there is incomplete vagotomy.
- Functions of the vagus at the stomach: 1. innervates parietal cells controlling gastric acid secretion; 2. regulates antral peristalsis; 3. controls gastrin secretion.
- Sympathetic nerve: gives pain fibers from T5-10.

## Gastric acid secretion (p.821-822)
- HCl is made by parietal cells with 3 main stimulants: 1. acetylcholine from the vagus nerve; 2. histamine; 3. gastrin from G cells in the antrum.
- Figure (meal -> G cell (+gastrin), D cell (somatostatin, inhibitory), vagus (acetylcholine); ECL cell -> histamine; all act on the parietal cell; acetylcholine inhibits D cell.)
- Acid secretion has 3 phases:
  1. Cephalic phase: from sight, taste, smell, thought, and hypoglycemia, which stimulate the brain via the vagus nerve to the parietal cell to make acid and stimulate G cells to release gastrin; this phase gives about 30% of acid output.
  2. Gastric phase: the main driver is the G cell at the antrum, when signals come via the vagus or from antral distension, raising gastrin, which stimulates parietal cells to secrete acid; gives about 60% (more than any phase).
  3. Intestinal phase: gives only 10%; peptides and enterooxyntin in food stimulate the stomach to secrete acid.
- Acid production in the parietal cell (figure): histamine acts on the H2 receptor (Gs protein, adenylate cyclase, cAMP); acetylcholine on the M3 receptor and gastrin on the CCK-B receptor (Gq, phospholipase C, IP3, Ca++); somatostatin on its receptor (Gi) inhibits; all converge to activate the H+/K+ ATPase proton pump, with K+ and Cl- channels.
- Acid production summary: 1. H+ and K+ are exchanged at the gastric mucosa by the enzyme H+/K+ ATPase; 2. this exchange needs the proton pump so that hydrogen is secreted as HCl; 3. PPIs block the proton pump, so less acid comes out.

## Gastric cell types and secreted substances (p.823)
| Cell type | Substance secreted | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Parietal cells | HCl | Kills microbes and activates pepsinogen |
| | Intrinsic factor | Binds vitamin B12 to allow its absorption |
| Chief cells | Pepsinogen | Protein digestion |
| | Gastric lipase | Fat digestion |
| G cells | Gastrin | Stimulates gastric acid secretion |
| ECL cells | Histamine | Stimulates gastric acid secretion |
| Mucus-neck cells | Mucus and bicarbonate | Protects stomach epithelium from acid |
| D cells | Somatostatin | Inhibits gastric acid secretion |
- Beware the distractor often seen in exams: glucagon, which comes only from the pancreas.
- Pepsin: chief cells release the enzyme as pepsinogen, the first enzyme in proteolysis, making protein smaller before it passes to be digested and absorbed in the small intestine. When the stomach is acidic, pH <2, pepsinogen converts to pepsin, which digests protein into polypeptides; when pH >5.5 chief cells stop releasing pepsinogen.
- Gastrin: a hormone made by antral G cells; the most potent stimulant of gastrin release is a meal; gastrin secretion is controlled by luminal pH: gastrin binds the gastrin receptor of the parietal cell to stimulate acid; when pH <2.5, gastrin secretion stops.
- Somatostatin: comes from D cells; inhibits gastrin and acid secretion.
- The stomach also secretes intrinsic factor, which helps absorb B12 at the terminal ileum.

### Hypergastrinemia with excessive gastric acid secretion
1. ZE syndrome; 2. gastric outlet obstruction; 3. retained gastric antrum; 4. G cell hyperplasia.

### Hypergastrinemia without excessive gastric acid secretion (p.824)
1. Pernicious anemia; 2. atrophic gastritis; 3. renal failure; 4. postvagotomy status; 5. short gut syndrome; 6. medical acid suppression; 7. hyperthyroidism (not hypo).
- In a DU patient, hyperacidity inhibits G cells, so gastrin secretion is less (remember).

## Motility (p.824)
1. Receptive relaxation: the body expands to receive food swallowed down; then the stomach contracts to mix food with acid and digestive juices.
2. When food is ground fine, called chyme, antral peristalsis pushes the finely ground food through into the duodenum; peristalsis is strongest at the distal stomach (antrum).
3. Food not yet ground fine enough to pass the pylorus is pushed back (retropulsion) up to the body to be ground again until fine.
4. Gastric peristalsis is controlled by the vagus nerve, with a pacemaker at the greater curve.
- Normal gastric transit time about 3-4 hours.
- Rapid gastric emptying: 1. previous gastrectomy or pyloroplasty or bypass; 2. ZE syndrome.
- Delayed gastric emptying: 1. drugs, e.g. morphine, anticholinergics; 2. DM; 3. myxedema.

## Peptic ulcer (p.824-826)
- Definition: an ulcer arising from digestion by acid and pepsin. Divided into duodenal ulcer (DU) and gastric ulcer (GU).
- Pathogenesis:
  1. The commonest: H. pylori, then NSAID.
  2. The rest: smoking, drinking alcohol, steroid.
  3. Hyperacidity, once believed the main cause of DU; now only 30% of DU have hyperacidity.
  4. Hypergastrinemia from ZE or G cell hyperplasia.
  5. Hyperparathyroidism: high calcium stimulates acid secretion.
  6. Miscellaneous: COPD, chronic renal failure, anxiety.
- Acute ulcer: a superficial ulcer, not through the muscularis mucosa; if time is used as a cutoff, present less than 3 months; the wound looks shallow.
- Chronic ulcer: a deep ulcer through the muscularis mucosa; symptoms longer than 3 months; the wound has scarring.
- Diagnosis: mainly clinical, especially history of epigastric pain location and symptoms related to food, history of drinking alcohol, smoking, taking NSAIDs. From history and physical exam one cannot separate DU from GU; the symptom resembles dyspepsia, so think of other diseases, e.g. HBP disease, GERD, cancer.

### Management of peptic ulcer
- If symptoms suggest PU, doing upper endoscopy, US or CT to exclude other diseases before treating is a bit too much; every day many patients come to OPD saying "pain when hungry, pain when drinking" in the hundreds; where to find time to scope them all.
- With history alone and no complications such as bleeding, obstruction or perforation, treat as PU first, since DU and GU have the same initial treatment, mostly eradicating H. pylori (needs scope with CLO test before treating); this point is debated: Shackelford 2019 advises investigating H. pylori before treating.
- In practice, uncomplicated PU with no NSAID history does not need a scope yet: stop alcohol, stop smoking, avoid spicy food, give PPI: more than 90% improve; but if after 6-8 weeks of treatment the pain does not go, or goes away and recurs, scope the patient, because the cause may be: 1. H. pylori infection; 2. patient still taking NSAID or steroid (found much in Thailand); 3. another disease, e.g. HBP disease (gallstones), chronic pancreatitis, ZE.
- Treatment (since we know PU arises from H. pylori and NSAID, there are good scopes and good drugs, so the role of surgery is much less; surgeons now depend on endoscopists): 1. Medical treatment: 1.1 treat H. pylori; 1.2 give acid-lowering drugs so intragastric pH >5.5, so that pepsinogen from chief cells is not activated. 2. Surgical treatment: before surgery know whether DU or GU, since the operation differs; the indications for surgery are complicated cases: perforation, obstruction and bleeding.

## Surgical concept of peptic ulcer (p.826-827)
- Current indications for surgical treatment: 1. uncontrollable bleeding despite endoscopic therapy; 2. perforation; 3. obstruction; 4. intractability despite maximum medical treatment; 5. inability to rule out cancer when an ulcer remains despite treatment and negative endoscopic biopsies, especially gastric ulcer.
- Goals of surgical procedures: 1. permit ulcer healing; 2. prevent or treat ulcer complications; 3. address underlying ulcer etiology; 4. minimize postoperative consequences.
- If asked how to choose the operation at the exam: 1. standard textbooks differ at some points; the author likes Shackelford 2019's recommendations since they are practical. 2. Data to consider: 2.1 patient condition: suitable for definitive surgery or not, or is the patient hanging on by tubes; 2.2 whether the patient can follow up regularly; 2.3 severity or complication of the PU; 2.4 can the risk of peptic ulcer disease be reduced or managed, e.g. a patient on NSAID: can the drug be stopped; 2.5 surgeon's expertise; 2.6 limits of the hospital: whether there is a scope, interventional treatment; if there is, ask whether it is available 24 hours. 3. Do not answer a surgical method you are not familiar with or have never seen, even if it is theoretically the best choice, e.g. HSV is the best for intractable DU, but if asked in the oral exam TV+A or TV+D has a lower chance of pain (less chance of failure/hard) [as printed: lower chance of getting hurt]. 4. DU, if surgery is needed: 4.1 do less rather than more (less is often better); 4.2 usually a complicated case, so investigate H. pylori too; 4.3 the ulcer has no relation to cancer. 5. GU: 5.1 has a greater chance of being operated than DU; 5.2 needs tissue diagnosis to rule out cancer; 5.3 the choice of operation needs the GU type. 6. Role of laparoscopic surgery in peptic ulcer: 6.1 in elective cases: lap HSV, gastrojejunostomy; 6.2 in emergency cases: used in DU perforation.

## Helicobacter pylori (p.827-)
- An infection to know well because it is the most important cause of PU; causes DU 90% and GU 80%.
- The discoverers: Marshall and Warren in 1984, which changed the face of peptic ulcer treatment.
- H. pylori is a gram-negative rod with flagella (spiral), with whip-like tails, like a spiraling bacterium.
- It penetrates the mucous layer and attaches to the mucosa, and can live comfortably in the acidic stomach, dividing and multiplying a lot, found mostly in the antrum because: 1. H. pylori makes many enzymes, the main one being urease, which breaks down urea into ammonia and CO2, with ammonia neutralizing stomach acid; 2. the stomach has specific adhesion receptors for H. pylori, called Lewis b blood group antigen [as printed: a receptor allowing H. pylori to attach to the mucosa].
- H. pylori produces cytotoxins such as Cag A, Vac A, causing an inflammatory response and acute gastritis at the antrum and first part of the duodenum; if left in chronic inflammation, metaplasia occurs and eventually cancer.
- (Figure: H. pylori virulence: flagella (motility and chemotaxis to colonize under mucosa); urease (neutralizes gastric acid; ammonia mucosal injury); lipopolysaccharides; outer proteins; exotoxins (vacuolating toxin VacA); secretory enzymes (mucinase, protease, lipase); type IV secretion system injecting effectors (CagA etc.): actin remodeling, IL-8 induction, host cell growth and apoptosis inhibition.)
- H. pylori is now classed as a class 1 carcinogen, increasing the risk of gastric lymphoma and gastric adenocarcinoma.
- Gastric adenocarcinoma from H. pylori is found more in the distal than the proximal stomach.
- Risk of H. pylori infection is higher in old and truly poor people, not in the well-off.
- H. pylori is NOT associated with: 1. reflux esophagitis; 2. SCC; 3. eosinophilic gastroenteritis.
- It enters the body like amoeba or typhoid, by the fecal-oral route; about 60% of normal people have H. pylori living in the stomach without symptoms; only 10% develop PU; so not everyone with the bug has PU.
- Mechanism of H. pylori causing PU: 1. hyperacidity from increased gastrin level; 2. production of toxic products (VacA and CagA), causing local tissue injury; 3. stimulates a local mucosal immune response; 4. reduced duodenal bicarbonate, so less buffering of acid; 5. inhibition of antral D cells, so the body cannot control acid secretion.

### Diagnosis (p.828-830)
1. Invasive testing: endoscopy with antral biopsy sent for histology, culture, rapid urease test.
2. Noninvasive testing:
   - 2.1 Active testing: urea breath test and stool antigen test, which seem suitable for determining the result of treatment after eradication.
   - 2.2 Passive testing: antibody to H. pylori, which can be tested from serum, saliva, urine; especially serum, which is cheap, easy and fast.
- Serologic test (passive testing, antibody in the patient's blood): sensitivity 80%, specificity >90%; the result is reliable if the case has never been infected with H. pylori before; disadvantage: the antibody stays positive forever even if there is no active infection or the bug has been eradicated, so it cannot be used to follow up treatment (remember).
- Fecal antigen test: detects H. pylori antigen in stool; good sensitivity and specificity; used to confirm that after giving the drugs H. pylori is eradicated, i.e. that the drugs killed it all. (Figure: stool Ag dipstick.)
- Urea breath test: since H. pylori produces urease, which breaks urea into ammonia and CO2: give oral urea labeled with radioactive carbon (13C) and collect expired breath to measure the labeled CO2 (nuclear medicine method); sensitivity and specificity 90-99%. (Figure: 13C urea, urease, 13CO2 in blood and breath.) Because the test is difficult and expensive, it suits research, to confirm diagnosis and follow up treatment.
- Rapid urease test or CLO test: the test we know best: during EGD cut a bit of tissue from the antrum and test for the urease enzyme made by H. pylori; a common one is the CLO test: a simple method using antral tissue biopsy: if there is H. pylori, it releases urease, which changes the pH indicator color (yellow = negative; red/pink = positive); sensitivity 80-95%, specificity 95-100%; disadvantage: false negative if the patient is being treated for H. pylori.
- Histologic test: biopsy at the antrum stained with H&E: sensitivity and specificity 90%.
- Culture: suitable for treatment failure cases where we suspect drug-resistant bugs; the method is slow and expensive.
- PCR: biopsy tissue tested for H. pylori DNA: very accurate, but expensive and not widely available.

### H. pylori eradication (p.830-831)
- Eradicating H. pylori prevents recurrence of PU by more than 90%, hence the conclusions: 1. every patient diagnosed with PUD should be screened for H. pylori infection, since the popular test is noninvasive and not as expensive as before; 2. all patients with complicated PUD (bleeding, obstruction, perforation) should be treated for H. pylori infection.
- Recommended regimen (Schwartz 11th) in uncomplicated DU with positive H. pylori:
  - First line: 1. PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin for 10-14 days; 2. PPI + clarithromycin + metronidazole for 10-14 days.
  - Second line: PPI + amoxicillin + levofloxacin x 10 days.
- The regimens have a PPI as the base together with 2-3 antibiotics, effective about 90%; the antibiotic with the most resistance is metronidazole (remember).

| Drug | Dosing | Duration (d) | Eradication rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| PPI, clarithromycin, amoxicillin | Standard dose BID, 500 mg BID, 1000 mg BID | 10-14 | 70-85% |
| PPI, clarithromycin, metronidazole | Standard dose BID, 500 mg BID, 500 mg BID | 10-14 | 70-85% |
| Bismuth subsalicylate, metronidazole, tetracycline, AND ranitidine OR PPI | 525 mg QID, 250 mg QID, 500 mg QID; 150 BID or standard dose QD to BID | 10-14 | 75-90% |
| PPI, amoxicillin; THEN PPI, clarithromycin BID, tinidazole | Standard dose, 1 g BID; THEN standard dose BID, 500 mg, 500 mg BID | 5 + 5 | >90% |

## NSAID (p.831)
- NSAIDs are weakly acidic, so have a local irritative effect on the gastric mucosa and also cause a defect of the mucosal barrier, believed to be from NSAID reducing prostaglandin synthesis, the agent that helps mucosal protection.
- When prostaglandin falls, mucus and bicarbonate production falls too; also blood circulation at the gastric mucosa falls, causing PUD.
- NSAID is a cause of GU more than DU, and is dose related; about 10% of patients taking NSAID once daily develop acute ulcer.
- In a patient taking NSAID or aspirin, we should give a PPI or H2 blocker together in these cases: 1. age >60 years; 2. history of PU; 3. needing steroid together with NSAID; 4. needing anticoagulant together with NSAID; 5. needing high dose or chronic NSAID use or chronic aspirin use >325 mg/day.

## Antacids (p.832)
- Lower the pH of the stomach and duodenum.
- Usual dose only reduces symptoms; to heal the ulcer one must take it often, regularly, at a dose of about 500 (as printed), useful mainly for patients who have an ulcer break.
- Because the drug is alkaline, taking it long can cause metabolic alkalosis, and with a lot of milk, milk-alkali syndrome: hypercalcemia, nephrocalcinosis, renal failure.

## H2 receptor antagonist (p.832)
- Known theory: histamine, acetylcholine and gastrin bind the histamine receptor to stimulate the parietal cell to secrete acid; when the H2 receptor is blocked, acid secretion falls directly.
- First drug of this group: cimetidine (Tagamet), but given for long it causes gynecomastia and CNS toxicity (confusion); later came ranitidine and famotidine; when PPIs became easy to get and cheap, H2 blockers lost glory.
- Advantages: 1. heals ulcers faster than antacid; 2. reduces acid secretion about as well as a vagotomy; the old days credited this group as "medical vagotomy".
- Disadvantages: 1. gynecomastia; 2. many recurrences after stopping; 3. effects on hepatic metabolism of warfarin, theophylline, propranolol and digoxin.

## Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) (p.832)
- Currently the most effective drugs, e.g. omeprazole or losec, and lansoprazole.
- PPIs reduce acid greatly: the mechanism of acid secretion needs an exchange between H+ and K+ with ATPase as the enzyme; the exchange needs the proton pump; when the proton pump is blocked, H+ and K+ cannot exchange, so acid secretion falls.
- When acid secretion falls, acidity falls, so G cells release more gastrin; therefore PPI can cause hypergastrinemia (remember).
- Taking it 4 weeks heals 85%; taking it 8 weeks heals 96%.
- In the past it was believed long-term PPI caused carcinoid; now proven not true in humans (carcinoid occurs only in rats); the clearly increased risk is community-acquired pneumonia.

## Sucralfate (p.833)
- Forms a mucus coat that covers the mucosa, like colloidal bismuth.
- As effective as H2 receptor antagonists for healing ulcers.
- Suitable for pregnant women since there is no systemic side effect on the baby (remember).

## Misoprostol (p.833)
- A synthetic prostaglandin E1 analog (trade name Cytotec).
- Suited to PU caused by NSAID: the prostaglandin reduces acid secretion and is cytoprotective like sucralfate.
- Cautions (exam): 1. pregnancy: may abort; 2. diarrhea (a lot, not a little); 3. abdominal cramp.

## Duodenal ulcer (p.833-836)
- As the name says, an ulcer of the duodenum, usually at the first part; formerly believed to arise from hyperacidity; now H. pylori and NSAID are much stronger causes.
- Clinical features: 1. epigastric pain; 2. history of NSAID, aspirin, alcohol, smoking; 3. history of previous ulcer.
- Acute ulcer: a shallow ulcer, present less than 3 months. Chronic ulcer: a ulcer with rolled firm edge and scarring, usually present longer than 3 months.
- Anterior ulcer: usually perforates. Posterior ulcer: usually bleeds from a branch of the GDA.
- DU is found often in blood group O (cancer in group A).
- Epigastric pain: 1. DU: often pain before meals, since then acid comes out, more when the stomach is empty, e.g. at midnight; after eating, food reduces acid concentration, so symptoms ease. 2. GU: often pain while eating, since swallowed food stimulates the stomach to contract and rub against the ulcer; the more one eats, the more it hurts.
- Treatment: mostly medical: besides treating H. pylori, give acid-lowering drugs; combined treatment includes giving sucralfate; if no stop smoking and no stop NSAIDs, will not heal (remember).
- Diet for DU: before (when the author was an intern, about 40 years ago) GI doctors gave a sippy diet or scheduled feeding; now there is no specific diet.
- Hospitalization has little benefit.
- Surgical treatment for DU: mainly HSV, TV+A, TV+D, subtotal gastrectomy: choose a procedure that reduces acid secretion; Schwartz believes less is often better: choose the one that does not need gastric resection, e.g. HSV, TV+D.
- Giant DU: a DU with diameter >2 cm is an aggressive ulcer; if surgery is needed and the patient is stable, consider definitive surgery.

## Vagotomy (p.834-837)
- Cutting the vagus nerve = cutting the cephalic phase of acid secretion.
- 4 types (figure: types of vagotomy: supradiaphragmatic, truncal, selective, with criminal nerve of Grassi, celiac branch, hepatic branch, anterior and posterior gastric nerves, pyloric branch):
  1. Truncal vagotomy (TV); 2. selective vagotomy (SV); 3. highly selective vagotomy (HSV); 4. supradiaphragmatic vagotomy: a chest approach used in cases that cannot be done through the abdomen, e.g. previous abdominal surgery.
- TV: cuts the vagus nerve that controls the stomach and GI tract up to the splenic flexure, including the hepatic and celiac branches of the vagus nerve. (Figure.)
- SV: cuts the vagus nerve but preserves the hepatic and celiac branches. (Figure.)
- TV and SV reduce gastric peristalsis and also upset coordination between stomach and duodenum; with reduced peristalsis, antral stasis occurs, then antral distension stimulates G cells to release gastrin, so parietal cells secrete more acid, and in the end acid rises again; to fix antral stasis, TV or SV always needs an added drainage procedure to let food pass easily, e.g. pyloroplasty or gastrojejunostomy.
- HSV: cuts only the vagal branches that innervate the stomach body, preserving the nerve of Latarjet, which controls antral peristalsis. (Figure.)
  - Advantages of HSV: antral peristalsis remains as before, so no need for a drainage procedure, so dumping and diarrhea are much less; though it can occur, it is far less; HSV has low morbidity and mortality; in theory, if the surgeon is skilled and has done many, it is the safest.
  - Disadvantages: HSV is not a popular procedure in Thailand; in theory it can be answered in the oral exam, but if the resident has never seen or helped, do not answer HSV; the examiner may ask something that sends you to the examiner's pit.
  - After HSV, acid secretion falls, the antrum becomes alkaline, stimulating G cells to release gastrin: so post-HSV basal gastrin is higher.
- Criminal branch of Grassi: a branch of the posterior vagal trunk, which leaves the trunk higher than the EGJ; we may find it near the cardiac notch around the angle of His; the problem is if we forget and do not cut the Grassi nerve, incomplete vagotomy occurs. (Figure.)
- Taylor procedure: since HSV is a difficult and time-consuming procedure, Taylor advised doing a posterior truncal vagotomy together with anterior seromyotomy along the lesser curve to divide all vagal fibers that enter the seromuscular layer to innervate the stomach; said to make HSV easier; the author has never done it and thinks most residents have never seen it; do not answer Taylor. (Figure.)
- Hill-Baker procedure: posterior TV together with anterior HSV; not popular, so it has disappeared from textbooks.

### Incomplete vagotomy (p.837)
- One cause of recurrent ulcer; seen with TV, SV and HSV.
- Investigation: 1. Hollander test: the principle is that hypoglycemia stimulates acid secretion via the vagus nerve: do gastric analysis (acid output as a baseline), then inject insulin to induce hypoglycemia; if the vagus was completely cut, the acid output must not change; complicated and time-consuming, the patient must have an NG tube to suck acid for 24 hours and inducing hypoglycemia is dangerous, so no longer used. 2. Modified sham feeding: safer than Hollander: let the patient chew food and spit it out as a stimulus for hunger and appetite; if the vagus has been cut completely, the acid level measured or serum pancreatic polypeptide should not change; no hypoglycemia induction, so safer. 3. Old tests (see Maingot 9th ed.): Burge test; Grassi test; Congo red test.
- Treatment of incomplete vagotomy: 2 options: 1. thoracoscopic or supradiaphragmatic TV: cut the vagus nerve in the chest, since after previous TV in the abdomen the esophageal hiatus has been dissected with many adhesions; 2. open the abdomen, do distal resection of the gastric remnant with GJ anastomosis and the marginal ulcer, followed by new reconstruction; TV added if possible, if not possible no need.

## Drainage procedures (p.837-838)
- Include pyloroplasty and gastrojejunostomy. (Figure: Heineke-Mikulicz pyloroplasty; Finney gastroduodenostomy; Jaboulay gastroduodenostomy; gastrojejunostomy.)

### Pyloroplasty (p.838-839)
- A drainage procedure with 3 techniques:
  1. Heineke-Mikulicz: open a pyloroduodenal incision along the longitudinal axis, then close it transversely. (Figure: Kocher mobilization, traction sutures, divided pylorus, inverting suture, serosal sutures.)
  2. Finney: open through the pylorus, then make a side-to-side gastroduodenal anastomosis. (Figure.)
  3. Jaboulay: make a side-to-side gastroduodenal anastomosis straight away, without bothering with the pylorus. (Figure: Connell through-and-through suture, Cushing seromuscular suture.)
- The author: Heineke only; if Heineke is difficult because of dense scar, he would rather skip the pyloroplasty and bring up a jejunum loop to the stomach, a gastrojejunostomy.

### Truncal vagotomy and antrectomy (TV+A) (p.839)
- Cuts out both the cephalic phase and the gastric phase; so ulcer recurrence is lowest, since acid secretion falls up to 90%; gastrectomy technique is written in later pages.

### Truncal vagotomy and drainage (TV+D) (p.839)
- Cuts only the cephalic phase, so ulcer recurrence is higher than TV+A, but the advantage is no gastric resection, so morbidity and mortality of TV+D are lower than TV+A.
- TV+D is divided in 2: 1. TV+P (pyloroplasty); 2. TV+GJ (gastrojejunostomy): suits cases where the duodenum is swollen, inflamed or heavily scarred, so that pyloroplasty seems unsafe. (Figure: gastrojejunostomy.)

### Subtotal gastrectomy (p.839)
- Removes the antrum with G cells and the body with parietal cells almost completely, so acid secretion is very low; when the stomach remnant is small, ulcer recurrence is low, but with only 25% of the stomach left, morbidity from postgastrectomy complications is the most.
- Nowadays subtotal gastrectomy for PUD is little used; the author still uses it for DU cases that need definitive surgery but TV is difficult, e.g. many adhesions around the EGJ, or the lower esophagus cannot be dissected free, or portal hypertension with bleeding from DU or gastritis where treatment through the scope still fails: in such patients veins around the lower esophagus are seen dilated, so cutting them while doing vagotomy would bleed uncontrollably; in those hard-TV cases do subtotal gastrectomy.

### Laparoscopic surgery for DU (p.839-840)
- Used for intractability and perforated DU; especially perforated DU: the laparoscopy group recommends trying the scope; suture closure of the hole, then an omental patch, or apply fibrin sealant; they say it gives fairly good results, low morbidity and mortality, short hospital stay; the limits that existed before, such as washing the abdomen not clean, are no longer true.
- For definitive surgery, laparoscopy is now used for HSV and Taylor.

## Gastric ulcer (p.840-)
- Common at the incisura of the lesser curve; the greater curve is found in only 5%.
- Main causes of GU: H. pylori and NSAID like DU.
- GU differs from DU in that a GU has a chance of being cancer, so when finding a GU, biopsy to exclude cancer always.
- Clinical features: 1. epigastric pain: the most common symptom, 70%; 2. dyspepsia: nausea, bloating; 3. loss of appetite 40-60%; 4. vomiting from gastric outlet obstruction: found with prepyloric ulcer; and if vomiting plus weight loss, beware cancer; 5. bleeding: also beware cancer.
- Physical exam: do not forget PR, which may show black stool, or if a rectal mass is palpable at the anterior side, it may be Blumer's shelf of advanced gastric cancer.
- Investigation: endoscopy is the gold standard; when a GU is found, biopsy to exclude malignancy; do not forget the CLO test for H. pylori.
- GU and gastric cancer: GU is related to gastric cancer: cancer is found in about 5%; actually GU itself does not undergo malignant transformation, since in a GU case, cancer cells are found in the peripheral mucosa at the ulcer margin; so tissue biopsy should be taken from the mucosa around the ulcer, about 8 pieces.
- Endoscopic appearance: 1. benign ulcer: found at the lesser curve, smooth base, radiating folds from the ulcer; remember 5% of cases that look benign turn out to be cancer on pathology. 2. Malignant ulcer: irregular ulcer with overhanging margin.

### Modified Johnson classification (p.841-842)
- Dr. Johnson divided GU into 5 types; remember them since each location needs a different operative procedure. (Figure.)

| Type | Location | Acid level |
|---|---|---|
| I | Lesser curve at incisura | Low to normal |
| II | Gastric body with duodenal ulcer | Increased |
| III | Prepyloric | Increased |
| IV | High on lesser curve (juxtaesophageal) | Normal |
| V | Anywhere (drug-related, NSAID-induced) | Normal |
- Type 1: ulcer at or below the incisura, right at the lesser curve; the most common (55-60%); acid secretion is normal or lower than normal; believed to arise from a defective gastric mucosal barrier.
- Type 2: GU at the incisura together with a DU (found 15%); associated with acid hypersecretion.
- Type 3: prepyloric or pyloric ulcer; found in 20%, the ulcer is not more than 3 cm from the pylorus; also hyperacidity.
- Type 4: high near the EGJ, juxtacardial type; rather rare; acid secretion is low, like type 1.
- Type 5: ulcer at any position in the stomach, but a GU caused by taking aspirin or NSAID.
- Types 1 and 4 are associated with malignancy, low acid; types 2 and 3 are associated with hyperacidity.
- The most common complication of GU is perforation, but for DU bleeding > perforation.

### Giant GU (p.842)
- A GU with diameter >3 cm, usually at the lesser curve, because the risk of malignancy is about 30%, and the larger the size, the higher the risk.
- Point: a giant GU should be operated on quickly, not treated with drugs for a long time like an ordinary GU.

### Treatment (p.842)
- Initial treatment of GU is medical like DU:
  1. If H. pylori positive, treat H. pylori.
  2. If H. pylori negative and there is NSAID history, stop NSAID; if needed give the one least irritating to the stomach; to treat GU give PPI 8-12 weeks, then assess ulcer healing: if ulcer healing <50%, re-biopsy; if the biopsy is cancer, treat as cancer; if not cancer, consider surgery for the GU.
- Figure (algorithm): Gastric ulcer -> biopsy all quadrants -> no cancer: treat with acid suppression + H. pylori eradication -> repeat endoscopy: healed: surveillance (repeat biopsy if symptoms recur); persistent: biopsy: no cancer: surgically excise; adenocarcinoma: see cancer figure. Adenocarcinoma at first biopsy: see cancer figure.

### Surgical treatment for GU (p.842-)
- Depends on the GU type; do not forget to try to remove the ulcer, to send pathology to check cancer.
- Type 1: distal gastrectomy with Billroth I reconstruction is best; HSV with ulcer excision, which used to be recommended (see guideline above), is not used now because recurrence is very high.
- Type 2 (combined ulcer) and type 3 (pyloric channel ulcer): because they are associated with acid hypersecretion, the operation must add TV to reduce acid; best is TV+A, second is HSV, but the author does not favor choosing HSV since leaving the stomach behind risks cancer; for type 3 HSV is not recommended since recurrence is as high as 35%, because the ulcer near the pylorus often has GOO, a rather tight hole, antral stasis, making HSV in type 3 GU recur a lot; and importantly, in surgery for GU do not forget to excise the ulcer.
- Type 4: besides excising the ulcer, since the GU is high, the gastrectomy technique should be done as: Csendes for an ulcer within 2 cm below the cardia; Pauchet for an ulcer more than 5 cm below the cardia [as printed: "within 2 cm" and "more than 5 cm"]; Kelling-Madlener, but if gastrectomy cannot be done, choose Kelling-Madlener. (Figure: Pauchet's procedure; Kelling-Madlener procedure; Csendes procedure (esophagogastrojejunostomy).)
- Type 5: the main treatment is non-operative: withdraw the drug, give PPI and a prostaglandin analogue; if not better, or the NSAID cannot be stopped, operate; standard text advises TV/A.
- Summary of operations for each GU type (figure: intractable gastric ulcer): Type I: partial gastrectomy and Billroth I, or ulcer excision and HSV. Type II: similar to duodenal ulcer: truncal vagotomy and antrectomy, or HSV. Type III: truncal vagotomy and antrectomy. Type IV: if ulcer within 2 cm of GE junction, Kelling-Madlener or Csendes procedure; for more distal lesions, Pauchet's procedure. Type V: if medication cannot be stopped, excision and truncal vagotomy.

## Intractable ulcer (p.843-844)
- Currently this indication is used less and less to justify surgery for PUD; it is poorly accepted since there are very good drugs.
- Criteria for calling it an intractable ulcer must come from scope findings, an ulcer that does not heal, not from symptoms.
- Intractable peptic ulcer means an endoscopic-proven ulcer with diameter more than 5 mm that does not heal after PPI for 12 weeks, or one that healed per scope and recurred within 1 year.
- Before sending the patient to the OR, rule out: 1. cancer by repeat scope + biopsy; 2. gastrinoma by fasting serum gastrin; 3. hyperparathyroidism by serum calcium; 4. whether the patient is still taking NSAID or aspirin; 5. confirm H. pylori eradication really finished.
- In the past, the choice of operation considered ulcer recurrence, postoperative complications and long-term problems such as postgastrectomy syndromes; but now, with acid-lowering drugs and good H. pylori control, ulcer recurrence is much less, so surgery is less harsh than before.

### Intractable DU (p.844)
- Surgical procedure: 1. HSV is the procedure of choice, can be done laparoscopically; 2. Taylor procedure is the second option, can be done laparoscopically like HSV; 3. TV+P is third; 4. TV+A is fourth, but Shackelford 2019 chooses TV/A as the second choice (the author agrees). (Figure: intractable duodenal ulcer: highly selective vagotomy; if limited experience with this procedure, a truncal vagotomy and antrectomy.)

### Intractable GU (p.844)
- Surgical procedure after biopsy to rule out cancer: 1. operate according to the type of GU; 2. do not forget that for GU type 2, 3 add TV.

## Perforated peptic ulcer (p.845-)
- Sudden severe abdominal pain in the upper abdomen, then diffuse over the whole abdomen, tenderness to the point of crying out; abdomen board-like rigid; bowel sounds weak.
- Early phase is chemical peritonitis for 12-24 hours, then bacterial peritonitis.
- The patient often has a history of PU, alcohol, smoking; if no NSAID history, it must be from H. pylori.
- Chest film upright shows free air in 80% (figure: free air under both domes of the diaphragm).
- If the patient cannot stand for a film, lay them down lateral decubitus, left side; if free air is not seen, may try pushing air via NG to look, but beware: the hole enlarges and more contamination occurs.
- CT is not particularly necessary; we usually see only a subhepatic collection, but in theory CT is more sensitive than plain film.
- Mortality 6-14%, with worse outcome in patients aged over 70, with major medical illness before surgery, with problems of infection or shock, and delay in surgery >24 hours.
- Principles of treatment: nowadays the trend is that definitive surgery is much less: open, close the hole first, then think whether definitive surgery is needed:
  1. Before going to the OR fix fluid and electrolytes and correct underlying disease until vital signs are stable and urine output is good.
  2. NPO, NG tube, Foley, broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  3. Choice of procedure considers: 3.1 DU or GU perforation; 3.2 condition of the patient, strong or hanging on, nearing death; 3.3 the surgeon's experience: a beginner just learning to drive, or a veteran.
- Contraindications to definitive surgery: 1. poor patient condition: shock all along, unstable, many comorbidities; 2. very contaminated or already pus; 3. perforated more than 24 hours (Cameron cut point is 48 hours); 4. surgeon has little experience, unsure; 5. recent ulcer symptoms.
- Indications for definitive surgery: 1. hemodynamically stable all along; 2. few comorbidities, or if any, not severe; 3. contamination not much; 4. perforation not more than 24 hours, at most not more than 48 hours (Greenfield 2017); 5. no history of H. pylori infection before, or ever treated for H. pylori but still recurring or perforating while being treated (previously failed medical treatment); 6. perforation with bleeding or posterior kissing ulcer; 7. a disease that needs NSAID that cannot be stopped, e.g. rheumatoid arthritis, SLE; 8. patient who does not take medicine and cannot follow up; 9. an experienced surgeon (note: should have good luck too).

### DU perforation (p.846-)
- Poor-condition patient: only simple suture with omental patch, then see whether H. pylori is positive, with a chance of being positive as high as 81%; if positive, treat H. pylori after surgery.
- Good-condition patient: now advice is to do just simple closure, then eradicate H. pylori after surgery as well; the problem is that during surgery we do not know at all whether this patient has H. pylori; the decision to do definitive surgery needs clear indications; definitive surgery in the text recommends HSV (if able to do it); in a real exam the teacher cannot accept that and may agree to TV+P or TV+gastrojejunostomy; the author thinks one should not do TV+A, as it is unsuitable for such an emergency; in the case of simple suture, after surgery if H. pylori positive, give H. pylori eradication and stop NSAID and stop smoking.
- Laparoscopic surgery has been advised with many supporting studies, including RCTs from China: laparoscopic surgery has mortality, operative time, wound infection, intra-abdominal abscess, re-operation rate not different from open, but the advantage is less pain and shorter hospital stay.
- In the past, though laparoscopy was reported, most surgeons still advised open since washing in the abdomen is cleaner than washing through scope ports; but now laparoscopic surgeons say outcomes of open and lap are not different; they advise using the scope; it is necessary to open when: 1. the scope shows generalized peritonitis; 2. presence of shock on admission (go open right away); 3. Mannheim peritonitis index with total score more than 21.

### Technique of closing the perforation (p.847)
- Generally use omentum as a patch. (Figures: primary repair + omental graft; omentum, space between duodenal serosa and omentum, ulcer perforation plugged with omentum, duodenum.)
- The problem is in a large hole, more than 2 cm up, especially a hole over 3 cm: simple suture and omental patch has a leak chance of about 10%; so Shackelford 2019 advises using an omental plug in large holes instead of an omental patch.
- Omental plug technique: start by pulling the tip of an NG tube out through the hole; sew the omentum to the NG tube tip with absorbable thread; pull the NG tube back into the stomach so the omentum plugs the hole; or some sew a needle-tipped thread at the end, sew through the omentum, then pull it into the hole. (The author has never done the plug; he does a single patch: the patch technique: first tie omentum, then sew from one edge of the wound across to the other; when the needle comes out, tie, using the omentum as a pillow so that the tie does not cut through; no leak so far.)
- Other methods for repairing a large hole, whether triple ostomy to reduce secretions from the stomach and small bowel, or a jejunal serosal patch over the hole: the author has done them with no problem either; the conclusion is they are only for knowledge, not needed in depth. (Figures: triple ostomy: repaired duodenum, gastrostomy, retrograde duodenostomy, feeding jejunostomy; serosal patch technique.)

### Non-operative treatment (p.848-849)
- Used very little; suitable for patients who are hemodynamically stable, in good condition, with no clinical sepsis, no regular steroid history, little pain, no sign of generalized peritonitis; usually a contained leak or an already sealed perforation, proven by water-soluble contrast that it really sealed. The non-operative treatment consists of: 1. NPO; 2. IV fluid, IV antibiotic, IV PPI; 3. H. pylori eradication; 4. observe symptoms and abdominal signs with serial abdominal examinations often: if worsening, fever, rigid abdomen, operate; for a patient who is well, before feeding send gastrografin once more: if the hole is sealed, allow feeding and discharge.
- The text also suggests not operating on a patient with multiple medical comorbidities who looks poor: whether operated or not they die anyway, so let them go comfortably without pain; but if not operating, discuss with the relatives clearly.
- For GU perforation, nonoperative management is not recommended at all.
- Figure (algorithm, perforated duodenal ulcer): Perforation >24 hrs and contrast study confirms sealed perforation: Yes -> conservative therapy. No -> long ulcer history/previous H. pylori treatment: is the patient stable? yes -> omental patching with truncal vagotomy and pyloroplasty, or omental patching with HSV; no -> omental patching. Recent onset of ulcer symptoms -> omental patching, H. pylori testing and treatment.

### GU perforation (p.849-850)
- Poor-condition patient: 1. biopsy the ulcer edge for CLO test and send pathology for cancer; 2. simple suture with omental patch; the author wedge-resects the ulcer out and sews the edges together (believes this is more correct, since it matches Schwartz, Sabiston and Shackelford 2019); then give oral drugs afterwards.
- Good-condition patient: 1. biopsy the ulcer then close the hole; 2. definitive surgery depending on the GU type: use the basic principle, distal gastrectomy, and for type 2, 3 add TV.
- Figure (perforated gastric ulcer): Long ulcer history/previous H. pylori treatment: is the patient stable? yes -> type I and IV: partial gastrectomy or omental patching; type II: truncal vagotomy and antrectomy or patching; type III: truncal vagotomy and antrectomy. No -> omental patching, biopsy of ulcer and H. pylori testing and treatment. Recent onset of ulcer symptoms -> omental patching, biopsy of ulcer, H. pylori testing and treatment; for type III ulcer recommend antrectomy and truncal vagotomy.

## Gastric outlet obstruction (GOO) (p.850-852)
- A complication of DU and GU type 3 (found more than other GU types). However, beware cancer, since some reports say GOO is caused by cancer in 60% and benign in only 40%.
- Clinical feature: projectile vomiting after meals; if the vomitus has food from the previous meal it shows the stomach has not yet emptied; the symptom is epigastric bloating, sometimes colicky.
- Physical exam: dehydrated; upper abdominal distension; may see visible peristalsis running left to right; shaking the patient's abdomen can produce a splashing sound (succussion splash).
- Lab: vomiting a lot, loss of chloride and acid, alkalosis; the body tries to compensate by retaining Na+ and H+ and excreting K+ through the kidney (renal loss); remember the metabolic abnormality of GOO: hypovolemic, hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis. (The handwritten note on the page: NSS 0.9% only.)
- Diagnosis: 1. history and physical exam; 2. plain film: a large stomach full of food particles; 3. upper endoscopy to confirm diagnosis, do the CLO test and biopsy to exclude cancer.
- Treatment: 1. correct dehydration, metabolic alkalosis and hypokalemia, including nutritional problems; 2. NG tube decompression: the author puts in a large NG tube and has staff lavage out food residue every day until the stomach shrinks and NG content falls; 3. IV PPI.

### Severity assessment of GOO (p.851)
- After initial treatment until the patient is better, not as drained as when first seen, send for endoscopy to assess how tight the obstruction is:
  1. A scope can pass through the pylorus fairly widely: GOO is mild, from spasm or mucosal swelling: treat with drugs and H. pylori eradication.
  2. More tight but the scope can pass a guide wire = incomplete obstruction: try balloon dilation: 65% improve; if it recurs, try dilating again, together with medical treatment and H. pylori eradication; GOO often gets better; Shackelford 2019 says dilate 2 times; if still obstructed, operate.
  3. If completely obstructed, pylorus hard, tight, scar clear: do not waste time dilating; when the stomach is ready and the patient is ready, operate.
- Surgical treatment: before surgery besides correcting fluid and electrolytes, empty and lavage the stomach until clean to prevent aspiration, and so the stomach shrinks and the wall thickens, so it does not become atonic postoperatively.
  - If no difficult duodenal stump, choose TV+A.
  - If there is a difficult duodenal stump, do TV or HSV together with a drainage procedure; Shackelford 2019 advises gastrojejunostomy or Jaboulay pyloroplasty.
  - Schwartz 11th divides GOO into from DU or GU, which matches Sabiston: 1. DU: they advise lap HSV + gastrojejunostomy; second is TV+A or TV+D, avoiding pyloroplasty. 2. GU: distal gastrectomy, and for type 2, 3 add TV.
- Debate topics: 1. Greenfield 2017 does not separate GOO from DU or GU since the treatment is the same. 2. For treatment by dilation, Greenfield (like Schwartz 2019) does not trust dilation much, since after dilatation in patients followed only 3 months, only 40% could still eat and the other 60% return to vomiting as before; the author believes dilation is only palliative treatment; conclusion: GOO patients should be taken to surgery more.

## Bleeding peptic ulcer (p.852)
- Of course for an unstable patient with a lot of blood loss and endoscopic treatment failing or impossible, rush to the OR quickly: suture the bleeding ulcer to stop bleeding first; for GU do not forget to biopsy the ulcer; then close up, say a prayer; and if H. pylori positive, do not forget to eradicate.
- But if hemodynamically stable, per theory (read more in the GI bleeding chapter, new trend): Shackelford 2019 p.694 advises doing definitive surgery at once, for these reasons: 1. about 40-70% of patients are H. pylori positive, so a % of H. pylori negative is rather large; 2. intraoperative CLO test has a false-negative rate of 18%; 3. in cases not given a definitive procedure, the re-bleeding rate is as high as 50%.
- DU: suture the bleeding point, then do TV+P. GU: distal gastrectomy as the basic; type 2, 3 add TV.
- Figure: bleeding duodenal ulcer: suture ligation of the bleeding vessels, pyloroplasty, and truncal vagotomy.

# Gastric cancer (book p.852-)
- An aggressive cancer; the type covered here is adenocarcinoma, the most common type of gastric malignancy (90-95%); lymphoma, carcinoid, sarcoma such as GIST are written separately.

## Incidence (p.853)
1. USA: less, but Japan: high; believed to be diet (smoked, grilled food) plus environment.
2. M : F = 2 : 1, except cancer in Crohn disease or postgastrectomy: F > M.
3. Low socioeconomic status: higher chance.
4. Age 60-70 years; 90% of gastric tumors are cancer.
5. 90-95% of cancers are adenocarcinoma; another 5% are GIST; the rest lymphoma and carcinoid.
6. Location: 6.1 Asia: often distal or antrum; 6.2 USA used to be distal like Asia, but now proximal location has overtaken, since Americans are obese and eat a lot, so cancer of the stomach is found second, with the antrum only about half as much (as printed: 10-9 times fewer); 6.3 lesser curve is found more than greater curve.
- Classification: many groupings: sporadic, early, locally advanced, advanced, gastric stump cancer, hereditary diffuse gastric cancer syndrome.

### Hereditary diffuse gastric cancer syndrome (p.853)
- NCCN promotes it much: about 5-10% of gastric cancers have a familial component and 3-5% are an inherited cancer syndrome, believed to arise from CDH1 gene abnormality.
- Suspect hereditary when: gastric cancer before age 40; or gastric cancer before age 50 with first- or second-degree relatives with gastric cancer, at least 1; or relatives with gastric cancer together with lobular carcinoma of the breast before age 50.
- Such history should get germline mutation testing of CDH1 (E-cadherin), a tumor suppressor gene.
- If CDH1 mutation is positive, the cancer that arises is diffuse (signet ring cell) gastric cancer, from age about 37; so advise prophylactic total gastrectomy without D2 lymph node dissection from age 18 up.

## Risk factors (p.853-855)
- Acquired factors: 1. diet: high salt, high nitrate, low protein, smoked diet, low vitamin A, low vitamin C, nitrosamine; 2. infection, e.g. H. pylori, EB virus; 3. cigarette smoking; 4. coal or rubber workers; 5. RT exposure; 6. previous gastrectomy; 7. obesity; 8. GERD.
- Genetic factors: 1. family history; 2. blood group A (remember), not group O; 3. pernicious anemia with achlorhydria; 4. HNPCC; 5. Li-Fraumeni syndrome.
- Precursors: 1. atrophic gastritis: the most common precursor of gastric cancer (remember); 2. gastric adenoma; 3. dysplasia; 4. intestinal metaplasia; 5. Menetrier's disease; 6. pernicious anemia.
- Protective factors (believed to reduce gastric cancer chance): 1. raw vegetable; 2. citrus fruit; 3. antioxidants: vitamin A and C; 4. selenium, zinc, iron; 5. green tea; 6. aspirin.
- Non risk factors (not related to gastric cancer): 1. eating high protein diet; 2. BRCA mutation (it is breast cancer's); 3. long-term H2 blocker or PPI; 4. previous gastric radiation (the risk is from resection) [as printed]; 5. esophageal atresia; 6. pyloric stenosis; 7. gastric bypass for morbid obesity; 8. aberrant pancreas.
- Mass screening programs exist in countries that find much gastric cancer, e.g. Japan, China, using EGD with NBI or dye staining, finding early gastric cancer; USA does not, since the risk is less.

## Pathological classification (p.855-856)
- Classes group gastric cancer by gross features and histology, as a guide to treatment (a resident not knowing is not acceptable).
- Borrmann's classification: by gross finding seen at endoscopy, 5 types (figure: type 1 = protruded type; types 2, 3, 4 = depressed type):
  - Type 1: polypoid or fungating lesion, a mass projecting into the gastric lumen.
  - Type 2: ulcerative lesion.
  - Type 3: ulcerative lesion with infiltration.
  - Type 4: diffuse type, some call it scirrhous type or linitis plastica.
  - Type 5: cannot be classified into any group.
- Pay attention to Borrmann type 4, which is very aggressive, with 5-year survival <2%; the proximal margin when cutting the stomach for cancer is usually 6 cm, but for Borrmann type 4 a proximal margin >10 cm is needed, so total gastrectomy is usually done (remember).
- Lauren's classification: by histology (pathological classification), 3 types:
  - Type 1 or intestinal type: found 53%, usually at the antrum, associated with environmental factors, i.e. salty food, smoked food, preserved food, chronic atrophic gastritis and H. pylori infection; the gross may be polypoid or ulcerative; derived from the mucosa layer, cells fairly well differentiated, tend to form glands, so prognosis is better.
  - Type 2 or diffuse type: tumor derived from the lamina propria and spreading into the submucosa; infiltrative submucosal growth pattern; found 33%; usually at the body and proximal stomach; behaves like Borrmann type 4, very aggressive; gross: the stomach has a thick wall with no distension; if the whole stomach is involved it is called linitis plastica; the cells of this type are poorly differentiated, do not form glands; like to spread to nodes and peritoneum causing carcinomatosis, so prognosis is very poor.
  - Type 3 or unclassified: found 14%.
- WHO: more confusing than Lauren; subdivides into 4 subtypes: 1. signet type: the worst prognosis; 2. mucinous type; 3. tubular type; 4. papillary type.

### Prognosis (p.857)
1. Lymph node metastasis is the most important prognostic factor; that is why LND is extremely important.
2. Lauren classification: diffuse type has extremely poor prognosis.
3. Borrmann classification type 4 is very bad.
4. Cell poorly differentiated, no gland formation or signet ring cell: if this appears, the patient dies quickly.

## Advanced gastric cancer (p.857)
- Means cancer that has invaded to the muscle layer; most gastric cancer cases are advanced cancer.
- Clinical features: in early cases few symptoms but present for a long time; the most common symptom is weight loss, loss of appetite, eating little, vague abdominal pain, weakness, fatigue; may have GI bleeding or GOO or dysphagia; physical exam: if a mass is palpable in the abdomen, or ascites, it usually shows a very advanced stage.
- Diagnosis: if history and exam do not fit well, suspect cancer, send upper endoscopy; if a suspicious ulcer is found, biopsy around the ulcer edge, not a piece at the ulcer base, then send tissue to the pathologist.
- Do not waste time on barium swallow since it cannot separate benign from malignant ulcer; tumor markers, whether CEA, CA 19-9, CA 125, CA 72-4, are not specific, and not for screening either.
- Summary: the most important diagnostic modality is scope with biopsy (remember).

## Upper endoscopy (p.857-)
- Indications of upper endoscopy in general: 1. age over 55 years with new-onset dyspepsia; 2. high risk group who are likely to get cancer: 2.1 high-risk nation, e.g. Japan; 2.2 high-risk disease, e.g. FAP, HNPCC, gastric adenoma, Menetrier disease, previous gastrectomy, intestinal metaplasia or dysplasia; 3. any patient with alarm symptoms.
- Alarm symptoms (remember; Schwartz 11th p.1115): 1. unintentional weight loss, anorexia
  2. Persistent or recurrent vomiting; 3. progressive dysphagia or recent-onset odynophagia; 4. unexplained GI bleeding; 5. unexplained iron deficiency anemia without a source on colonoscopy; 6. palpable abdominal mass or lymphadenopathy; 7. family history of upper GI cancer.
- Relative contraindications: 1. inability to tolerate the procedure or sedation; 2. inadequate patient cooperation; 3. suspicious perforation; 4. recent MI, stroke, pneumonia; 5. coagulopathy.
- Technique: when a suspicious ulcer is seen, with a risk of being cancer, biopsy around the ulcer crater (remember again); a biopsy from the ulcer base gets only necrotic tissue. In the past cut at least 7 pieces; today 6-8 pieces with a standard-size endoscopy forceps. Cytologic brushing is not necessary at first, except when the biopsy is non-diagnostic (repeat biopsy is likely to work better).

## Staging tools (p.858-862)
- When the biopsy is adenocarcinoma, the next step is staging to plan curative or palliative treatment.
- Start with chest film, then CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with oral and IV contrast. Large USA centers advise PET/CT (skull base to mid-thigh) as in esophageal cancer, for assessing N2, N3.
- NCCN V.2 2018 goes further and advises PET/CT: if CT finds no M1, do PET to be sure there is truly no M1; but most standard textbooks do not make PET routine; send PET only when: 1. locally advanced gastric cancer (T3-4 or N+); 2. CT suspects occult metastasis; 3. to assess response in a patient given neoadjuvant treatment.
- CT scan: request chest, abdomen and pelvis with IV and oral contrast to look at liver, nodes, extragastric spread; enough to roughly assess T stage: T1: mucosa still looks normal; T2: gastric wall thickened but not beyond the stomach; T3: fat stranding around the stomach; T4: invasion of an adjacent organ. (Figures: T3 gastric cancer: no significant change in attenuation of pancreas and relatively distinct fat plane; T4 gastric cancer: pancreatic invasion.)
- Limitations of CT: 1. lesions smaller than 5 mm (the reader needs very sharp eyes); 2. detecting small peritoneal seeding; 3. lesions at the cardia.
- Less-done tests: laparoscopy, MRI, tapping ascites fluid for cytology (do if there is a good reason).

### Diagnostic laparoscopy
- Advantage: poking a hole and putting in the scope to look: 1. can upstage 23-37%; 2. diagnoses peritoneal carcinomatosis that imaging does not show.
- Selection criteria for laparoscopy (Schwartz 11th p.1146): 1. questionable CT finding such as an enlarged node, unsure if metastatic or inflammatory; 2. suspicion of small peritoneal implant or liver metastasis; 3. lesion at the EG junction (proximal location) with adenopathy on CT; 4. diffuse gastric cancer; 5. high risk for surgery, so that if carcinomatosis is present the patient need not be explored.
- NCCN version 2-3 years ago advised laparoscopy with peritoneal washing for cytology in patients whose imaging suspects occult metastasis or advanced cancer (clinical T3 or T4 or N+), because if carcinomatosis is present, resection would be unnecessary. But V.2 2018 says if clinical stage T1b or higher, do it in everyone except when M1 is already present (this view is still debated; theory says follow the 2018 version). In patients given neoadjuvant therapy before surgery, do laparoscopy with peritoneal washing as a baseline first.
- Sabiston 2017 p.1221 believes gastric cancer has occult metastasis 23-37%, so to avoid unnecessary surgery advises staging laparoscopy in every case; the author's belief is a selective approach per NCCN 2018, since Sabiston cites references 20 years old.
- If M1 is already shown (ascites, Blumer shelf), diagnostic laparoscopy is not needed (remember).

### EUS
- Thickness assessment is very important in cases thought to be early gastric cancer treatable by EMR; EUS can also see perigastric nodes, and if suspicious, FNA can be done. Large USA centers advise EUS in every M0; NCCN V.2 2018 advises EUS in suspected early gastric cancer, or when unsure whether early or locally advanced; also if the patient has ascites, fluid can be tapped for cytology to rule out peritoneal spread.
- Disadvantage of EUS: operator dependent; if the operator is poor the reading is poor.
- The author's view: if the hospital has EUS use it; wherever you do, scope is needed anyway; with luck it is early cancer (Tis or T1a) and can be treated by scope.

### MRI
- Results are no better than CT, since it is expensive and not widely available; do not send MRI.

### Peritoneal tapping
- If ascites is found, tap with a fine needle for cytology; finding malignant cells means M1 disease with very poor prognosis, so do not do curative resection (an important point). Schwartz 2015 says if no detectable distant metastasis, resect regardless of cytology (the author does not agree much; NCCN V.3 2016 says positive cytology is M1).

### Supraclavicular lymph node biopsy
- If a node is palpable, biopsy it: if adenocarcinoma metastasis and the primary is from the stomach, the case is usually inoperable (Virchow node), and the patient who asks what to do is told to go home to enjoy the rest.

### Biomarker testing
- E.g. MSI or MMR, for patients with inoperable locally advanced, recurrent or metastatic disease, and hereditary groups such as Lynch syndrome; HER2 testing helps decide trastuzumab for inoperable locally advanced, recurrent, metastatic adenocarcinoma; cost is too high; not necessary to know in depth.

## Staging system (p.861-)
- 2 systems: TNM from the AJCC (USA) and JGCA (Japan).
- AJCC/TNM 8th ed 2017: T and N are the same as the 7th ed 2009. (Figure: T stage defined by depth of penetration into the gastric wall: Tis, T1a (lamina propria/muscularis mucosa), T1b (submucosa), T2 (muscularis propria), T3 (subserosal connective tissue), T4a (serosa, visceral peritoneum), T4b (adjacent organ).)
- T stage (7th ed): T1 divided into T1a (invades only lamina propria or muscularis mucosa) and T1b (invades the submucosa); T2 invades the muscularis propria; T3 invades the subserosa but has not penetrated the visceral peritoneum or adjacent structure; T4 invades the serosa or penetrates the visceral peritoneum out to adjacent structures: T4a invades only the serosa (visceral peritoneum); T4b invades an adjacent organ.
- N stage (7th ed 2009): NX = cannot assess regional lymph node; N0 = node negative; N1 = positive 1-2 nodes; N2 = positive 3-6 nodes; N3a = positive 7-15 nodes; N3b = positive 16 nodes or more.
- M criteria: M0 = no distant metastasis; M1 = distant metastasis, or positive peritoneal cytology = M1 at once.
- Histologic grade: G1 = well differentiated; G2 = moderately differentiated; G3 = poorly and undifferentiated.
- The point where AJCC 8th 2017 differs from 7th 2009 is that the stage grouping was re-set: separate clinical and pathological staging:

### Node stations (Japanese numbering) (p.863-864)
- Stations: 1 right paracardial; 2 left paracardial; 3 lesser curve; 4 greater curve (divided into left group and right group: 4sa, 4sb, 4d); 5 suprapyloric; 6 infrapyloric; 7 left gastric; 8 common hepatic; 9 celiac; 10 splenic hilum; 11 splenic artery; 12 hepatoduodenal; 13 retropancreatic; 14 mesenteric root; 15 transverse mesocolon (nodes along the middle colic vein); 16 paraaortic.
- Figure grouping: N1 (perigastric): 1 right cardiac, 2 left cardiac, 3 lesser curvature, 4d (and 4sa, 4sb: short gastric/left gastroepiploic, right gastroepiploic), 5 suprapyloric, 6 infrapyloric. N2 (branches of celiac axis): 7 along root of left gastric artery, 8 along common hepatic artery, 9 around celiac axis, 10 splenic hilum, 11 along splenic artery. N3: 12 hepatoduodenal ligament, 13 retropancreatic (peri-duodenal), 14 root of mesentery. N4: 15 along middle colic vein, 16 para-aortic.
- D1 gastrectomy = remove only the N1 group. D1+ gastrectomy = N1 together with some of N2 (see table). D2 gastrectomy = remove the N2 group.

| Gastrectomy | D1 | D1+ | D2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distal gastrectomy | 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 | D1 + 8a + 9 | (D1+) + 11p + 12a |
| Total gastrectomy | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 | D1 + 8a + 9 + 11p | (D1+) + 10 + 11d + 12a |
- Remember: in cancer of the antrum, station 2 (left paracardial node) need not be removed.

## Spreading (p.864-865)
- Gastric cancer spreads by many routes:
  1. Direct extension: into lower esophagus, 1st part of duodenum, pancreas, transverse colon, liver.
  2. Lymphatic spread: via submucosa and subserosa; gastric cancer spreads by lymphatics up to 80%; may palpate a node in the left supraclavicular area = Virchow node.
  3. Hematogenous spread: to liver, lung, kidney, adrenal gland, bone, brain.
  4. Intraperitoneal seeding: 4.1 T4 has peritoneal seeding up to 40%; 4.2 to omentum, often with ascites; 4.3 to the pelvic cul-de-sac or pouch of Douglas: on PR a hard anterior mass = rectal shelf of Blumer; 4.4 to the ovary: a large mass called Krukenberg tumor; 4.5 to the umbilicus: a mass called Sister Mary Joseph nodule; 4.6 to the axilla: Irish node. (Figures: peritoneal thickening on CT; Sister Mary Joseph umbilical nodule.)
- Before taking the patient to the OR, understand the Japanese surgeons' thinking: gastric cancer patients usually die of metastasis by 5 routes; if we can block the routes cancer cells go, everything is cured and the patient lives long:
  1. Intramural spreading along the gastric wall: cut with free margin on both upper and lower sides.
  2. Direct invasion to adjacent organ: resect en bloc, e.g. stuck to the mesocolon, take the mesocolon out.
  3. Lymphatic spreading: a very frequent poor-prognosis route, hence the Japanese try extensive LND.
  4. Intraperitoneal metastasis: hence anterior bursectomy and omentectomy to reduce seeding.
  5. Hematogenous spreading to liver or lung: if single organ metastasis and resectable, they advise resecting it.
- Resident must know the basic lymphatic drainage of gastric cancer per Japanese belief, to understand why bursectomy and D2 LND are done: 1. lymphatic drainage follows the arterial supply, whether the cancer lies proximal or distal, lesser curve or greater curve; 2. from the stomach, it goes to join at the upper border of the pancreas, then the celiac axis group, finally to the SMA and para-aortic nodes; 3. SLN study does not work well yet, since gastric cancer has quite a lot of skip metastasis.

## Surgical treatment of gastric cancer: JGCA guideline 2018 (p.866-867)
- Figure (algorithm): Gastric carcinoma -> M0 or M1. M0: cT1: cN0: cT1a (M): differentiated, <=2 cm, UL(-): yes -> endoscopic resection; no -> gastrectomy D1. cT1b (SM): differentiated, <=1.5 cm: yes -> gastrectomy D1; no -> gastrectomy D1+. cT1 cN+: standard gastrectomy, D2. cT2/T3/T4a: standard gastrectomy, D2. cT4b: gastrectomy, combined resection, D2. M1: chemotherapy, radiotherapy, palliative surgery, palliative care medicine.
- Starting from the JGCA classification 14th ed and treatment guideline 4th ed 2014 (the current trend in Thailand), then the update JGCA treatment guideline 5th ed 2018.
- Start with staging: M0 or M1.
- In 2014, M1 patients got chemo, RT, and if bleeding or obstruction, palliative surgery.
- In 2018, the Japanese try to operate on M1 more; points to know (their advice is not yet generally accepted; the author would wait for NCCN to accept first): 1. nodes beside the aorta between the celiac and the left renal vein (16a2) and from the lower border of the left renal vein to the IMA (16a2/b1): give chemotherapy first; if it responds well, operate and do D2; if after drug it still cannot be operated, palliative care as in 2014. 2. Even with a liver metastasis: if only a single mass, do D2 + liver resection. 3. In positive peritoneal cytology but cancer not yet widely spread: wash the abdomen with plenty of saline, do D2, then give intraperitoneal chemotherapy followed by adjuvant chemotherapy; if peritoneal seeding is extensive, nothing can be done: palliative care as in 2014.
- Those M0, operable: the treatment depends on T and N:
  - T1a N0: mass <2 cm, mucosal lesion, well differentiated, no ulcer: choose EMR first (matches NCCN V.1 2019); if unsuitable for EMR, do D1.
  - 2014: T1b N0: invasion to submucosa, well differentiated, mass not more than 1.5 cm: choose D1; if larger than 1.5 cm, D1+; but T1b N0 in 2018 says SM1 invasion: advise ESD can be done; if SM2, SM3: D1+.
  - T1a-b N1: choose D2 only.
  - T2-4a any N: choose D2; but if N is bulky (nodes enlarged in large numbers), advise neoadjuvant chemotherapy first, then D2.
  - T4b any N: choose D2 (with combined resection).
- NCCN V.1 2019 on neoadjuvant treatment: give perioperative chemotherapy in cT2 or higher or N+ as level 1, while chemoRT is 2B.
- Even with the variation, the Japanese basically still resemble the old pattern, only the madness has eased, with more consideration (especially JGCA's M1). Surgical resection remains the only treatment for cure; even a large resection is still the best palliation.
- If planning to operate, do R0 resection: cut the stomach to free margin on both proximal and distal sides; for LN clear up to N2, i.e. D2 gastrectomy, and remove both greater and lesser omentum.
- Remember: open in, no liver, no peritoneal seeding, cytology negative: the Japanese lads hope for cure.

## Early gastric cancer (EGC) (p.867-)
- Means cancer still limited to the mucosa and submucosa only.
- Japan finds EGC up to 20%, since there is public surveillance with scope and indigocarmine spraying (chromoendoscopy): wherever suspicious, biopsy; so Japan finds EGC often; 5-year survival after treatment of EGC up to 90%. USA finds EGC only 10%.
- Before understanding treatment, a resident should keep these numbers in mind: from Sasako's data (thickness by EUS), the relation of T to N: T1 positive node 4%; split more finely: T1a positive node 1-3%, T1b positive node 14-20%.
- Sasako's data, positive node by T: T1 4% (T1a 1-3%, T1b 14-20%); T2 17-28%; T3 41%; T4 43%. Summary: the EGC suitable for endoscopic treatment is the T1 lesion only.
- Endoscopic gross pathology: Japanese classification of early gastric cancer, 3 types (figure: type I, IIa, IIb, IIc, III, III + IIc):
  - Type 1: elevated or protruded lesion >5 mm.
  - Type 2: superficial lesion, subdivided: 2A elevation; 2B flat; 2C depression.
  - Type 3: mucosal ulceration.

### Management of EGC (p.868)
1. Endoscopic treatment = EMR and ESD, suitable for a T1 lesion with gross type 2.
2. After EMR or ESD, if pathology returns showing submucosal invasion, lymphovascular invasion, or poorly differentiated histology, there are 2 options: 2.1 limited gastrectomy; 2.2 standard D2 gastrectomy.

### Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) (p.868-869)
- Inject NSS via the scope into the submucosa and look for the lifting sign: if the mucosa lifts up, it suggests no submucosal invasion.
- Criteria for selecting EMR patients: 1. tumor size <2 cm; 2. EUS negative node and lesion confined to mucosa (T1a); for T1b debated: if treated by scope it must be superficial submucosa only; 3. focal nodule, no ulceration, no lymphovascular invasion; 4. well-differentiated or moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma is tolerable, poorly differentiated is not accepted; 5. absence of other gastric lesions needing gastrectomy.
- The Japanese, by gross pathology seen at the scope: those with an ulcer they do not do; depressed type they still do: 1. tumor size not more than 2 cm in elevated type; 2. tumor size not more than 1 cm in depressed type. (Figure: EMR steps.)

### Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) (p.869-870)
- Cutting EGC out en bloc with a high-frequency electric needle knife; the selection criteria are the same as EMR, and it can be used with EGC larger than 2 cm, but should not exceed 3 cm because of the high chance of positive node. (Figures: ESD steps: marking, injection, cutting mucosa, dissection, proceeding dissection, complete resection; endoscopic photos.)
- The cut: inject a lifting solution (NSS) at the submucosal layer, then cut with an endoscopic knife, peeling the tumor out as a sheet; ESD can remove large tumors.
- Expanded criteria of ESD in JGCA 2018: begins to include ulcerative type G1, G2 with size <3 cm, even G3, G4 without ulcer with size <2 cm, reporting good results with positive node only 2%; the author waits for NCCN to approve it into the guideline first.

### Limited surgical resection (p.870)
- Used in cases unsuitable for EMR or ESD: a T1 lesion with no enlarged node on EUS, or a mass larger than 3 cm, or pathology returning with submucosal invasion but negative nodes; to do a D2 gastrectomy for these seems a bit much, except cases with positive nodes, which need D2 gastrectomy.
- Problem: how to be sure the node is truly negative; there are studies on the role of SLN in gastric cancer.
- Limited resection: removing part of the stomach; for nodes, remove only perigastric nodes. Methods: 1. partial or wedge resection; 2. segmental gastrectomy; 3. proximal gastrectomy; 4. pylorus preserving gastrectomy; 5. laparoscopic gastrectomy.

### Summary NCCN guideline V.1 2019 (p.870)
1. The author's view: NCCN is more acceptable than the Japanese (too dedicated, hard to follow); a resident must know both Western and Eastern, to get something from both.
2. cTis or cT1a: choose endoscopic resection.
3. cT1b: D2 gastrectomy.
4. cT2: perioperative chemo before surgery, followed by D2.
5. cM1: no operation; palliative treatment.

## Gastric resection (p.871-)
1. Consider by location of the tumor:
   1.1 Distal stomach: do radical subtotal or near-total gastrectomy, then reconstruct with B2; there is no need to do total gastrectomy because studies conclude no survival difference.
   1.2 Proximal stomach: must do total gastrectomy + Roux-en-Y esophagojejunostomy only; do not do just proximal gastrectomy, because alkaline reflux gastritis is common and very troublesome.
2. Consider by pathological classification: if Borrmann 4 or Lauren diffuse type or linitis plastica or scirrhous type or poorly differentiated cell type, believed to have extensive intramural spread, so total gastrectomy was advised, but nowadays total gastrectomy is done less; read the resection margin below.

### Resection margin (p.871)
- Quite varied, depending on each person's belief. The commonly used figures come from Papachristou's report: 1. margin 2 cm: positive margin 30%; 2. margin 4-6 cm: positive margin 10%; 3. margin >6 cm: positive margin 0%.
- From this data it is advised: in diffuse type, margin must be >10 cm; for intestinal type cut proximal margin more than 6 cm; for the distal side cut the duodenum 2 cm.
- Shackelford 2019 uses Lauren classification: type 1 or intestinal type must have margin >5 cm; type 2 or diffuse type must have a free margin of more than that (the book does not say how much), together with frozen section of the margin.
- NCCN 2019: in T1b-T3, gross margin of 4 cm or more; T4 should be cut en bloc. Greenfield 2017: proximal margin at least 6 cm and distal margin 5 cm, controlled by frozen section. JGCA 2018: at least 3 cm; but if infiltrative growth pattern, 5 cm, with frozen control of the margin. The author leans to Shackelford 2019.
- Local invasion: whatever organ is invaded or stuck, if it can be cut, remove it all, whether distal pancreas, spleen, colon, diaphragm; except abdominal wall: en bloc abdominal wall resection in a case with invasion out there has not shown added survival, unlike colon cancer with abdominal wall invasion, where en bloc resection of the abdominal wall does increase survival.
- Routine splenectomy does not increase survival; even if we need to clear nodes at station 10, 11, splenic preservation can be done; remove the spleen only if it is invaded.
- Routine distal pancreatectomy has no place either; even in cases that must have the spleen removed, Maruyama advises using a pancreas-preserving splenectomy technique, dissecting slowly, not hastily; do distal pancreatectomy only when there is direct invasion of the pancreatic tail.
- In patients who will need chemoradiation after resection, people become nauseated and cannot eat; therefore at surgery place a feeding jejunostomy.

## Lymph node dissection (p.872-)
- In the USA D2 is done more; Sabiston 2017 p.1225 also advises as before: formal D2 resection as standard practice for gastric cancer surgery, but some centers still do D1 resection.
- NCCN 2017-2018 hedges but leans toward D2.
- The number of nodes: obtain more than 15 nodes for accurate staging (as printed).
- In Japan, D2 means removing both N1 and N2 nodes, including stripping off the peritoneum in front of the pancreas and the anterior mesocolon; the number of nodes retrieved must be at least 15.
- Why Japan advises D2, from the belief: 1. gastric cancer that has been there for some time spreads by lymphatics more than by distant metastasis; therefore aggressive local treatment, all-out, is the answer for long-term survival; so they sweep every node station in the anatomical area clean; conclusion: D2 dissection is the standard treatment for gastric cancer; 2. comparing D2 with D1: Japan doing D2 gets 5-year survival 63.8% whereas USA doing D1 gets only 20-30%; 3. comparing same stage, Japan doing D2 has long-term survival about 10% longer than USA doing D1 in every stage; 4. D2 dissection yields more than 15 nodes for examination, hence accurate staging and adequate treatment.
- The Japanese therefore conclude D1 is inappropriate treatment; only D2 will do.
- Early on, some USA centers tried Japanese-style D2 and the results showed no longer survival, with even higher mortality and morbidity than D1.
- The Japanese then blame the USA that D2 in the USA does not work because: 1. a surgeon who can do D2 well must have trained for 1.5 to 2 years
  2. That surgeon must have done more than 15-20 D2 cases; 3. a surgeon at expert level should have done more than 100 D2 cases; 4. each year that surgeon must operate on more than 15 gastric cancers; 5. the hospital must have more than 80 D2 cases a year; 6. USA patients are obese and many cancers are diffuse type, so D2 does not give results as good as Japan.
- Most USA standard textbooks, including Schwartz 11th (written by older teachers), still advise D1; Sabiston and Cameron lean toward D2; NCCN 2019 and Shackelford 2019 seem to lean toward D2.
- Even the Dutch trial, which used to advise D1, turned around to advise D2, because after 15 years of follow-up the D2 patients had better survival and lower local and regional recurrence, while operative morbidity and mortality were not greater than D1; Italy, Germany, Holland do D2; USA and UK have no clear conclusion.
- For Thai residents, when the Japanese trend is strong, answer D2 only, do not hedge.
- The author predicts that in a few years the Japanese may advise D3; but in 2012 the Japanese split the operations into D1, D1+, D2 and no D3, since removing N3 (group 16) nodes was found not to add survival.
- Surgical technique of D2: read elsewhere.
- Pattern of postoperative recurrence: 40-80% occur within 3 years; follow closely because anastomotic recurrence is found often; also intra-abdominal recurrence at the surgical bed, peritoneum, liver; next, lung, brain, bone.
- Follow up: first 2 years every 3-6 months; years 3-5 every 6 months; then once a year. History, physical exam, CBC, LFT, chest film; CT or endoscopy only when recurrence is suspected; in patients treated with gastric resection, especially total gastrectomy, monitor nutritional deficiency, e.g. B12, iron.
- Laparoscopic D2 gastrectomy: reports from Japan, Italy, Korea, mostly from single institutions, say it can be done laparoscopically, but the number of nodes removed is unclear; the strongest paper comes from Korea but is stage 1 gastric cancer; so the overall picture awaits conclusions on oncologic outcome, number of nodes, operative morbidity and mortality.

## Postoperative management (p.874)
- Depends on postoperative pathology.
- R0 resection: 1. pTis or pT1 N0: surveillance; 2. pT2 N0: 2 options: surveillance or chemotherapy; 3. pT3, pT4 any N, or any pT N+: give chemotherapy.
- R1 or R2 resection: give chemoradiation.

## Palliative treatment (p.874-875)
- Criteria of unresectability for cure: 1. locoregional advanced gastric cancer: 1.1 lymph node N3 (para-aortic) suspicious on imaging or confirmed by biopsy; 1.2 disease infiltrating the root of the mesentery; 1.3 invasion or encasement of major vascular structure (excluding splenic vessels). 2. Distant metastasis or peritoneal seeding, including positive cytology from washing; once seeding is present patients usually live about 4-5 months; surgical resection should be kept only for cases with complications such as bleeding.
- Treatment: for patients who cannot be resected for cure, surgery generally does not add survival; we should send them to chemo or RT to ease the burden, except cases with complications such as obstruction or bleeding, where surgical resection is the best palliative procedure; if needed, choose a non-operative treatment first.
- Non-operative management:
  1. Obstruction: try laser, PDT, RT, or place a self-expandable metallic stent (SEMS) first; after the stent is placed, the stent usually lasts not more than 2 months, so patients for stenting should be expected to live not more than 2 months on average; if expected to live longer than 2 months and the condition tolerates chemotherapy, choose surgery: open or laparoscopic bypass gastrojejunostomy is enough, since palliative distal gastrectomy does not make the patient live longer.
  2. Bleeding: try endoscopic treatment first; if that does not work, try embolization.
  3. Pain: analgesics. 4. Loss of appetite: supplement as appropriate.
  5. Then look at the patient's condition: 5.1 still tolerable: try chemotherapy, give 2 courses to see if there is a response (most drop out); 5.2 depleted: end-of-life care.
- Palliative procedure: about 50% of gastric cancers cannot have R0 resection but must be operated on because of complications from advanced cancer; recommendations from NCCN: 1. limited gastric resection even with positive margin is acceptable (often linitis plastica); 2. no LND needed; 3. if obstruction: bypass gastrojejunostomy; 4. wherever you operate, put in a feeding jejunostomy and leave it. (Figure: obstructing antral gastric carcinoma; jejunum anastomosed to stomach proximal to the obstructing tumor.)

## Adjuvant treatment (p.875)
- Preoperative neoadjuvant treatment: its role is not yet concluded; phase 3 RCTs are ongoing on whether to give; NCCN V.2 2018 says give perioperative chemotherapy in cT2 or higher, any N, or stage IB or higher: give 5-FU, epirubicin, cisplatin for 3 cycles before surgery and 3 cycles after surgery; results better than surgery alone: 5-year survival rises from 23% to 36%.
- Postoperative chemoRT should be given if: 1. positive margin; 2. pT3, pT4 any N, or any T N+.
- (The author does not know which drug and dose they give.)

## Follow-up and surveillance (p.875-876)
- Recurrence after surgery is mostly found in the first 2 years, 70-80%; within 5 years 90%.
- H&P every 3-6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months until 5 years, then once a year.
- EGD has a role in cases treated by EMR or ESD; look at the mucosal surface; if abnormal, biopsy 4-6 pieces: 1. T1s: EGD every 6 months for 1 year, then once a year for 3 years; 2. T1a: EGD every 6 months for 1 year, then once a year for 5 years; 3. in cases treated with gastric resection, EGD when recurrence is suspected.
- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with oral and IV contrast: 1. in pathologic stage 1: when recurrence is suspected; 2. if stage 2 or 3, send every 6-12 months for 2 years, then once a year to complete 5 years.
- Monitor nutritional deficiency, iron and B12 deficiency, if the patient had gastric resection, especially total gastrectomy.

## Gastric remnant carcinoma (p.876)
- After gastrectomy, whatever the cause (PU or other), the remnant has a chance of becoming adenocarcinoma about 10-15 years after surgery.
- Mechanism: believed to arise from chronic bile reflux irritating the gastric mucosa until chronic gastritis, followed by intestinal metaplasia; now H. pylori is suspected to cause more chronic inflammation, but still less than bile reflux.
- Location of the cancer: often far from the stoma.
- Incidence found more: 1. women more than men; 2. B2 reconstruction more than B1; 3. gastrectomy for GU more than DU; 4. gastrojejunostomy type more than Roux-en-Y.
- Treatment: like proximal gastric cancer.
- Those whose stomach was resected more than 10 years ago, even if for a benign cause, should have gastroscopy for cancer surveillance once a year.

## Perforated gastric cancer (p.876)
- Found in 0.3-3% of gastric cancer.
- The patient presents with abdominal pain like PU perforation; most learn the hole came from cancer only from pathology after surgery.
- Treatment: GU perforation patients are often old and have many comorbidities, so it should be done in two stages: first operation only biopsy and close the hole, or wedge-resect the ulcer and close; after the permanent pathology is known, stage and consider radical gastrectomy + D2.

## Adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) (p.877-)
- NCCN calls it esophagogastric junction cancers (EGJ).
- The EGJ, Z line, true cardia or anatomical cardia has many names: it is the junction of the squamous epithelium of the esophagus with the columnar epithelium of the stomach; some call it the transitional area.
- This cancer, AEG, is a cancer within 5 cm above the Z line and 5 cm below it; the areas above and below the Z line have different lymphatic drainage patterns, so treatment is not the same.
- In a patient presenting with dysphagia, find the cause by upper endoscopy; then assess resectability with CT chest/abdomen, EUS, PET, laparoscopy as an additional option (selected patients) in cases suspected of occult metastasis, for Siewert type 2 and type 3.
- EGJ cancers to consider inoperable follow the same principle as esophageal and stomach cancer, but the special one is EGJ cancer with supraclavicular lymph node involvement, already settled (inoperable).
- The problem of AEG is whether to treat as esophageal or gastric cancer: the answer is J. Rudiger Siewert from Germany, who reported more than 2000 AEG cases, so NCCN 2018 uses the Siewert classification when speaking of EGJ cancer.

### Siewert classification (p.877)
- Divides AEG into 3 types focusing on the position of the tumor center as seen at endoscopy; with the AJCC staging system revised in 2017, Siewert type 1 and type 2 are classed as adenocarcinoma of the esophagus. (Figure: EGJ adenocarcinoma: levels 5 cm above EGJ, 1 cm above (type I upper), 0 = EGJ, 2 cm below (type II), 5 cm below (type III).)
  - Type I: adenocarcinoma of the lower esophagus with the center located within 1 cm to 5 cm above the anatomic EGJ.
  - Type II: true carcinoma of the cardia with the tumor center within 1 cm above and 2 cm below the EGJ.
  - Type III: subcardial carcinoma with the tumor center between 2 and 5 cm below the EGJ, which infiltrates the EGJ and lower esophagus from below.
- Siewert type 1: AEG whose tumor center is 1-5 cm above the Z line; so the length is 4 cm; classed as adenocarcinoma of the distal esophagus, which is common in the USA with much Barrett. Treatment for type 1: treat like esophagus: esophagogastrectomy + 2-field lymphadenectomy; Siewert himself prefers the transthoracic approach.
- Siewert type 2: the middle AEG, tumor center 1 cm above the Z line and up to 2 cm below it; so this type is 3 cm long. Treatment for type 2: treat as adenocarcinoma of the esophagus like Siewert type 1.
- Siewert type 3: AEG 2-5 cm below the Z line (subcardial carcinoma); so this type is 3 cm long. Treatment for type 3: treat as proximal gastric cancer, i.e. total gastrectomy D2 with preservation of the pancreas; some patients may need extra esophageal resection to get an adequate margin.
  - In type 3, Siewert does not remove the spleen but Maruyama of Japan removes the spleen in all. The author agrees with Siewert that there is no routine splenectomy, especially for a tumor at the lesser curve.
- For the proximal margin, Siewert says 3 cm is acceptable but the Japanese say it should be more than 6 cm; the author thinks the Japanese have reasons (and will go with the Japanese from time to time).
- If asked in the exam, the author would ask Siewert type 1 and 3.
- Agreed points accepted today for surgery in gastric cancer: 1. R0 resection is the only hope for cure; 2. in advanced cases, resection is still the best palliation; 3. to satisfy Thai examiners, answer D2 gastrectomy.

## Future trend of gastric cancer (p.878-879)
1. The Japanese have started sentinel lymph node study, similar to that in breast cancer, especially in early gastric cancer cT1b, which has positive nodes about 20%; if wanting to do little, e.g. endoscopic treatment or limited gastric resection, SLN helps be sure the cT1b has not spread to nodes. (Figure: cT1b (<4 cm) N0M0 gastric cancer: laparoscopic sentinel lymphatic basin dissection, intra-operative diagnosis of SN: SN metastasis negative -> laparoscopic function-preserving surgery (partial (wedge) resection, segmental resection, pylorus-preserving gastrectomy, proximal gastrectomy, endoscopic resection (ESD/EMR)); SN metastasis positive -> laparoscopic distal/total gastrectomy with D2 dissection.) But the USA does not yet accept this, since the view is that lymph node metastasis of gastric cancer has many skip metastases, so SLN has no clear role.
2. T4 lesion: if operable, resect en bloc everything.
3. Single liver metastasis: if R0 is possible, resect by nonanatomical resection.
4. Peritoneal seeding or positive cytology on peritoneal washing is considered M1; there are attempts to do peritonectomy followed by intraperitoneal hyperthermic chemotherapy.
5. The role of targeted therapy is still only for M1 disease.
6. Besides HER2, 3-4 more tumor markers are being studied, to find new targeted therapies that work better than Herceptin.
- We are used to the 2014 management guideline from Japan, but Europe has started to put tumor markers into guidelines; for added interest, here are the French 2016 and Europe 2018 guidelines.

- Figure (Europe intergroup guideline 2018, gastric adenocarcinoma): Operable stage T1N0: consider endoscopic/limited resection. ## Submucosal lesion of the stomach (p.880)
- On finding a submucosal lesion, the resident should think of: 1. polyp; 2. leiomyoma or sarcoma; 3. lymphoma; 4. carcinoid; 5. lipoma; 6. heterotopic pancreas; 7. early gastric cancer; 8. GIST.
- Lymphoma, carcinoid, GIST are written separately (residents sitting boards must know; medical students need not know in depth).

## Gastric polyps (p.880-883)
- Means a luminal lesion projecting above the plane of the mucosal surface.
- The most common benign tumor of the stomach; on finding a polyp know what type, and rule out the possibility of cancer; therefore all gastric polyps should be biopsied.
- Forceps biopsy alone cannot exclude foci of HGD or early gastric cancer; so polypectomy is the most suitable method, especially for a polyp of 1 cm or larger.
- In multiple polyps, removing every one is hard; advise choosing a few, the largest ones, for histology, which guides treatment, and at the same time biopsy normal mucosa for a CLO test to see H. pylori infection, since many polyp types, e.g. adenomatous polyp and hyperplastic polyp, are associated with H. pylori infection; especially hyperplastic polyp, eradicating H. pylori may cause regression of hyperplastic polyps in some.
- Polyps are divided into 2 groups: neoplastic and non-neoplastic. The neoplastic group has malignant potential: hyperplastic polyp 0.53-3% (as printed in the polyp group), while adenomatous polyp has a chance of becoming cancer of 10-20%.

### Hyperplastic polyp (p.880-881)
- Or regenerative polyp: the most common type (75%), usually in the antrum (remember); 50-75% found in patients with H. pylori infection with atrophic gastritis, from the reaction of chronic inflammation causing regenerative hyperplasia; the polyp is usually small, about 1 cm, dome-shaped with a stalk.
- Exam question: malignant potential: current data find it turns into cancer 0.53%; in polyps larger than 2 cm the chance of becoming cancer is 1-3%, especially multiple hyperplastic polyps; so remember a hyperplastic polyp of the stomach can turn into cancer.
- Treatment: endoscopic polypectomy then repeat EGD in 1 year.

### Adenomatous polyp (p.881)
- WHO 2012 calls it raised intraepithelial neoplasia; found 6-10%, usually in the antrum; 10-20% turn into cancer as in the colon, especially polyps larger than 2 cm, villous polyps, or with high-grade dysplasia.
- The appearance is sessile more than pedunculated.
- In FAP patients gastric polyps can also be found, and 10% of those polyps are cancer.
- This type, once diagnosed, needs polypectomy for pathology; follow up or surveillance without doing anything is a big mistake.

### Hamartomatous polyp (p.881-882)
- Rather rare in the stomach; usually a single mass at the antrum, with very low malignant transformation; but beware hamartomatous polyp in Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS), which has a chance of cancer, and more than 50% have extra-GI malignancy. (Figure: gastric polyps, histology, perioral pigmentation.)
- Surveillance recommendation in PJS patients found to have gastric polyps: EGD every 3 years; if a polyp larger than 1 cm is found, remove it; or if more than 5 polyps are found, polypectomy of the largest ones for examination.

### Inflammatory polyp and heterotopic polyp (p.882)
- Both types have no malignant potential (remember); however, biopsy is still needed to see the nature of the polyp; if the result is one of these 2 types, nothing needs to be done, except if it causes obstruction.

### Fundic gland polyp (FGP) (p.882)
- The medical doctors like to talk about it, since FGP is associated with long-term PPI use, so FGP is found often; the location is usually the fundus and multiple FGP are found in 40%; size from 0.1-0.8 mm [as printed]; since FGP is hyperplasia of normal fundic glands, the chance of progressing to cancer is extremely low. (Figure: fundic gland polyps.)
- Management of FGP: 1. withdraw long-term PPI; 2. biopsy to see the nature of the polyp; 3. polyp 1 cm or larger: remove it for examination; if many, choose the largest; 4. beware FGP found in FAP: it has a chance of being cancer, so appoint EGD every 2 years.

### Indication for removal of polyp (p.882)
1. All symptomatic in any size; 2. larger than 1 cm; 3. adenomatous type.

### Treatment method (p.882)
1. If it can be done through the scope, choose endoscopic polypectomy and biopsy the surrounding gastric mucosa; in cases whose pathology returns as cancer, do standard cancer surgery next. (Figures: endoscopic polypectomy.)
2. Benign gastric polyp: after polypectomy follow up with upper endoscopy once a year.
3. High-grade dysplasia: polypectomy alone then follow up EGD every 6 months; no need for total gastrectomy.
4. Eradicate H. pylori.

## Gastric lipomas (p.883-884)
- A submucosal lesion, yellow like ordinary fat; uncommon, about 2-3% of benign gastric tumors; solitary in 75%.
- Most are asymptomatic and found incidentally at scope or x-ray; except a big lipoma may ulcerate and present with bleeding, pain, obstruction.
- CT shows pliability, changing shape of the tumor, with low density.
- Endoscopy: a single yellowish mass pushing up the gastric mucosa; mucosa looks normal and smooth; pressing the mass with the scope tip leaves an indentation like a pillow: positive pillow sign. (Figure: positive pillow sign.)
- EUS: hyperechoic homogeneous mass with regular border in the 3rd layer (submucosa).
- Histology: uniform adipose cells with thin overlying mucosa, compressing the muscularis mucosa.
- Treatment: 1. no symptoms and mass smaller than 2 cm: can observe and follow up safely. 2. Indications for excision (scope, open or lap): 2.1 mass larger than 2 cm; 2.2 pedunculated lesion seen at scope: snare it off; 2.3 symptomatic lesion; 2.4 bleeding or obstruction; 2.5 growing lesion: beware liposarcoma.

## Gastric diverticulum (p.885)
- Very uncommon; the least common location of GI diverticulum; almost always solitary and often in males.
- Location: posterior wall about 2 cm below the EGJ, at the fundus or posterior cardia.
- 2 types: 1. congenital: both true and false diverticulum; 2. acquired: a false diverticulum.
- Clinical features: most are asymptomatic, found incidentally at scope or barium swallow for another disease; some may have epigastric pain; usually patients are 40-70 years; serious complications are rare: infection, perforation, hemorrhage.
- Diagnosis: 1. upper GI shows a smooth pouch projection; 2. EGD to rule out penetrating ulcer or tumor with central necrosis. (Figures: barium and endoscopic view.)
- Treatment: 1. asymptomatic: no treatment; 2. symptomatic: laparoscopic or open diverticulectomy.

## Gastric volvulus (p.885-)
- Twisting of the stomach; associated with paraesophageal hiatal hernia.
- In gastric volvulus the ligaments loosen, so the stomach moves and twists; in some patients, besides the stomach twisting, the splenic ligament is lax too, so the spleen moves and twists (wandering spleen).
- Reported association with eventration of the diaphragm and with laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding.

### Axis of volvulus (p.886)
- Two directions: organoaxial volvulus and mesoaxial volvulus.
  1. Organoaxial: twist along the longitudinal axis of the stomach; found less than the mesoaxial type; but more serious: symptoms are usually severe, since it is a closed-loop obstruction at both upper and lower ends of the stomach, so organoaxial volvulus is dangerous, rather urgent; if treated late, the stomach rots, the patient has sepsis and dies. (Figure.)
  2. Mesoaxial: twist along the vertical axis of the stomach; usually partial obstruction, symptoms are intermittent; luckily found more often, so doctors have time to breathe. (Figure.)

### Clinical features (p.886-887)
1. Acute: presents with complete obstruction followed by infarction and necrosis of the stomach, usually in organoaxial volvulus; the patient has the classic Borchardt triad: (1.1 severe abdominal pain; 1.2 upper abdominal distension; 1.3 inability to vomit, or retching without vomiting; NG tube cannot be passed).
   - Upright chest film shows a gas-filled viscus in the chest or upper abdomen; with barium swallow the stomach looks like an upside-down stomach. (Figures: chest films, barium, endoscopy.)
   - Nowadays CT is used more, to detect predisposing factors such as diaphragmatic hernia, adhesion, gastric pneumatosis or pneumoperitoneum, which suggest the stomach has perforated or necrosed. (Figure: CT gastric volvulus.)
2. Chronic: presents with recurrent epigastric pain, partial obstruction, usually mesoaxial type, twisting and untwisting from time to time.

### Treatment of gastric volvulus (p.888)
1. No symptoms: observe, only follow up.
2. Symptomatic: treat as paraesophageal hiatal hernia; laparoscopic repair is popular now.
3. High-risk, elderly: do gastropexy.
4. Emergency with strangulation: open the abdomen at once; if the stomach is necrotic, resect the necrotic part; if the condition allows, bring up a jejunum to reconstruct.

## Menetrier's disease (p.888-889)
- Many names: hypertrophic gastritis or hypoproteinemic hypertrophic gastropathy; a rare disease, worth knowing a little.
- No familial inheritance.
- In the past the cause was unknown; some blamed CMV and H. pylori; currently believed to arise from local overexpression of transforming growth factor alpha in the gastric mucosa; this growth factor stimulates the epidermal growth factor receptor causing overgrowth of surface mucus cells at the fundus and body, causing protein loss and increased mucus production.
- CT or gastroscope shows enlarged rugae at the fundus; the mucosa looks swollen and thick, twisted like convolutions or gyri of the cerebral cortex; with a picture like this rule out cancer, lymphoma, ZE syndrome, polyp. (Figures: enlargement of gastric rugae; CT with thick swollen rugae.)
- Histopathology: mucus cell hyperplasia.
- The disease does NOT involve the antrum (interesting, remember).
- Clinical features: in men aged >40 years: upper abdominal pain, hypoproteinemia, edema, diarrhea, weight loss; some present with UGI bleeding from achlorhydria and gastric erosion; hypoproteinemia comes from protein leak at the mucosal surface.
- EGD + biopsy in every case, since the risk of gastric cancer and gastric lymphoma is about 5%.
- Treatment: 1. nutrition, protein supplement, treat symptoms, acid-lowering drug, treat H. pylori; 2. cetuximab, an epidermal growth factor receptor blocking monoclonal antibody, has reports of success; some give octreotide too; 3. indication for total gastrectomy (remember): 3.1 uncontrollable hemorrhage; 3.2 severe hypoproteinemia; 3.3 dysplasia or cancer.

## Eosinophilic gastroenteritis (p.889-890)
- EGD shows a polypoid lesion; on biopsy eosinophilic infiltration is found. (Figure: EGD polypoid lesion.)
- Found at the antrum and there are no rugae enlarged; this point is used to differentiate from Menetrier's disease.
- Treatment: never asked in exams, just differentiate it from Menetrier's disease.

## Acute gastric dilatation (p.890)
- A stomach blown up like a balloon causes epigastric fullness or pain, with symptoms and signs from vasovagal response: pallor, sweating, bradycardia, hypotension.
- The danger of this condition is aspiration; x-ray shows a large stomach distended with air.
- Treatment: NG tube for decompression.

## Complications of gastric surgery (p.890-)
- Overall, complications of gastric surgery are found about 10-15%, severe complications about 1%.
- The term postgastrectomy syndromes is often met, but is not very apt, since patients suffer such complications as dumping after pyloroplasty without any stomach resected.
- Because gastric resection for peptic ulcer disease has decreased, postgastrectomy syndromes are now found more in gastrectomy for cancer or bariatric surgery such as gastric bypass.
- This chapter writes complications from operations for gastric diseases; complications in bariatric and metabolic surgery are in the obesity chapter.
- Complications can be divided into 2 big groups (only those often asked are written; for more read Michael W. Mulholland's surgical complications text): 1. technical complications: bleeding, leakage, obstruction; 2. anatomical and physiological complications: atony, diarrhea, dumping, etc.
- Guideline for guessing answers on postgastrectomy complications: 1. 99% are treated conservatively without needing surgery: atony, dumping, diarrhea; 2. those with obstruction, a tight hole: dilate or bypass; 3. a stagnant small bowel, a dilated stomach: atony, Roux syndrome: if needing surgery, resect more than before; 4. passing too fast, e.g. diarrhea, dumping: if surgery is needed, make it slower, e.g. reverse peristalsis.

## Esophageal perforation (p.891)
- Often perforated during TV.
- Treatment: close the hole then bring something to cover and reinforce: buttressing repair, also called reinforced repair (read the esophagus chapter on perforation).

## Splenic injury (p.891)
- The most common cause of intraoperative bleeding.
- Cause: usually the surgeon's hand pulling too hard during mobilization of the stomach, or during vagotomy, where the stomach must be pulled and the spleen goes along the side.
- When the spleen tears, pack and press first; while waiting for bleeding to stop, ask the resident or intern to fix it (the author's comment on assistants who pull retractors hard).
- Treatment: conservative: keep the spleen if possible; suture or wrap; splenectomy should be the last resort.
- Prevention: 1. mobilize the stomach gently, not thinking the stomach is a rough bag; 2. open wide enough for exposure when doing vagotomy or subtotal or total gastrectomy.

## Suture line bleeding (p.891)
- Often occurs within 48 hours: NG content is bright red, or active bleeding, possibly bloody.
- Use the endoscope first to look for the cause of bleeding; if suture line bleeding, most bleed from the gastric site.
- Treatment: 1. start with endoscopic treatment: inject adrenaline, cauterize, or clip as convenient; 2. if that does not work, open the abdomen, make a gastrotomy above the GJ line, then suture with silk 3/0 figure-of-eight.

## Inadvertent gastroileostomy (p.891-892)
- Occasionally met in a gastrojejunostomy where the surgeon grabs the ileum instead of the jejunum to join.
- Even in an obese patient or with many adhesions, that should not be an excuse.
- Symptoms: after eating, food goes down the ileum into the colon; severe diarrhea, almost bedbound with dehydration, loss of electrolytes, utterly weak.
- Diagnosis: GI follow-through, swallow contrast; the ileum enters the colon and contrast comes out at once.
- Treatment: revised anastomosis.

## Duodenal stump blowout (p.892)
- Found in cases of gastrectomy with B2 reconstruction, usually chronic DU.
- Occurs within 7 days, commonly 2-5 days after surgery.
- Symptoms: sudden abdominal pain like PU perforation, fever, fast heart rate, low blood pressure.
- Imaging: plain abdomen first, then US or CT showing fluid collection at RUQ; if aspirated it is bile-stained, murky fluid.
- Causes: 1. a surgeon who, even though he sees a pylorus and duodenum with much adhesion and scar, still tries to dissect to free the duodenum (read "gastrectomy: how I do it", last chapter on managing difficult duodenal stump); 2. afferent loop obstruction raising duodenal pressure very high; 3. localized infection making the tissue soft and friable.
- Treatment: 1. NPO, IV, IV antibiotic; 2. adequate drainage to make a controlled fistula: 2.1 percutaneous drainage under US or CT guidance: suits stump leakage with a localized collection; 2.2 if generalized peritonitis, must go to the OR, open again, dissect gently, the stomach and bowel are inflamed, red and friable; then place a duodenostomy tube to drain duodenal content and lower duodenal pressure, together with an NG tube or gastrostomy tube to decompress the stomach; do not forget a feeding jejunostomy, since NPO will be long.
- Prevention: 1. the surgeon must know his own limits, how skilled; if on opening he finds the pylorus and duodenum with heavy scar, stuck hard, and cannot dissect, there are other ways to choose, e.g. vagotomy + gastrojejunostomy or vagotomy + Jaboulay pyloroplasty; 2. if after exploring it does not look like the duodenum can be freed, one should retreat a little rather than go ahead, since the bile duct and pancreas may be injured (the author's story about the Bancroft technique he used to ask residents to watch; at that time there were no losec and no knowledge of H. pylori, so surgery had to be done; now with age prostate enlarges, etc.; rest of the aside not study content).
- (Author's tail of the stump blowout section: in such a case he now does only vagotomy + retrocolic gastrojejunostomy, with a PPI and H. pylori eradication postoperatively.) Remember: if we do not resect the stomach there is no duodenal stump, and no stump blowout.

## Afferent loop syndrome (p.893-894)
- Arises after gastrectomy with B2 or Roux-en-Y reconstruction where the problem is obstruction of the A (afferent) limb; if only partial, there is only vomiting of bile without food (since food passes through the E limb down below).
- Etiology: the A limb is too long, i.e. longer than 30-40 cm (the normal A limb is 8-10 inches long), and causes: 1. kinking and angulation; 2. internal herniation behind the afferent limb; 3. stenosis of the gastrojejunostomy anastomosis; 4. redundant twisted afferent limb (volvulus); 5. adhesion involving the afferent limb. (Figure: etiology of A-limb syndrome.)
- Presentation in 2 forms:
  1. Acute: symptoms arise rapidly from complete obstruction of the A-limb; vomiting does not relieve the pain; then the bowel loses blood supply, vomits blood, and the duodenal stump has a chance of blowing out, ending in septic shock and death; so acute complete obstruction needs urgent surgery.
  2. Chronic: the patient presents with bilious vomiting without food particles at the point of obstruction; may occur intermittently, e.g. kinking; after vomiting, pain usually eases; fortunately most patients are of this pattern.
- Treatment: start with conservative; then investigate whether it is truly A-loop syndrome, since symptoms resemble alkaline reflux gastritis, which must be differentiated since treatment differs; in cases with nonoperative treatment that do not improve or recur often, take to surgery.
- Diagnosis: CT scan with contrast is very good: the A-limb is dilated with fluid in it (figures: dilated afferent loop; CT shows massively dilated duodenum posterior to the SMA and anterior to the spine); another way: upper endoscopy cannot find the A-limb hole.
- Surgical approach: the purpose is that A-loop does not obstruct; if obstructed, do a bypass; look for the cause and fix it. (Figure: options.)
- Surgical options: 1. A-E anastomosis: join the A-limb with the E-limb so contents from the A-limb flow into the E-limb and go down; 2. lysis of adhesion if fibrous tissue constricts the A-limb; 3. resection of the A-limb if it is too long; 4. Roux-en-Y: cut the A-limb and join it to the E-limb in a Y shape; 5. convert B2 to B1.
- Remember: the surgical procedure options are to look at the picture of the gastrectomy joined as B2, and choose the procedure that after doing fixes the A-limb obstruction.
- Balloon dilation may be used in cases where the cause is anastomotic stricture at the A-loop hole.
- Prevention: 1. the length of the A-loop should be just right, not longer than 30-40 cm (learn in the OR by assisting often, or read the gastrectomy "how I do it" in this chapter); 2. retrocolic reconstruction in the gastrojejunostomy; 3. isoperistaltic reconstruction.
- Efferent loop syndrome: usually an SBO problem from adhesion; vomiting with food, since food is in the E-limb; surgical treatment is like A-loop syndrome.

## Alkaline reflux gastritis (p.895-897)
- Symptoms arising from bile and pancreatic juice refluxing into the stomach; in normal people too, bile can reflux back into the stomach without symptoms, but when the refluxed content: 1. is large in amount; 2. contains bile salts that damage the gastric mucosal barrier, alkaline reflux gastritis occurs.
- Gastritis from bile reflux is very painful and troublesome, a postgastrectomy complication that often needs surgery, and the results of surgery are fairly good.
- Primary: found less; a patient who never had any operation, whose bile suddenly flows down to the side and refluxes back into the stomach like GERD.
- Secondary: found more than primary; in those who had gastric surgery before: 1. pyloric ablation, e.g. pyloroplasty; 2. gastrojejunostomy; 3. gastrectomy with B2 reconstruction: the most common cause found (remember); 4. dilated pylorus.
- Symptoms: classic: burning pain, pain all the time, not colicky, and not postprandial pain; even if the patient vomits bile, pain does not go away; this differs from A-loop; the patient is thin, loses weight, looks more miserable than A-loop syndrome.
- Differential diagnosis from vomiting after surgery: 1. chronic afferent loop syndrome; 2. gastric stasis; 3. gut obstruction.
- Diagnosis: clinical + evidence of bile reflux from EGD or HIDA scan: 1. start with gastroscopy: generalized gastritis with friable erythematous mucosa with bile staining or bile pooling (figure: EGD shows bile reflux with gastritis); 2. CT shows no obstruction of the A-loop and helps rule out biliary or pancreatic causes; 3. HIDA scan helps demonstrate bile reflux.

### Differentiating alkaline reflux from chronic afferent loop (p.896)
| | Alkaline reflux gastritis | Chronic afferent loop syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | Burning pain unrelated to meal; continuous; vomiting does not relieve pain | Colicky pain after meal; intermittent; vomiting relieves pain |
| Vomiting | Bile; non-projectile | Bile without food; projectile |
| Anemia | Positive (from gastritis) | - |
| Weight loss | 3+ | 1+ |
| Malnutrition | 3+ | 1+ |
| Gastroscopy | Severe gastritis with friable mucosa | - |
| CT | No A-limb obstruction | A-limb obstruction present |
| Required surgery | Usually | Sometimes |

### Treatment (p.897)
1. Initial treatment is still conservative first: may try agents that bind bile salts, e.g. alum milk, cholestyramine, but the results of drug treatment are not very good.
2. Surgical treatment to divert bile and pancreatic secretion from the stomach; the results of operation are fairly good; Cameron advises operating after the patient's condition has improved.
3. Principle: do whatever so that bile does not flow back into the stomach (figure: options):
   3.1 The best is Roux-en-Y gastrojejunostomy with a Roux limb length of 45-60 cm, which diverts bile away from the gastrojejunostomy anastomosis.
   3.2 If the gastrojejunostomy anastomosis has many adhesions, unwilling to take down the anastomosis: use Tanner Roux 19 (figure: Billroth II -> Tanner-Roux-19, 45 cm).
   3.3 Join the A-limb and E-limb side-to-side.
   3.4 Use an isoperistaltic jejunal segment about 20-40 cm long to connect between the stomach and duodenum, called Henley's loop interposition; this method resembles a duodenal switch operation, which looks very difficult. (Figure: Henley loop interposition: 40 cm.)
- The author recommends Roux-en-Y as the first choice; if there are many adhesions he would do Tanner; if a resident does not understand, open the book for more.
- (The author adds that if Roux limb option is chosen and a resident does not understand, read the book.)

## Jejunogastric intussusception (p.898)
- A rare complication, found in simple gastrojejunostomy more than B2 gastrojejunostomy; the E-limb at the anastomosis intussuscepts into the stomach; a few case reports, until it appeared in SESAP 15, so it is a point to know a bit for the board even though the author has never seen one. (Figures: operative intussuscepted bowel; CT: intussuscepted jejunal segment lying in stomach and the site of posterior gastrojejunostomy.)
- Symptoms: upper gut obstruction.
- Treatment: no reduction; just operate.

## Dumping syndrome (p.898-900)
- A group of symptoms arising from eating high-osmolarity food, usually liquid or high carbohydrate, passing down into the small bowel very quickly, so that fluid is drawn from the intravascular space into the small bowel lumen, causing intravascular volume depletion, stimulating a vasomotor reflex and release of several gut hormones, causing symptoms: sweating, dizziness, palpitation.
- Dumping can occur with any operation, e.g. 1. pyloroplasty: loss of pyloric regulation so food passes to the duodenum quickly; 2. after gastrectomy: the more stomach removed and then joined B2, the quicker food passes to the small bowel, the more dumping.
- Classification: 2 types by time of onset: early dumping and late dumping; early is found more often.
- Early dumping syndrome: symptoms arise within 20-30 minutes after eating, when high-carbohydrate hyperosmolar food passes into the small bowel rapidly, so fluid shifts into the intestinal lumen, together with gut hormones released such as VIP, serotonin, causing hypovolemia (CVS): palpitation, sweating, hypotension, with GI symptoms: nausea, vomiting, borborygmi (stomach rumbling), abdominal cramping, diarrhea.
  - Sigstad proposed a diagnostic scoring system: score >7 indicates true dumping; for a resident taking boards, ignore the Sigstad score.
- Late dumping syndrome: symptoms arise 1-3 hours after eating a high carbohydrate diet, with vasomotor symptoms from hypoglycemia: anxiety, palpitation, tremor, sweating, tachycardia, wanting to lie down, needing to put sugar on the tongue, low blood sugar.
  - Cause: 1. high-carbohydrate food in the small bowel stimulates release of enteroglucagon, so blood sugar rises, the liver and pancreas release a lot of insulin until hypoglycemia occurs; 2. including carbohydrate being absorbed rapidly causing hyperglycemia which triggers insulin and catecholamine release.
- Diagnosis: 1. swallow radionuclide-labeled food then scan to see whether gastric emptying is truly rapid; 2. drink 25% glucose solution about 300 cc, test blood sugar and observe for symptoms of dumping.
- Treatment: both early and late dumping start with conservative treatment, giving drugs that slow bowel emptying plus diet modification (the most important); most symptoms resolve within 6-12 months; the main matter is what and how they eat.
  - Diet modification: 1. eat small amounts at a time, eat slowly, chew long, swallow gradually, eat often up to 6 meals a day; 2. eat a low carbohydrate diet, avoid milk and sweet drinks; 3. eat dry food, a high fiber diet, so food passes the small bowel slowly; 4. to make food pass down slowly avoid liquid food; 5. do not drink while eating; 6. no lying down after meal.
- Different treatment points: for early dumping give octreotide 100 mcg IV 15-60 minutes before meals to slow transit and reduce release of hormone mediators; found useful.
  - Surgery is used in only 1%; the indication is conservative treatment with diet modification fails, in early dumping; the purpose of surgery is to make food pass down the small bowel slowly, by taking a jejunal segment about 10 cm long as reverse peristaltic interposition between the stomach and small bowel, but this method has lost popularity because of obstruction problems, until the patient is taken to the OR again. (Figure: reversed jejunum peristalsis, 10 cm.)
  - Another way is a long limb Roux-en-Y anastomosis, with the jejunojejunostomy about 60 cm from the gastrojejunostomy, hoping the long Roux limb, which is often flaccid, delays emptying time. (Figure: long Roux limb anastomosis, 60 cm.)
  - The new surgical technique for gastric resection used to prevent dumping is pylorus preserving segmental gastrectomy. (Figure: pyloric preserving gastrectomy: anterior vagus, hepatic branch, pyloric branch.)
- For late dumping, just adjust eating behavior, no need for surgery; carry sweets or toffee to eat at hypoglycemia; in severe cases may need octreotide.

## Post vagotomy diarrhea (p.901)
- Found about 5% in cases with TV, but if HSV is done, diarrhea occurs <1%.
- Cause is unclear; many theories, only those needed: 1. postvagotomy there is no control of bowel movement, intestinal dysmotility happens often; 2. loss of receptive relaxation of the stomach; 3. rapid gastric emptying; 4. fat malabsorption; 5. bile acid malabsorption, since a lot of bile acid is found on testing; 6. reduced acid production after vagotomy results in bacterial overgrowth, so diarrhea.
- Luckily most improve by themselves within 6-12 months after the small bowel has adapted.
- Treatment: drugs together with diet modification cure >99%: 1. high fiber diet; 2. give loperamide for diarrhea; 3. give cholestyramine to bind bile salt in the small bowel; 4. octreotide: but some oppose, since it lowers pancreatic enzyme so fat is absorbed less until diarrhea increases a lot; the author concludes it seems unusable.
- Surgical treatment: used in <1%; the principle is to make food and stool pass through the bowel slowly: 1. reversed interposition of a jejunal segment 10 cm long placed about 100 cm from the ligament of Treitz (figure: vagotomy, pyloroplasty, ligament of Treitz, 70-100 cm, 10 cm reversed jejunum interposition); 2. onlay antiperistaltic distal ileal graft.
- Results of both procedures: stool frequency drops, but new problems of obstruction and bacterial overgrowth may occur.

## Gastric stasis (p.902-)
- Or gastroparesis: a problem of delayed gastric emptying.
- Normally after gastrectomy the patient should eat in not more than 4-5 days, and the patient is happy.
- Postoperative day 7 still unable to eat, vomiting all the time, NG output more than 800 cc/day (a problem for the doctor: nerves lost, mood swings).
- Symptoms: epigastric fullness, bloating, belching; on plain film, a large stomach; NG output over 800 cc/day.
- The key point is to rule out atony vs obstruction, since treatments differ; barium swallow cannot differentiate them well, since the stomach is dilated and contrast pools in it either way.
- If still unable to eat at day 7, do gastroscopy (be careful when insufflating; look at both A-limb and E-limb): 1. atony: a dilated stomach and the anastomosis still wide as before; 2. obstruction: the anastomosis hole is narrow, or if the scope goes down into the E-loop may find obstruction.
- Investigation starts with checking electrolytes, DM, hypothyroid, whether morphine is used, rule out small bowel obstruction from adhesion.
- If motor dysfunction is suspected, may need advanced investigation such as gastric emptying scan, GI manometry, electrogastrography.
- Treatment: 1. NPO, NG tube decompression to deflate the stomach (the best); 2. prokinetic agents, e.g. metoclopramide, domperidone, erythromycin, hoping to stimulate peristalsis, but most do not help much, though better than leaving the patient idle; but morphine should not be used; 3. nutritional support: choose between TPN and jejunostomy feeding; but anyway we do gastroscopy; if atony is found, place an NJ tube and feed through it.
- Most patients improve, especially those caused by anastomotic edema or hematoma, which resolve with conservative treatment; it takes not more than 6 weeks to 3 months; if not improved, re-operate (most advise about 3 months; wait about a month or so, not too little).
- What can surgery do: 1. high-frequency gastric pacing: electrical stimulation implanted at the proximal stomach to mimic normal physiologic peristalsis; must wait for new reports since few have done it and there are no RCTs; 2. resect the stomach to be smaller so food goes into the small bowel faster, depending on the previous procedure: 2.1 previously TV + drainage: do subtotal gastrectomy with B2 and Braun enteroenterostomy; 2.2 previously subtotal: now near-total or total, then reconstruct as Roux-en-Y.
  3. If adding a feeding jejunostomy, leave it in place for a while, in case it is needed (the author's aside).
- If one does not want to meet postoperative gastric atony, before surgery prepare the stomach well: place a large NG tube, wash out all food particles, then connect the NG tube to suction until the stomach is flat, correct fluid and electrolytes; at laparotomy a flat stomach with thick firm wall is happy; but if the stomach is dilated, the wall does not feel thick or firm: the author avoids vagotomy and does only gastric resection of 50-75%.

## Roux stasis syndrome (p.903)
- Atony of the Roux limb without obstruction; the cause is not clear but believed: 1. at gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y reconstruction, the Roux limb was made too long, causing Roux limb stasis; 2. or abnormal peristalsis of the Roux limb itself; 3. or found in patients with vagotomy who still have a lot of gastric remnant.
- Symptoms: vomiting, chronic postprandial epigastric pain, bloating, weight loss.
- Investigation: 1. upper endoscopy to rule out obstruction: the remaining stomach is dilated with food residue, while the anastomosis is still patent, and the Roux limb is dilated; 2. UGI study or CT shows delayed gastric emptying of both solid and liquid diet; the stomach and Roux limb are dilated with no evidence of mechanical obstruction; 3. gastric emptying scan looks like the UGI study; 4. GI motility test shows abnormal motility of the Roux limb.
- Treatment: 1. start conservative like gastric atony, which works in about 50%; 2. if surgery is needed, the principle is the same as gastric atony; depending on the size of the remaining stomach and the status of the Roux limb: 2.1 in a case with distal gastrectomy left behind: subtotal or, if the first was subtotal, total gastrectomy, then revise the Roux limb anew, shortening it to 40-50 cm, or change to B2 with Braun enteroenterostomy; 2.2 pacing at the stomach or small bowel is still in trial.

## Promotility agents (p.903-904)
- Drugs used to treat atony by accelerating gastric emptying; the resident should remember about 5 of them (remember the drug names and the mechanism of erythromycin):
  1. Metoclopramide and domperidone: dopamine antagonists.
  2. Erythromycin: motilin agonist, stimulating GI smooth muscle contraction; given 7-10 days improves gastric emptying.
  3. Bethanechol and neostigmine: cholinergic agonists.

## Small gastric remnant (p.904)
- Early satiety: full quickly because the stomach has been resected to a tiny remnant; weight loss.
- Diagnose by upper endoscopy.
- Treatment: 1. eat small amounts at a time but often; 2. if surgery is needed, the option is to construct a pouch using a jejunal pouch construction.

## Recurrent marginal ulcer (p.904-905)
- After a small bowel is joined to the stomach, an ulcer arises again; the ulcer is usually on the small bowel side.
- Causes: 1. hypergastrinemia, e.g. ZE, antral G cell hyperplasia, retained antrum; 2. H. pylori infection; 3. technique problems, e.g. incomplete vagotomy, inadequate resection, inadequate drainage; 4. taking NSAID; 5. others, e.g. HPT with hypercalcemia.
- Investigation: 1. talk to the pathologist to review the slide for whether there was incomplete vagotomy or retained antrum; 2. fasting serum gastrin to rule out ZE; 3. serum calcium to rule out HPT, which comes with peptic ulcer; 4. upper endoscopy + biopsy to rule out H. pylori and see whether the anastomosis is still patent.
- Antral G cell hyperplasia: to differentiate it from ZE, inject IV bombesin stimulation: if after bombesin the serum gastrin rises, it is G cell hyperplasia, whereas in ZE the serum gastrin does not change after bombesin; another way: gastrin in G cell hyperplasia is high only after meals, whereas ZE is high all the time.
- Retained antrum: usually arises when we do antrectomy and the antrum on the duodenal side is not all cut out, so a portion of antrum is stuck at the duodenal stump, so that antrum is bathed in bile and pancreatic juice all the time, which stimulates gastrin secretion.
  - We can separate hypergastrinemia of retained antrum from gastrinoma by secretin infusion: if gastrinoma, gastrin rises; in retained antrum the serum gastrin does not change.
- Treatment: 1. conservative as before; if fail, then operate; 2. operation: resect more stomach; if the remaining stomach is less than 30%, reconstruct as Roux-en-Y.

## Anemia after gastrectomy (p.905-906)
- Iron deficiency anemia: (figure: hypochromic microcytic anemia.) The most common cause of iron deficiency has 2 mechanisms: 1. iron is absorbed at the duodenum; with B2 reconstruction, iron does not pass through the duodenum; 2. the iron we eat is in ferrous form, which combines with acid and becomes ferric, a form that can be absorbed; after gastrectomy the acid drops, so ferric falls too.
  - Peripheral smear: hypochromic microcytic anemia, RBC smaller than a monocyte (nucleus). Treatment: iron supplement.
- B12 deficiency anemia: (figure: normal blood cells vs megaloblastic anemia cells.) Ingested B12 binds with gastric parietal cell intrinsic factor into a cobalamin-intrinsic factor complex, to protect it from hydrolysis by pancreatic enzyme; the complex is then absorbed at the terminal ileum, which has receptors enabling B12 absorption.
  - The body stores B12 in the liver 2000-5000 mcg and uses 1-3 mcg a day, so symptoms of pernicious anemia take about 3-5 years to occur; since folate is also absorbed at the duodenum, if folate is deficient too symptoms come faster; besides anemia, the tongue of a patient lacking B12 is red, smooth from atrophy of the papillae.
  - Peripheral smear: RBC hypochromic (as printed) megaloblastic anemia, RBC larger than a monocyte; the big red cells arise from B12 deficiency (a coenzyme that helps make DNA); without B12 there is no DNA, so red cells do not divide; so the red cells coming out of the marrow are huge megaloblasts with low hemoglobin.
  - Treatment: inject B12 1 mg once a month, or oral 1 mg daily for life, together with folate.

## Bone disease (p.906)
- Calcium is absorbed at the duodenum; patients become calcium deficient and may get osteoporosis because: 1. B2 reconstruction means calcium does not pass the duodenum; 2. reduced acid, and the blind loop with bacterial overgrowth, interferes with calcium absorption.
- Patients who should have skeletal monitoring: 1. women older than 50 years or men older than 65 years; 2. smoking; 3. history of previous fracture.
- Treatment: give calcium with vitamin D.

## Gallstone (p.906-)
- TV causes gallbladder dysmotility, so bile stasis and stones form easily.
- If no gallstone is found, prophylactic cholecystectomy does not seem reasonable.
- But if there is any of these conditions cholecystectomy is indicated: 1. at operation, the GB looks abnormal, e.g. thick white with adhesion, showing previous inflammation; 2. at operation gallstones can be felt, or preop US shows stones.

| Procedure | % of gastric resection |
|---|---|
| Antrectomy or partial gastrectomy | 35-40 |
| Distal or hemigastrectomy | 50 |
| Subtotal gastrectomy | 75 |
| Neartotal gastrectomy | 95 |
| Total gastrectomy | 100 |
(Figure: stomach anatomy: esophagus, cardia, angle of His, fundus, body, lesser curvature, incisura, greater curvature, zone of transition, antrum, pylorus, duodenal bulb; vascular map with 50% and 75% resection lines through right gastroepiploic, left gastroepiploic, vasa breves.)
- The author: nowadays residents get less chance to assist or do gastrectomy than 30 years ago; in those days H. pylori was unknown and there was no endoscopic treatment for UGI bleeding, so residents cut the stomach until bored and used single hand-sewing with no staplers to play with.
- For gastrectomy he mixes the techniques of Oliver Beahrs, Sawyer and Zollinger into his own technique.
- Incision: in an obese patient with previous surgery and many adhesions: open wide, enter with no tightness, since at closing he lets a young staff or chief resident help close; then he checks. Position: supine; if the stomach is high, may raise the patient's head slightly above the feet.
- Freeing the stomach: open the greater curve and lesser curve along avascular planes, use a penrose drain looped around to pull while mobilizing the stomach down and to the right side; beware splenic injury.
- (Figure: pyloric region: right gastric artery, pyloric vein, pancreaticoduodenal artery, right gastroepiploic artery ligated, pancreas, middle colic artery.)
- Gradually free the stomach toward the distal side; assess the duodenum: after cutting the stomach, will the duodenum be easy to close? At the least one must free the duodenum 1-2 cm below the pylorus; if it looks hard to close the duodenal stump, the author considers a bypass rather than dissecting in.
- If the duodenal stump can be closed without difficulty, he always sews it to the anterior surface of the pancreas, like the Nissen technique.
- At the posterior wall of the stomach it may stick to the pancreas: do not rush, use a finger to gently sweep.
- When separating the stomach from the transverse colon, take care of the middle colic artery; in some cases it sticks to the stomach so much that the author once cut it and the colon turned black.
- Reconstruction: per theory B1 is preferred, but he uses B2 only; for the gastrojejunostomy anastomosis he likes the Finsterer-Hofmeister method; on the gastric side he ties or sews bleeding points with silk 3/0 to be sure there is no bleeding at the anastomosis postoperatively. (Figure: Billroth I and Billroth II.)
- B2 reconstruction (figure of the historical types: Billroth II 1885, Kronlein 1887, von Eiselsberg 1889, Braun 1892, Roux 1893, Reichel 1908/Polya 1911, Finsterer-Hofmeister 1914, Balfour 1917, Moynihan 1923): the author uses type G (Finsterer-Hofmeister).
  - A-limb: take the shortest proximal jejunum so there is no kink, but do not let it be tight; before joining jejunum to stomach always identify the ligament of Treitz so that the part picked up is jejunum, not ileum.
  - In gastric resection whether benign or cancer, he places the gastrojejunostomy antecolic.
  - But in a bypass gastrojejunostomy for a benign case without resecting the stomach, he places it retrocolic, and closes the gap by suturing the mesocolon to the stomach, because if it is sewn to the jejunum, obstruction is likely.
  - If more than 80% of the stomach is resected, he makes the gastrojejunostomy a Roux-en-Y.
  - The NG tube he places with its tip in the A-limb, but cuts extra side holes above and below the gastrojejunal anastomosis, and suction with Gomco decompresses both the stomach and jejunum.
- Whoever joins B1 does so as convenient. (Figure: B1 reconstruction types: Billroth 1881, Kocher 1890, Kutscha-Lisberg 1925, von Haberer 1920/1922, Finney 1923, Winkelbauer 1927, Schoemaker 1911, Harkins-Nyhus 1960.)

## Difficult duodenal stump (p.910-912)
- Even though gastrectomy will decrease, certain situations force a decision to cut the stomach, especially obstruction and cancer.
- The problem to beware most: those with a large, hard, firm ulcer, scar or many adhesions around it; in these, when the stomach is cut and the stump closed, it is hard to sew, or leaks, or blows out.
- Normally we should free the duodenum from below the pyloric ring; we can find the position of the pylorus by palpation, thick, and see the vein of Mayo from the anterior side: 1. if free 2 cm and the tissue is not hard and invertible, that shows the case is not difficult; 2. if the tissue is hard, firm, and cannot be mobilized past the pylorus: 2.1 a new hand: consider alternative procedures such as bypass gastrojejunostomy; 2.2 but if gastrectomy is necessary, assess your own experience, are you good enough; if not sure do not dive in, call a mother (i.e. a senior) to help: the senior who did well.
- Bancroft-Plenk technique: suitable for the case where the duodenum is stuck and cannot be lifted at all. (Figures.) Method: 1. adequate mobilization of the greater and lesser curve; 2. preserve the right gastric and right gastroepiploic artery; 3. circular incision about 7 cm from the pylorus on the upper side; 4. once the incision goes through the seromuscular coat of the antrum, grasp the seromuscular edge with a Babcock and hold it taut; 5. the author prefers to inject NSS into the submucosa to lift the mucosa, and grasps the mucosa with an Allis, rotating like a screw; 6. then dissect the mucosa down gradually until reaching the pyloric ring, and put a pursestring suture of chromic 3/0; 7. cut the mucosal tube out then invert the seromuscular layer. The author likes Bancroft since he is used to it, but a new hand must beware, when peeling the mucosa it bleeds diffusely, and the more one pokes and injects, the more it tears; nothing wrong in not knowing how.
- Nissen technique (figure): used in cases where the duodenum can still be mobilized past the pyloric ring, but is stuck at a posterior ulcer that has penetrated into the pancreas. Method: 1. Kocherization and mobilization of the duodenum posteriorly as far as possible; 2. cut the duodenum leaving the ulcer crater in situ on the pancreas; 3. sew the free edge of the duodenum to the edge of the ulcer; 4. sew the seromuscular layer of the duodenum to the pancreatic capsule.
- Lahey technique (figure): used in cases where the ulcer is stuck to the bile duct; frightening, since mobilizing the antrum might injure the CBD: he advises exploring the CBD then putting a Bake pass down as a guide while dissecting. (The author writes only this much, thinking this method is not so good.)
- Duodenostomy technique (figure: tube duodenostomy): if worried about leak or duodenal stump blowout, make it a controlled fistula at once: push a Foley balloon catheter size 14-16 into the stump, which has been closed over it; then inflate the balloon so it fits; sew a pursestring around the Foley to prevent leakage; then cover with omentum once more; leave the Foley 2 weeks then remove the tube.
- Duodenojejunal anastomosis (figure: stomach, jejunum, pancreas, duodenum, SMA; 10-15 cm; 15-20 cm): this technique is borrowed from surgery for SMA syndrome and adapted for a difficult duodenal stump: connect a Roux-en-Y jejunum to the duodenal stump, the purpose being to reduce tension at the stump; some people also place a T-tube into the CBD for bile drainage; (the author praises such surgeons' stamina).
- Note: if the duodenum looks difficult to close, the author asks himself whether he can avoid cutting the stomach; if not, change to another method; if unavoidable, he does Bancroft; Nissen, Lahey and tube duodenostomy he does not like.
