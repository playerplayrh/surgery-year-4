# Lymphatic Diseases 2020
Source: Surgical Board Review 2 (2563), นพ.พงษ์สันติ์ ทองเนียม · book pages 686-697 (PDF pages 690-701)

- Intro: lymphatic diseases in surgery are rarely asked; standard textbooks write little; the examiner will probably ask only cystic hygroma, lymphedema, chylothorax and chylous ascites; this chapter is short; a resident who meets such a case should decide whether to treat it themselves or refer to a senior; what is flagged "remember" must be remembered.

## Anatomy
- Skin and subcutaneous tissue:
  1. Lymphatic ducts form a network or plexus; the duct looks like a capillary but differs in that the lymphatic duct has no basement membrane.
  2. In the superficial dermis there are no valves (remember); but where it drains lymph into the subdermis there are valves, and the duct then runs long alongside the major vessels, with lymph nodes catching it at intervals, and the end of the route enters the heart.
  3. From the leg: after the groin it runs into the retroperitoneum to the cisterna chyli in the abdomen, then the thoracic duct in the chest, draining into the left subclavian vein.
  4. From the left arm and left side of the neck: collects together before entering the thoracic duct.
  5. From the right arm and right side of the neck: the lymphatic ducts collect and drain into the right subclavian vein.
- Between lymphatic ducts are lymph nodes at intervals, filtering lymphatic fluid and working in the immune system.
- Major anatomical pathway in the abdomen: 1. in the abdomen lymphatic drainage collects at the cisterna chyli at L2; 2. then forms a single duct called the thoracic duct running on the right, through the aortic hiatus into the right chest; 3. the thoracic duct lies behind, on the right, in front of the vertebral bodies, between the aorta and azygos vein behind the esophagus, at level T10-T12 (a constant anatomy of the thoracic duct: so if a surgeon ligates the thoracic duct, stitch massively at this spot, 2 stitches, and it will surely be hit); 4. the thoracic duct runs up in a single line, and at level T5 crosses from right to left, behind the aortic arch, hugging the esophagus in the posterior mediastinum; 5. then the left thoracic duct passes up to the neck behind the carotid sheath, lying behind the thyroid and vertebral body, medial to the anterior scalene muscle; 6. then turns down to drain at the junction of the left internal jugular vein with the left subclavian vein. ## Physiology
- Normal chyle flow about 1.5-2.5 liters per day, with protein about 150 gm; flow increases after eating.
- Flow depends on intrinsic contractility of the lymphatic vessel, the major role.
- Body structures with no lymphatic system: 1. epidermis of skin; 2. cornea; 3. CNS (brain); 4. cartilage; 5. tendon.
- Major function of the lymphatic system is fat transport absorbed from the digestive system: long chain fatty acid from the GI tract is converted in the enterocyte to chylomicron in lymphatic fluid.

## Imaging Studies
- Duplex US: little use; keep it for investigating a leg swollen from CVI; finding duplex in lymphedema leads one astray.
- Lymphoscintigraphy or lymph scan: inject radioactive sulfur colloid into the subdermis of the web space of the foot, then use a gamma camera to see lymphatic vessels, with the radioactivity going to collect at nodes; used little for lymphatic disease, but applied as sentinel lymph node study, e.g. in breast, works very well.
- Lymphangiogram: inject dye contrast into the web space of the hand or foot then film to look, like an IVP, seeing lymph vessels and nodes clearly; since good CT, scans have replaced the gram in investigating lymphatic disease; remaining use is for leakage, e.g. chylothorax or chylous ascites.
- CT scan: sees nodes; used to investigate suspected malignancy.
- Contrast magnetic resonance lymphangiography (MRL): a new diagnostic test to improve accuracy, using a new contrast, gadobenate dimeglumine, injected at the web space of the fingers or toes; it sees anatomy and lymph flow more clearly.
- Indocyanine green fluorescence lymphography: uses non-radioactive dye; often used at follow-up.

## Cystic Hygroma
- Some call it cystic cavernous lymphangioma; the same disease.
- The disease is a mass arising from abnormal development, so the lymphatic system does not connect to the venous system, with sequestration or obstruction of the lymphatic vessel forming a mass. Figure: neck photo (skip).
- Found 1 in 12,000 live births, about 65% found since birth; 90% present by age 2 years.
- Location: 1. most common at the posterior triangle of the neck; 2. next the axilla and mediastinum.
- Physical examination: classic: soft compressible non-tender mass with positive transillumination.
- Differential diagnosis: 1. hemangioma: has a bluish tone, location at buttock or groin [as printed]; 2. branchial cleft cyst: location along the SCM anteriorly, different from cystic hygroma, which usually lies posteriorly; 3. thyroglossal duct cyst: mass lies at the midline and moves with tongue protrusion.
- Treatment: once diagnosed as cystic hygroma, treatment is surgical excision sparing normal tissue and vital structures, because if left there will be compressive symptoms, especially airway obstruction, and cyst infection.
- Remember: observing to wait for the mass to shrink, or biopsy of the mass, is not the answer.

## Lymphedema
- A chronic condition from ineffective transport of lymph fluid, causing lymph fluid to be congested, edema and skin change.
- Divided into 2 types:
  1. Primary lymphedema: patients are more often women than men; 3 types:
     - 1.1 Congenital or Milroy's disease: found 10-15%; found since birth (present at birth), both legs swollen, X-linked, with a family history; the good part is the disease does not progress and often improves with age.
     - 1.2 Lymphedema praecox or Meige disease: found 65%; patients are usually teenage girls just starting to develop, growing puberty, age not over 35, usually unilateral edema, left leg more than right leg about 3:1; remember praecox is the most common type, found 80% (know this much).
     - 1.3 Lymphedema tarda: found 10%; remember it is found in somewhat older people, age usually over 35, or present later in life.
  2. Secondary lymphedema: causes are many, most common infection (filariasis), then LN dissection, trauma, tumor, radiation; common complication is lymphangitis and cellulitis; sarcoma is rare.
- Differential diagnosis for limb edema (know only what might be asked): 1. unilateral edema: most common from CVI; 2. bilateral edema: most common from CHF, cirrhosis, renal failure; 3. most common leg swelling of teenage girls or pretty ones standing all day working: from orthopedic edema (remember).
- How to separate the cause of limb edema between lymphedema and CVI:
  1. Lymphedema: 1.1 rare; 1.2 early stage pitting edema, but in the late stage that is severe, pressing does not dimple: non-pitting edema; raising the leg high does not reduce the swelling; the skin is rather hard, the color not dark; 1.3 the dorsum of the foot and toes swell too, with nails yellowish (remember); 1.4 confirmed with lymphoscintigraphy.
  2. CVI: 2.1 far more common than lymphedema; 2.2 pitting edema; 2.3 swelling only up to the ankle, the dorsum of the foot and toes do not swell; raising the leg reduces the swelling; 2.4 severe hyperpigmentation, found more than in lymphedema. Figure: photo of bilateral leg lymphedema (skip).

### Management of lymphedema
1. Start with conservative treatment always, the main treatment: a patient who still has only a swollen leg that still pits improves 30-45%:
   - 1.1 Leg elevation. 1.2 Elastic support and stocking.
   - 1.3 Intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) with pressure 30-60 mmHg for 4-6 hours a day: results are fairly good, but in the long run maintenance loses to elastic support and stocking.
   - 1.4 PT, massage and skin care.
   - 1.5 Emotional problem, especially in young women: if met, must treat.
   - 1.6 There is a drug called coumarin (not Coumadin), a benzopyrone group drug, believed in the past to increase proteolysis and the pumping action of lymphatic vessels, but RCTs found it not better than the group that did not receive it; so it awaits further research.
2. Surgical treatment: remember: whoever has the disease, try not to touch it, because of a high failure rate; find a way to refer; the important indicator is functional impairment.
- Surgical procedure: the chance of a lymphedema patient being operated is under 5%; those who operate many cases are few; summarized (names enough): and if any resident gets inspiration, go and read Cameron and Rutherford.
  1. Excisional or debulking procedure: suits severe cases: elephant leg, thick hard skin, non-pitting leg:
     - 1.1 Charles procedure: a radical procedure used for extreme lymphedema with severe skin change: remove skin, subcutaneous tissue and fascia, peeling the leg until only the muscle remains, then take a skin graft from the thigh to cover; patients take months to be able to be active; the result is poor cosmetically (the postoperative leg is awful), so this procedure is no longer popular. Figure: Charles procedure steps A-C and photo (skip).
     - 1.2 Kondoleon or Homan procedure: divided into 2 steps: 1st step: make an incision along the length of the leg like a fasciotomy, remove redundant skin and subcutaneous tissue, then leave the wound open (or sutured) for about 6 months to wait for the leg to shrink; 2nd step: incision on the lateral side then leave the wound for another 6 months; when 1 year is complete, close the wound, suture or graft as convenient. Figure: Kondoleon steps (skip).
  2. Reconstructive or physiologic procedure: lymphovenous and lympholymphatic anastomosis: used in secondary lymphedema with large lymphatic duct size; at present lymph surgeons operate this way.

## Chylothorax
- Means chyle in the pleural space.
- Etiology: 1. surgical procedure is the most common cause, esophagectomy being number 1, next pneumonectomy; 2. infection, e.g. TB; 3. trauma, e.g. penetrating wound into the thoracic inlet; 4. tumor; 5. congenital.
- Symptoms: cough, chest tightness, or after esophagectomy (Ivor Lewis) the ICD output turns milk-colored.
- Diagnosis: from thoracentesis.
- Gross: the fluid drawn or from the ICD is milky fluid, odorless, with fat content greater than plasma, with triglyceride (TG) level over 110 mg/ml; but if the patient is NPO, the fat level in the fluid is less, TG may be only 50 mg/ml; but when the fluid is sent to check for chylomicron, if positive it shows the fluid in the chest is chylothorax, even if the TG value is under 110.
- So the definitive diagnosis is positive chylomicron in the fluid.
- Management depends on cause, the amount of fluid that comes out, and the patient's state:
  1. Start with conservative first:
     - 1.1 ICD is sometimes life saving if the lung is expanded; sometimes tamponade until leak stops, even if difficult.
     - 1.2 NPO together with TPN; do not eat low fat [as printed: "ไม่ควรกิน low fat"].
     - 1.3 If fluid leak is over 500 cc/24 hr, wait in the past over 2 weeks (not 4 weeks), then take to operate; at present the new recommendation is over 7 days then operate straight away; or in cases with fluid out more than 1000 cc/24 hr for longer than 5 days, consider operating, because chyle loss causes loss of water, loss of protein making the patient thin, loss of lymphocytes causing easy infection, and loss of electrolytes causing severe metabolic effect.
  2. Surgical treatment: many ways; the one used often is thoracic duct ligation: the problem is we often cannot find the duct, since it is only 1-2 mm in size; the advice is to give cream, fatty food or olive oil via NG tube before going to cut; when the chest is opened we see the milky white fluid trickling out, then manage by suture ligation above the diaphragmatic aortic hiatus, via VATS or open right thoracotomy as convenient. Figures: thoracic duct ligation (esophagus, azygos vein, thoracic duct, aorta, aortic hiatus; double ligation) (skip).
- Other methods reported: use of fibrin glue, decortication, or even pneumonectomy so that fluid does not accumulate again; if chylothorax has a cause from cancer, may need to give RT after.
- Somatostatin: some mention it but the result is not certain.
- A new toy: interventional radiological treatment by having the radiologist use x-ray to help do percutaneous needle cannulation and thoracic duct occlusion, which some call thoracic duct embolization; a success report in 2013 with only 1 case, then it disappeared.
- Prognosis: in the past chylothorax had mortality up to 50%, but after surgery was done earlier, results were very good, with mortality down to only 10%.
- Figure (Schwartz edition 11 p.743 algorithm), as steps: thoracentesis -> confirm diagnosis -> chest tube -> conservative management: NPO, chest tube to suction, central hyperalimentation -> wait 2 weeks*: (non-malignant) drainage persists (over 500 mL/d): thoracotomy: duct ligation, mass ligation, decortication, pleurectomy; drainage decrease (under 250 mL/d): continue 1 week: drainage stops: remove chest tube; drainage persists: medically unstable: pleural peritoneal shunt; medically stable: thoracotomy; malignant chylothorax: radiation therapy.

## Chylous Ascites
- Etiology: 1. intraabdominal malignancy: the number 1 cause in the USA; 2. infection from TB or parasite filaria, often the cause in poor countries; 3. surgical procedure, e.g. aortobifem in AAA, retroperitoneal LND; 4. trauma; 5. cirrhosis.
- Mechanism of chylous fluid leaking into the abdomen: 1. from cancer or parasite making obstruction of lymphatic vessels until chyme leaks; 2. trauma or surgery that hits a lymphatic vessel until it tears. Figure: operative photo of milky fluid (skip).
- Diagnosis: 1. a person with abdominal bloating for weeks or months; 2. paracentesis gives milky fluid with TG level over 110; 3. CT scan to rule out intraperitoneal malignancy; 4. localization with lymphangiography and lymphoscintigraphy. Figure: lymphoscintigraphy: 99Tc sulphur colloid suspended in saline; interdigital webspaces; massaged for 2 mins; images taken over 3-4 hours tracking spread (skip).
- Management:
  1. Initial treatment is NPO to rest the bowel, TPN, octreotide, the mainstay treatment; after that, if the patient has no sepsis, is stable, bowel sounds positive, at this period we can let them eat by mouth, giving a high protein diet and restricting fatty food; as to fatty food, medium chain triglyceride can be eaten, because medium chain TG absorption goes through the mucosa of the intestine to the liver directly without passing the lymphatic vessel, so it does not add chyme volume.
  2. Give octreotide to help reduce lymph flow.
  3. Treat underlying cause.
  4. Surgical treatment: chance of being operated about 33% for chylous ascites; if not closed in 4-6 weeks one should go in to operate; before operating do lymphoscintigraphy or lymphangiography; while exploring, control leakage with ligation of cisterna chyli with non-absorbable suture; for Schwartz the writer advises to repair, or if a leak is seen from the small bowel mesentery, do small bowel resection straight away. Figure: lymphatics from liver and intestines to cisterna chyli, SMA (skip).
  5. If really a problem, peritoneovenous shunting is an option.
- For chylous ascites, the issues one may be tricked on:
  1. Paracentesis of the fluid out only and leaving it is not right, because in the early period one must do NPO, TPN, octreotide.
  2. Giving medium chain TG right away in the early period is wrong, because in the early period one must be NPO first.
  3. Doing a LeVeen shunt is wrong, because it has not been used for ascites for many years.
